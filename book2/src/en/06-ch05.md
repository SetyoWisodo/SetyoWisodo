# Water: Too Much, Too Little, and Sinking Ground

::: lead
Flood policy fails for a reason that is easy to state and hard to accept: water is not destroyed by being moved. A levee protects the land behind it by concentrating the risk on the land beside it, and the second piece of land is usually poorer.
:::

## Three floods, three defences

Chapter 3 introduced the three-way flood problem in Jakarta. It is general, and it is the reason single-solution flood policy keeps failing.

**Fluvial flooding** arrives from a river. The water comes from rain that fell days earlier, possibly in another jurisdiction, and the peak arrives after the rainfall has stopped. The defence is catchment-scale: storage upstream, floodplain that is allowed to flood, and channels that are not narrowed.

**Pluvial flooding** — from rainfall directly, in the city, on the night — overwhelms local drainage. It is the flood of short, intense storms and impermeable surfaces. The defence is at the scale of the street: fewer hard surfaces, retention, drainage designed for current intensities rather than for the decade in which it was built.

**Coastal flooding** arrives from the sea, driven by tide, surge, and rising mean level. The defence involves engineering, but the more important variable is what is built on the land behind it, and how fast the land itself is sinking.

A levee built for fluvial flooding is irrelevant to tidal flooding. A drainage system that handles pluvial flooding fails when the river is high and the outfall cannot discharge. The three interact at exactly the moment when all three occur — which is the moment that gets into the photographs.

## The levee paradox

Large flood infrastructure has a consistent political economy, and it is worth stating plainly because it explains a great deal of maladaptation.

A levee is built, and land behind it becomes "safe". Property development follows. Population and asset density behind the levee rise. The levee's residual risk — the probability that it is overtopped or fails — is unchanged, but the consequence of that residual events grows enormously, because there is now more behind it to be destroyed.

This is why flood damages in rich countries keep rising even where flood defences are improving. The engineering has been successful; the land-use response to the engineering has undone the benefit (@undrr2022).

A standard illustration is the Netherlands, which has some of the highest flood standards in the world and has shifted its policy toward giving rivers room by deliberately re-flooding polders — buying land back from development in order to lower downstream peaks. The Dutch have the fiscal capacity to do this. The lesson, for places without that capacity, is that preventing development behind a levee is much cheaper than retrofitting the levee later.

## Drought is a flood problem in slow motion

Drought is usually framed as a rainfall deficit. It is more usefully framed as a water-accounting failure, because the same rainfall can produce either a drought or a good harvest depending on what is done with it.

The mechanism is well understood. When rainfall arrives in fewer, more intense events, more of it runs off and less infiltrates. Soil compaction, deforestation, and impermeable surfaces reduce infiltration further. The rain still falls; it just leaves too fast, taking topsoil with it, and the water that should have carried the crop through the dry season has gone downstream.

This is why catchment restoration, terracing, check dams, and soil organic matter can reduce drought impact without changing rainfall at all. The intervention is not in the sky but in the ground's capacity to hold water.

The failure mode is when groundwater is treated as a reserve rather than a stock. In aquifers where extraction exceeds recharge — the North China Plain, the Indo-Gangetic plain, the High Plains of the United States, and the Jakarta basin — the water table falls, pumping costs rise, and land subsides. The drought then becomes permanent, because the reserve that buffered the dry years is gone.

## The Texas 2021 water failure, revisited

Chapter 2 used Texas 2021 as a case of infrastructural chaining. It belongs here as well, because it shows that a water failure in a wealthy country is not a shortage but a distribution problem.

During the February 2021 freeze, water systems failed because pumps lost power and pipes froze. More than 1,400 public water systems issued boil-water notices across 190 counties, affecting roughly 14.4 million people (@ferc2021). The water did not disappear. The system that moved it stopped.

The recovery was also revealing. In some districts, boil-water notices stayed in force for days after pressure was restored, because the testing and certification chain had not kept pace. The failure of the second link — verification — extended the first link's harm.

## Groundwater and subsidence: the irreversible part

Flood and drought are reversible in principle: the water returns, and the field can be replanted. Subsidence is not.

When groundwater is pumped from a confined aquifer, the clay layers compact, and that compaction is largely permanent. The land surface drops and does not rise again. In Jakarta, north of the city has fallen more than three metres in places over three decades, at rates of 1 to 15 centimetres per year and up to 28 in specific locations (@abidin2011). In Semarang, 14 to 19 centimetres per year.

The consequence is arithmetic. If the land drops 10 centimetres a year and the sea rises 0.4, the relative sea-level rise experienced by the people living there is 10.4 centimetres per year — twenty-five times the global rate. Every coastal defence is designed against a moving floor.

Subsidence is one of the few climate-adjacent hazards that can be stopped almost immediately by policy, because it is caused by pumping that can be metered and licensed. It is rarely stopped, because stopping it requires providing an alternative water supply to the people doing the pumping.

## The counter-case: the city that stored water instead of draining it

Not every city has followed the drain-and-defend model. Some have gone the other way, and the results are worth examining because they are cheap.

**Singapore** has invested heavily in both drainage and retention, and its ABC Waters programme converts canals and drains into multifunctional spaces — a waterway that is a park in dry weather and a channel in a storm. The engineering is expensive, but the land is used twice.

**Rotterdam** built water plazas: sunken squares that are playgrounds most of the year and flood basins during heavy rain. The intervention is small, distributed, and does not require a single large structure.

**Ahmedabad and Chennai** have revived traditional tank systems — temple tanks and eris — that store monsoon water and recharge aquifers. The revival is contested, because the land around the tanks has often been built on, but the principle is sound: the cheapest storage is where the water used to go.

None of these replace large defences. They reduce the peak that the large defences have to handle, which is the only thing that makes large defences affordable.

## Water as a justice problem

Every water decision allocates. A levee allocates risk to the other side of the river. A dam allocates water to downstream users and displaces upstream residents. A tariff allocates access by ability to pay. A drainage upgrade allocates protection to the neighbourhoods that get it first.

This is not a reason to avoid water infrastructure. It is a reason to make the allocation explicit, because the alternative is that it is made implicitly by whoever has the strongest claim on the budget. In most cities, that is the neighbourhood with the highest property values, which is usually the neighbourhood with the least flood exposure and the most political voice.

The pattern repeats across rich and poor countries. In Jakarta, the seawall protects the business district; the kampungs behind it flood. In New Orleans, the levee failures of 2005 were concentrated in the Lower Ninth Ward, which was poorer and lower-lying than the French Quarter.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that flood risk is set more by land use and drainage than by rainfall totals, that large defences create new risk by attracting development, and that distributed storage is systematically underfunded relative to structural defence.

It would be wrong if, across cities with similar rainfall and topography, flood damage were predicted by the size of the defence infrastructure, with land-use variables adding nothing. That is the implicit model behind most flood-management budgets.

The evidence suggests the opposite, and the clearest cases are cities that increased defence spending while allowing development behind the defences and saw damages rise anyway.
