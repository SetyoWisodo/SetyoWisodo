# Water: Too Much, Too Little, and Sinking Ground

::: lead
Flood policy fails for a reason that is easy to state and hard to accept: water is not destroyed by being moved. A levee protects the land behind it by concentrating the risk on the land beside it, and the second piece of land is usually poorer.
:::

::: {.box title="Box 5.1 — How a flood return period is calculated" kind="note"}
"A 1-in-100-year flood" is a probability statement, not a schedule. It means a flood of that magnitude has a 1 per cent chance of occurring in any given year.

Three consequences follow, and all three are routinely misunderstood.

**It can happen twice in a decade.** Independent events at 1 per cent per year produce clusters that look impossible and are not.

**The estimate depends on the record.** A return period calculated from 40 years of data has wide confidence bounds, particularly for the rarest events, which by definition have been observed few times or never.

**The record is not stationary.** Under a changing climate, the return period calculated from the historical record understates the current frequency. A structure designed to the 1960 record may be below the current 1-in-50-year level.
:::

## Three floods, three defences

Chapter 3 introduced the three-way flood problem in Jakarta. It is general, and it is the reason single-solution flood policy keeps failing.

**Fluvial flooding** arrives from a river. The water comes from rain that fell days earlier, possibly in another jurisdiction, and the peak arrives after the rainfall has stopped. The defence is catchment-scale: storage upstream, floodplain that is allowed to flood, and channels that are not narrowed.

**Pluvial flooding** — from rainfall directly, in the city, on the night — overwhelms local drainage. It is the flood of short, intense storms and impermeable surfaces. The defence is at the scale of the street: fewer hard surfaces, retention, drainage designed for current intensities rather than for the decade in which it was built.

**Coastal flooding** arrives from the sea, driven by tide, surge, and rising mean level. The defence involves engineering, but the more important variable is what is built on the land behind it, and how fast the land itself is sinking.

A levee built for fluvial flooding is irrelevant to tidal flooding. A drainage system that handles pluvial flooding fails when the river is high and the outfall cannot discharge. The three interact at exactly the moment when all three occur — which is the moment that gets into the photographs.

## New Orleans, 2005: the levee that did not fail for the reason everyone thought

The standard account of Hurricane Katrina is that the levees failed. The engineering investigation
found something more specific and more useful: most of the failures were not overtopping, but
undermining — the water did not go over the wall, it went under it [@ipet2007].

The distinction matters because the two failure modes have different remedies and different politics.
An overtopping failure is a statement about height: the storm was bigger than the design. An
undermining failure is a statement about construction, soil, and maintenance: the wall was not built to
the standard its design claimed.

The investigation found that several of the failures occurred in sections whose foundations had been
compromised by the soft deltaic soils, and that the design assumptions about soil strength were
optimistic. It also found that the maintenance of some structures had been inadequate for years.

Three lessons follow, and they generalise far beyond Louisiana.

**The design flood is a political number.** The height of a levee is set by a standard, and the
standard is set by a decision about acceptable risk, which is set by a budget. A levee that protects
against a 100-year flood is a decision that a 200-year flood is acceptable. In New Orleans, the
standards had been set decades earlier under different assumptions about subsidence and sea level.

**Subsidence and sea-level rise are the same problem.** A levee is a relative structure: its effective
height is the difference between the crest and the water, and the crest is falling while the water is
rising. The investigation documented that the delta had been subsiding, partly because of the
confined river and the extraction of oil and gas, which reduced the levee's effective height without
anyone changing the design.

**The failure was not the storm.** Katrina was a strong storm and not a record one. The disaster was
produced by the intersection of a hazard with a protection system whose performance had been
overestimated, in a city where the population least able to leave was concentrated in the lowest
ground.

The comparison with the Netherlands is instructive. The Dutch response to the 1953 flood was a
national programme with a legal standard, a dedicated institution, and a permanent funding mechanism.
The American response was a system of local levee boards with fragmented authority and no single
standard. The difference in outcomes over the following decades is not primarily a difference in
engineering skill [@ipet2007].

## The levee paradox

Large flood infrastructure has a consistent political economy, and it is worth stating plainly because it explains a great deal of maladaptation.

A levee is built, and land behind it becomes "safe". Property development follows. Population and asset density behind the levee rise. The levee's residual risk — the probability that it is overtopped or fails — is unchanged, but the consequence of that residual events grows enormously, because there is now more behind it to be destroyed.

This is why flood damages in rich countries keep rising even where flood defences are improving. The engineering has been successful; the land-use response to the engineering has undone the benefit (@undrr2022).

A standard illustration is the Netherlands, which has some of the highest flood standards in the world and has shifted its policy toward giving rivers room by deliberately re-flooding polders — buying land back from development in order to lower downstream peaks. The Dutch have the fiscal capacity to do this. The lesson, for places without that capacity, is that preventing development behind a levee is much cheaper than retrofitting the levee later.

## Drought is a flood problem in slow motion

Drought is usually framed as a rainfall deficit. It is more usefully framed as a water-accounting failure, because the same rainfall can produce either a drought or a good harvest depending on what is done with it.

The mechanism is well understood. When rainfall arrives in fewer, more intense events, more of it runs off and less infiltrates. Soil compaction, deforestation, and impermeable surfaces reduce infiltration further. The rain still falls; it just leaves too fast, taking topsoil with it, and the water that should have carried the crop through the dry season has gone downstream.

This is why catchment restoration, terracing, check dams, and soil organic matter can reduce drought impact without changing rainfall at all. The intervention is not in the sky but in the ground's capacity to hold water.

The failure mode is when groundwater is treated as a reserve rather than a stock. In aquifers where extraction exceeds recharge — the North China Plain, the Indo-Gangetic plain, the High Plains of the United States, and the Jakarta basin — the water table falls, pumping costs rise, and land subsides. The drought then becomes permanent, because the reserve that buffered the dry years is gone.

## The Texas 2021 water failure, revisited

Chapter 2 used Texas 2021 as a case of infrastructural chaining. It belongs here as well, because it shows that a water failure in a wealthy country is not a shortage but a distribution problem.

During the February 2021 freeze, water systems failed because pumps lost power and pipes froze. More than 1,400 public water systems issued boil-water notices across 190 counties, affecting roughly 14.4 million people (@ferc2021). The water did not disappear. The system that moved it stopped.

The recovery was also revealing. In some districts, boil-water notices stayed in force for days after pressure was restored, because the testing and certification chain had not kept pace. The failure of the second link — verification — extended the first link's harm.

## Groundwater and subsidence: the irreversible part

Flood and drought are reversible in principle: the water returns, and the field can be replanted. Subsidence is not.

When groundwater is pumped from a confined aquifer, the clay layers compact, and that compaction is largely permanent. The land surface drops and does not rise again. In Jakarta, north of the city has fallen more than three metres in places over three decades, at rates of 1 to 15 centimetres per year and up to 28 in specific locations (@abidin2011). In Semarang, 14 to 19 centimetres per year.

The consequence is arithmetic. If the land drops 10 centimetres a year and the sea rises 0.4, the relative sea-level rise experienced by the people living there is 10.4 centimetres per year — twenty-five times the global rate. Every coastal defence is designed against a moving floor.

Subsidence is one of the few climate-adjacent hazards that can be stopped almost immediately by policy, because it is caused by pumping that can be metered and licensed. It is rarely stopped, because stopping it requires providing an alternative water supply to the people doing the pumping.

## The anatomy of a flash flood

Pluvial flooding is the least understood of the three types, and the most amenable to local action, so
it is worth setting out how it actually happens.

A short, intense storm drops a large volume of rain in a small area. The runoff depends on how much of
that area is impermeable, how much storage exists in the drainage system, and how quickly the system
can discharge to a receiving watercourse.

Three failure modes follow.

**Capacity.** The pipe is too small for the storm. This is the classic failure, and it is a design
decision made decades earlier against a rainfall record that has since changed.

**Blockage.** The pipe is the right size but is clogged with sediment, rubbish, or roots. This is a
maintenance failure, and it is the most common cause of urban flash flooding in cities with adequate
drainage design.

**Outfall.** The pipe is adequate and clear, but the river or sea it discharges into is high, so the
water cannot leave. This is a system failure, and it is the one that turns a moderate storm into a
major flood.

The three modes have different remedies, and confusing them wastes money. A city that responds to
blockage-driven flooding by building larger pipes has spent a great deal and solved nothing. A city that
responds to outfall-driven flooding by cleaning its drains has done the same.

The diagnostic question is therefore not "how big should the drain be" but "where does the water
actually stop moving, and why". It is answerable with a survey, and it is rarely asked.

## The counter-case: the city that stored water instead of draining it

Not every city has followed the drain-and-defend model. Some have gone the other way, and the results are worth examining because they are cheap.

**Singapore** has invested heavily in both drainage and retention, and its ABC Waters programme converts canals and drains into multifunctional spaces — a waterway that is a park in dry weather and a channel in a storm. The engineering is expensive, but the land is used twice.

**Rotterdam** built water plazas: sunken squares that are playgrounds most of the year and flood basins during heavy rain. The intervention is small, distributed, and does not require a single large structure.

**Ahmedabad and Chennai** have revived traditional tank systems — temple tanks and eris — that store monsoon water and recharge aquifers. The revival is contested, because the land around the tanks has often been built on, but the principle is sound: the cheapest storage is where the water used to go.

None of these replace large defences. They reduce the peak that the large defences have to handle, which is the only thing that makes large defences affordable.

## Cape Town, 2018: the drought that was managed

The counter-case literature usually celebrates the Netherlands and Singapore, both rich and both
engineering-led. Cape Town is more instructive, because it is a middle-income city that came within
weeks of running out of water and then did not.

The sequence is well documented. Three consecutive dry winters reduced the reservoirs that supply the
city to around a fifth of capacity. The municipality published a "Day Zero" projection — the date on
which the taps would be turned off — and moved the date forward and back as consumption changed.

What followed was a large, rapid reduction in demand. Households cut consumption, the city imposed
tariffs and restrictions, and agricultural users were cut back. Per-capita demand fell by roughly half
in a little over two years, which is an extraordinary change in behaviour for a city of four million.

The interesting questions are why it worked and what it cost.

**It worked because the signal was unambiguous.** "Day Zero" is a message with no ambiguity, unlike a
percentage reduction target. The publication of a date converted an abstract constraint into a
household calendar.

**It worked because the burden was not evenly distributed.** The reductions fell hardest on poor
households, who had less discretionary use to cut, and on agriculture, which lost water and employment.
The city avoided running out of water and did not avoid a distributional conflict.

**It worked, and the infrastructure was not built.** The desalination plants that were announced were
mostly not completed, and the augmentation schemes were slower than promised. The city was saved by
demand management, which is cheap and reversible, rather than by supply, which is expensive and
permanent.

The lesson is not that demand management solves drought. It is that the demand side is often larger
than the supply side in the short run, and that it is the side that gets the least investment because
it produces no infrastructure to inaugurate.

## Water as a justice problem

Every water decision allocates. A levee allocates risk to the other side of the river. A dam allocates water to downstream users and displaces upstream residents. A tariff allocates access by ability to pay. A drainage upgrade allocates protection to the neighbourhoods that get it first.

This is not a reason to avoid water infrastructure. It is a reason to make the allocation explicit, because the alternative is that it is made implicitly by whoever has the strongest claim on the budget. In most cities, that is the neighbourhood with the highest property values, which is usually the neighbourhood with the least flood exposure and the most political voice.

The pattern repeats across rich and poor countries. In Jakarta, the seawall protects the business district; the kampungs behind it flood. In New Orleans, the levee failures of 2005 were concentrated in the Lower Ninth Ward, which was poorer and lower-lying than the French Quarter.

## The water that is not counted

One category of water loss is systematically invisible to urban water accounting, and it matters
because it is often the largest single number in the system.

**Non-revenue water** — water that is produced and not billed — is the sum of leaks, illegal
connections, metering errors, and unbilled consumption. In many cities in low- and middle-income
countries it exceeds 30 per cent; in some it approaches half. It is a supply-side loss that looks like
a drought.

The reasons it persists are institutional. A utility that is not paid for its water cannot afford to
maintain its pipes, and a utility that cannot maintain its pipes loses more water. The loop is
financial, and it is broken by tariff reform and by investment, both of which are politically costly.

The climate significance is that non-revenue water converts a rainfall deficit into a supply crisis
faster than it would otherwise. A city that loses a third of its water has an effective drought that is
50 per cent worse than the rainfall record suggests.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that flood risk is set more by land use and drainage than by rainfall totals, that large defences create new risk by attracting development, and that distributed storage is systematically underfunded relative to structural defence.

It would be wrong if, across cities with similar rainfall and topography, flood damage were predicted by the size of the defence infrastructure, with land-use variables adding nothing. That is the implicit model behind most flood-management budgets.

The evidence suggests the opposite, and the clearest cases are cities that increased defence spending while allowing development behind the defences and saw damages rise anyway.

## Flood insurance and the price signal

A chapter on water that omits insurance is incomplete, because in many countries insurance is the
mechanism through which flood risk is priced — and mispriced.

Three patterns recur.

**Premium subsidies suppress the signal.** A scheme that charges the same premium regardless of
location obscures the difference in risk between a house on a hill and a house on a floodplain. The
household is not told, through the only channel it pays attention to, that its location is dangerous.

**Withdrawal transfers the risk to the state.** When private insurers leave a market, governments
typically become the insurer of last resort. The risk does not disappear; it moves to a budget that is
not priced.

**Compensation after the event can reward the wrong decision.** A scheme that rebuilds in place after
every flood, without conditions, subsidises the decision to stay exposed. The alternative — buyouts,
relocation support, and conditions on rebuilding — is politically difficult and is the only version
that changes the long-run exposure.

The design problem is genuinely hard, because the households on the floodplain are often poor and did
not choose their location freely. The answer is not to remove support but to attach it to a change in
circumstance: support to move, not support to rebuild in the same place.

## What this chapter implies for a city budget

The practical consequence of the argument is a shift in what a water budget should buy.

Less on new storage, more on maintenance of what exists. Less on a single large defence, more on
distributed retention and permeability. Less on supply augmentation, more on reducing non-revenue
water. Less on engineering that moves water quickly, more on land that holds it.

These are not new ideas; they are the consensus of the water management literature and have been for
two decades. They remain underfunded because each of them produces no monument, and because the
benefit appears as an event that did not happen.
