# Displacement: The Choice That Always Arrives Late

::: lead
No government wants to be the one that moved a village. So the decision is deferred until the water arrives, at which point the move is no longer a decision — it is an emergency, and the people affected have lost the ability to negotiate its terms.
:::

## Falepili: a treaty written before the water

### Falepili: a treaty written before the water

In November 2023, Australia and Tuvalu signed the Falepili Union. The name comes from a Tuvaluan word meaning neighbourly care. The treaty does three things that matter for this book.

It establishes a pathway for Tuvaluans to live, study, and work in Australia — an initial allocation of 280 people per year, with the detail of eligibility to be settled (@falepili2024).

It commits Australia to assist Tuvalu in responding to a major natural disaster.

And it commits both parties to recognise Tuvalu's statehood and sovereignty, and to continue to recognise them even if Tuvalu's territory is lost to sea-level rise (@falepili2024).

That third commitment is unprecedented. It separates statehood from territory, which international law had not clearly done before. It is a legal innovation produced by a physical problem.

The treaty is not a solution to sea-level rise. It is an agreement about what happens when a solution is not available. For this chapter, its value is that it treats migration as a planned arrangement rather than a failure — and it was signed while the country was still habitable, which is precisely when the terms can be negotiated.

### The numbers, and why they are disputed

Estimates of climate displacement are unusually divergent, and the reason is definitional before it is
empirical.

The most cited figure — 216 million people internally displaced by 2050 under a high-emissions scenario
— comes from a modelling exercise [@iom2023] that projects changes in crop productivity, water availability, and
sea-level exposure, and then translates them into migration flows using historical relationships. It is
a scenario, not a forecast, and the authors say so.

The lower estimates come from a different method: observing what people actually do after an event. On
that evidence, most displacement is local, short-distance, and temporary, and most people return.

The divergence between the two is not a failure of either. It is the difference between asking "what
would happen if current trends continued" and "what has happened". Both questions matter, and neither
answers the other.

Three cautions are worth keeping.

**The projection assumes no adaptation.** If a coastal city builds defences, the number falls. If it
does not, the number rises. The projection is a counterfactual, not a prediction.

**The base is population, which is itself uncertain.** A projection of displaced people inherits every
uncertainty in the population projection.

**Displacement is not migration.** A person displaced within a district for three weeks is counted in
the same category as a permanent move across a border in some reporting, which makes the totals
incomparable.

The honest statement is that the scale of future displacement is unknown within an order of magnitude,
and that the policy-relevant question is not the total but the conditions under which people move.

## What makes displacement voluntary

### What makes displacement voluntary

The distinction between voluntary and forced migration is central to policy and is almost never clean.

A household that moves because its land is salinised is displaced. A household that moves because it
expects the land to be salinised in ten years is adapting. The two are on a continuum, and the point on
the continuum where a move becomes "forced" is a legal and political decision, not an empirical one.

Four conditions make a move closer to voluntary.

**Time.** A move made with years of notice allows the household to sell assets, arrange schooling, and
choose a destination.

**Information.** A household that knows the trajectory of the hazard can plan. A household that is
surprised cannot.

**Resources.** A move with capital is a relocation; a move without is a flight.

**A destination.** A place to go with housing, work, and a community is the difference between migration
and displacement.

Climate policy can affect all four, and usually does not. The result is that most climate displacement
is involuntary not because the move was unplanned by nature, but because it was unplanned by
institutions.

### The staged reality of displacement

Displacement is rarely a single move from home to a new country. It is a sequence, and each stage has different legal status, different vulnerabilities, and different policy levers.

**Stage 1 — displacement within the affected area** [@iom2023]. People move to a relative's house, a shelter, a mosque. This is the largest category and the least visible. It is usually temporary and frequently becomes permanent.

**Stage 2 — internal displacement.** People move to another district or city, often to informal settlements on the edge of urban areas, where they are exposed to a different set of hazards — flooding, fire, eviction.

**Stage 3 — cross-border displacement.** People move to another country, legally or irregularly. This is the smallest category and receives the most attention.

**Stage 4 — planned relocation.** A government moves a community and provides land and housing. This is the rarest and the most contested.

The policy significance of the sequence is that most displacement is internal and most of it is unplanned. International frameworks focus on the border crossing, which is the smallest part of the problem (@iom2023).

## Bangladesh, and why planned relocation fails

### Bangladesh: the country that has been doing this for decades

Bangladesh is the most important case in the displacement literature, because it has more experience of
planned relocation than any other large country.

Two programmes are relevant.

**The chars.** The riverine islands of the Brahmaputra and Meghna are formed and destroyed by the rivers,
and the households that live on them are displaced repeatedly. The government's response has been
partly to support resettlement on the mainland and partly to allow the chars to be inhabited with
services that can move. The second approach has, in practice, been more durable than the first, because
the households return to the chars regardless.

**Climate-resilient housing and cyclone shelters.** Rather than relocating, Bangladesh has invested in
raising houses, building shelters, and improving warning, which allows people to stay in place with
reduced risk. This is the "stay option" in practice.

The lesson from both is that relocation is a last resort even in a country where the hazard is extreme,
and that the interventions which work are the ones that preserve the household's existing livelihood and
network. A relocation programme that moves a family away from the river it fishes from is a programme
that has solved the flooding and created a poverty.

The Bangladeshi experience also complicates the international narrative. The country most often
described as the future of climate displacement has, so far, mostly managed to avoid mass displacement
through in-place adaptation — at a cost, and with large internal movement to cities that is driven by
economic opportunity as much as by hazard.

### Why planned relocation so often fails

Planned relocation has a poor record, and the reasons are consistent across countries.

**The new site is worse.** Relocated communities are frequently moved to land that nobody else wanted — steeper, more exposed, further from the river that provided their livelihood. The relocation reduces exposure to one hazard and increases vulnerability to another.

**Livelihoods are not relocated.** A fishing community moved away from the coast has lost its income. A farming community moved to land without water has lost its income. Compensation for the house does not compensate for the livelihood.

**Social networks are broken.** If the community is relocated as a unit, some ties survive. If it is dispersed, they do not. The evidence on this is consistent: unit relocation preserves far more social capital than dispersed relocation (@aldrich2012).

**Consent is manufactured.** Consultation processes that present a single option are not consultation. Communities that resist are often described as irrational, when they are accurately assessing that the new site is worse.

**The process takes years.** A relocation planned in 2020 may be completed in 2030, by which time the original site may be uninhabitable and the community's bargaining position has collapsed.

The cases that work share a pattern: the community was involved in site selection, livelihoods were planned alongside housing, and the relocation happened before the site became uninhabitable, not after.

## Staying is a legitimate choice

### Staying is a legitimate choice

There is a tendency in climate writing to treat staying in a high-risk place as a failure of adaptation — a refusal to accept reality. This is both condescending and analytically wrong.

People stay for reasons that are rational: land, family, culture, the cost of moving, the absence of a destination, and the belief — often correct — that the risk is manageable with the right support.

The policy question is not how to persuade people to leave. It is how to make staying as safe as possible, and how to ensure that the choice to leave is available and dignified when it is made.

This is the argument for **staged retreat**: not abandonment, but a planned withdrawal from the most exposed areas over time, with services maintained in the interim. It is expensive, politically difficult, and cheaper than an emergency evacuation.

### The household that moves first

Displacement is usually analysed at the scale of a population, and the decision is made by a household.

The household that moves first is, in most cases, the one with the most resources: a member already
working elsewhere, savings, a contact in the destination. The movement is therefore selective, and the
selection removes exactly the people who could have organised the community through the later, more
difficult years.

The households that remain are older, poorer, and more dependent on the place. They are also the ones
least able to move when the situation becomes intolerable.

This is the mechanism behind the "trapped population" finding in the displacement literature: the people
who would prefer to leave are not the people who can. The policy implication is that support to move
should be targeted at the households that cannot afford it, because the market will move the rest.

## The destination problem

### The destination problem

Every discussion of displacement eventually reaches the question that wealthy countries prefer to avoid: where do people go?

The Falepili Union answers it for 280 Tuvaluans per year. That is a rounding error against the population of the Pacific, and it is more than most countries have offered.

The legal architecture is fragmented. The 1951 Refugee Convention does not cover climate displacement, because it requires persecution. The UN's Global Compact on Migration is non-binding. The Nansen Initiative produced a protection agenda but no obligation, and the Global Compact is a framework without a court [@iom2023; @undrr2022].

The result is that most cross-border climate displacement happens through irregular channels, which means it happens without rights, without documentation, and without the possibility of return.

This is not a problem that will be solved by any single agreement. It is a problem that will be managed, badly or well, and the difference is measured in lives.

Derived from the cases that have worked.

1. **Site selection with the community**, including a livelihood assessment, not just a hazard assessment.
2. **Legal title at the destination**, in the name of both spouses where applicable. A house without title is a house that can be lost.
3. **Phased movement**, so that the community can maintain its networks and return for harvests during the transition.
4. **A funded timeline**, because relocation that stalls leaves people in limbo for years.
5. **A stay option**, with services maintained for those who choose not to move.

### The receiving city

Displacement policy focuses on the origin and ignores the destination, which is where most of the harm
happens.

A household that moves to a city arrives into a housing market it cannot afford, a labour market that
may not recognise its skills, and a service system that was not planned for it. The result is often a
move from a rural hazard to an urban one: from a floodplain to a hillside settlement, from a coastal
village to a room above a workshop.

Three features of a receiving city determine whether the move is a recovery or a second displacement.

**Affordable housing.** Without it, the arrival ends up in an informal settlement, which is usually more
exposed than the place left behind.

**Registration and rights.** A household that cannot register cannot access services, school, or
healthcare, and is therefore permanently in a legal limbo.

**A community.** The presence of people from the same place, speaking the same language, is the single
strongest predictor of successful integration.

The policy implication is that displacement planning has to be urban policy, and that the cities that
receive displaced people need support that is rarely budgeted.

## What a receiving city does

### What the receiving city does with a hundred thousand people

Most displacement in the climate record is rural-to-urban, and the urban end of the journey is where the
policy is thinnest.

Three phases determine the outcome.

**Arrival (weeks).** The household needs shelter, registration, and cash. The shelters are usually
informal — a relative's room, a rented shack, a construction site — and the registration is the critical
bottleneck. A household without a document cannot enrol a child in school, access a clinic, or open an
account.

**Consolidation (months).** The household finds work, usually in the informal sector, at wages below the
urban average. The housing becomes a rental in an informal settlement, which is often on a slope or a
floodplain — the household has exchanged one hazard for another.

**Settlement (years).** The household either moves up — a formal rental, a school for the children, a
regular income — or becomes stuck. The determinant is rarely the household's effort and usually the
availability of formal work and affordable housing.

Three policy instruments change the trajectory.

**Registration on arrival.** A simple, fast process that does not require documents the household lost.

**Rental support.** A cash transfer sufficient for a formal rental in the first year, which prevents the
move into the informal settlement.

**School enrolment without documentation.** The single most predictive intervention for the children's
long-run outcome.

None of these is expensive. All of them require the receiving city to plan for a population it did not
expect, which is the political difficulty.

### The place that receives nobody

There is a category of displacement that is rarely discussed: the place that is emptied and does not
receive anyone in return.

A village that loses its young people to the city and its remaining households to a flood becomes a place
with no one to maintain the school, the clinic, the mosque, or the drainage. The infrastructure decays, the
services withdraw, and the remaining households become more vulnerable — not because of the hazard, but
because of the loss of the people who maintained the ordinary systems.

This is a slow displacement that no count captures. It is also, in many regions, the dominant form: not a
sudden evacuation but a gradual emptying that leaves the most vulnerable behind.

The policy implication is uncomfortable. Supporting a place that is emptying can be a way of supporting
the people who cannot leave, which is a legitimate goal, and it can also be a way of postponing a decision
that will have to be made. The honest approach is to name which of the two is being done.

### The counter-case: a relocation that worked

Not all relocations fail. The best-documented successes share the features listed above.

In Fiji, the village of Vunidogoloa relocated inland in 2014 after repeated tidal flooding. The process took eight years, involved the community in site selection, and included land for food gardens. It is not a perfect case — the new site has its own problems, and the community's relationship to the sea has changed — but the relocation was completed before the old site became uninhabitable, and the community remained together (@mcleman2019).

In Vietnam, a planned resettlement programme for communities in the Mekong Delta moved households to higher ground with land and credit. The outcomes varied, and the households that received land as well as a house fared much better than those that received only a house.

The pattern is that relocation works when it is treated as a development project with livelihood components, and fails when it is treated as a construction project.

## The legal vacuum, and the return that is not a success

### The legal vacuum, and the two ways out

International law has no category for a person who crosses a border because their country is becoming
uninhabitable. The Refugee Convention requires persecution, which climate does not constitute, and no
binding instrument has replaced it.

Two routes are being negotiated.

**A new protocol.** A dedicated instrument under the climate convention, creating a protected status and
an obligation on receiving states. The advantage is clarity; the disadvantage is that it requires
states to accept an obligation they have consistently refused.

**Bilateral and regional agreements.** The Falepili Union is the model: a specific arrangement between a
specific pair of states, with defined numbers and defined rights [@falepili2024]. The advantage is that
it can be negotiated now; the disadvantage is that it covers a tiny share of the people affected.

The second route is producing real agreements and the first is not, which suggests that the near-term
architecture will be a patchwork of bilateral deals. A patchwork is better than nothing for the people
inside it, and it is systematically unequal for those outside.

The practical recommendation that follows is unglamorous: countries with exposed populations should
negotiate bilateral arrangements now, while they still have something to offer and while their
populations are still resident. The terms available to a state negotiating before displacement are
better than those available after.

### The return, and why it is not a success

A large share of people displaced by a disaster return home within a year, and the return is often presented as recovery [@iom2023].

The evidence is more complicated. A household that returns to a damaged house in an area that will flood
again has recovered its location, not its situation. The return may reflect the absence of an
alternative rather than the restoration of safety.

Three questions distinguish a successful return from a return of necessity.

**Was the house repaired to a standard?** A return to an unrepaired house is a return to the same risk.

**Was the hazard reduced?** If the drainage, the embankment, or the warning system is unchanged, the
return is a bet on luck.

**Was the household's livelihood restored?** A return without work is a return without recovery.

The policy lesson is that the return rate is a poor indicator of success, and that the indicators that
matter — repair quality, hazard reduction, livelihood — are rarely measured.

## What a receiving city should have ready

### What a receiving city should have ready

A short operational list, derived from the cases in this chapter.

**A registration desk** at the point of arrival, open in the first week, that does not require documents
the household does not have.

**Emergency rental assistance** for six to twelve months, paid directly, sufficient for a formal unit.

**School enrolment** without documentation, with a named official responsible for exceptions.

**A health screening** at registration, because displaced populations arrive with untreated conditions and
with incomplete vaccination.

**A community liaison** from the displaced population, paid, whose job is to explain the city to the
newcomers and the newcomers to the city.

None of these is expensive relative to the cost of an unplanned arrival, and all of them require a city
to have planned for a population it did not expect.

### The paperwork of displacement

A practical observation that determines outcomes more than any policy: the documents.

A displaced household typically loses its identity documents, title deeds, school records, and medical
records, because they were in the house. The loss is not only material; it determines access to every
service and every compensation scheme.

Four documents matter, in order.

**Identity.** Without it, nothing else is possible.

**Proof of residence.** Required for services in the destination, and difficult to establish for a household
that has just arrived.

**Proof of ownership or tenancy** at the origin, required for compensation.

**Medical and school records**, required for continuity of care and education.

The policy response that works is pre-event registration: a household record held by the municipality, in a
form that survives, with a copy given to the household. Several cities have done this for other reasons —
social protection, taxation — and the record becomes the basis for post-disaster assistance.

The cost is small. The benefit is that a household does not have to prove its existence after losing
everything.

### The land that nobody owns

A legal problem that blocks relocation in many countries and is rarely discussed.

A household that has occupied land without title for decades may have rights under customary or statutory
law, and those rights may not be recognised by the compensation process. A relocation programme that
requires title will exclude the people it is meant to help, and the exclusion will be invisible in the
programme's reporting.

Three approaches have worked.

**Community-based verification.** A process that accepts testimony from neighbours and local officials rather
than requiring documents.

**Presumptive registration.** A register compiled before the event, from whatever records exist.

**Collective titling.** A title held by a community rather than by households, which recognises the
customary arrangement.

Each is a legal innovation, and each is cheaper than the litigation that follows an exclusion.

## Five implications for policy

### Five implications for policy

A chapter on displacement that ends without policy implications is incomplete, so here are five, stated
plainly.

**Plan for internal movement first.** Most displacement is within a country and much of it is within a
district. The instruments are housing, registration, and services, not international agreements.

**Negotiate the terms before the crisis.** A bilateral agreement signed while a population is still resident
produces better terms than one negotiated under pressure.

**Move communities, not households.** The evidence on network preservation is unambiguous. Dispersed
relocation destroys the resource that makes recovery possible.

**Fund the receiving city.** The destination is where the harm concentrates, and it is where no one budgets.

**Name the choice.** A government that supports people to stay is making a choice, and so is one that
supports them to leave. The choice should be stated and defended, not left to the market.

### The school that is a relocation decision

A detail that determines whether a relocated community survives: the school.

A community that moves with its school remains a community, because the school is where the children meet,
where the parents meet, and where the identity is transmitted. A community that moves without its school
disperses within a generation.

The evidence from resettlement programmes is consistent: the retention of the school is the strongest
predictor of whether the second generation remains in the new site.

The practical consequence is that a relocation plan should be organised around the school, not only around
the houses. The school's catchment, its building, and its staff are the institutional core of the community,
and moving them is the difference between a relocation and a dispersal.

### The number that is not a forecast

A caution that applies to this whole chapter.

The projections of future displacement are scenarios, not forecasts, and they should be read as such. A
scenario tells you what would happen under a set of assumptions; it does not tell you what will happen.

The assumptions that matter most are adaptation and development. A world that invests in coastal protection
and in rural livelihoods will see less displacement than one that does not, at the same level of warming.

The policy implication is that the projection is a choice, not a fate. The number that is published is the
number that results from current policy, and current policy is changeable.

### The choice that is made under duress

An observation about consent.

A relocation decision is often presented as a choice: the household may accept the new site or remain. When
the alternative to acceptance is remaining in a place that floods twice a year, the choice is not free.

The evidence from resettlement programmes is that the consent obtained under these conditions is often
nominal, and the outcomes are worse than in programmes where the community had genuine alternatives.

The practical implication is that a relocation programme should be evaluated on whether the household had a
viable alternative to accepting. If it did not, the consent is not consent, and the programme is a
displacement.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that displacement is inevitable in some places, that planned relocation fails when it ignores livelihoods and networks, and that the terms of migration should be negotiated before the site becomes uninhabitable.

The falsifier is a follow-up comparison. Take relocation schemes with documented livelihood support and schemes without, matched on hazard exposure and distance moved, and compare household income, employment, and return rates three to five years after the move. The chapter is wrong if the schemes with support performed no better than those without, once pre-move income is controlled for.

The evidence on Fiji and Vietnam points the chapter's way, and it is thin: the number of well-documented relocations with follow-up data is small enough that a single well-run study could move the conclusion [@iom2023]. That is the honest state of the claim.

The timing claim is testable against process records: schemes where terms were agreed before the site became uninhabitable should show lower rates of secondary displacement — households moving again within five years — than schemes negotiated under emergency conditions. If the rates were the same, the chapter's policy conclusion would be wrong.
