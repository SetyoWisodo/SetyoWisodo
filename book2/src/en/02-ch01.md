# Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability

::: lead
Three terms are used interchangeably in public argument and separately in the literature. The most common error in climate discussion is not a wrong number. It is a right number attached to the wrong term.
:::

## Why the distinction is not pedantic

A climate disaster begins with a hazard. That part is described by physics, and it is the part most easily blamed. The middle position often taken — "this is not a natural disaster, it is a social one" — is correct as a correction, but it stops too early. It merges three things that have to be separated if we want to know what can be repaired.

Hazard is the physical event: extreme heat, extreme rainfall, cyclone wind, drought. It is measured in physical units — degrees, millimetres per hour, metres per second. Exposure is the presence of people, buildings, and assets where the hazard occurs; it is measured in people or in value. Vulnerability is how easily those things break when the hazard arrives [@undrr2022; @ipcc2022a].

The working relationship in the disaster literature is usually written as **Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability**. This is not a physical equation. It is a frame, and its value is that it shows risk can fall without the hazard falling. If hazard doubles while exposure and vulnerability each fall by a third, net risk still rises — but far less than it would have.

The distinction also decides what counts as an answer. If damage is treated as an act of nature, the only coherent policy is to wait and rebuild. If damage is treated as the product of social arrangement, there is a list of decisions that can be changed now.

## Hazard: what can and cannot be changed

Hazard has a property that matters: it does not respond to policy on a short timescale.

Global mean surface temperature in 2024 was about 1.55 °C above the 1850–1900 baseline; in 2025 it was about 1.43 °C (@wmo2025). The 2025 decline is not recovery. It is the dissipation of El Niño, not the effect of policy. Atmospheric CO₂ reached about 422.8 ppm in 2024 (@noaa2025), and annual emissions remain near 42 billion tonnes of CO₂ (@forster2023).

Because carbon dioxide persists, the hazard of this decade was set by emissions of previous decades. No policy can cancel this year's heatwave. What policy can do is slow the rise of hazard for the next decade while exposure and vulnerability are reduced for the present one.

The practical conclusion is uncomfortable and is often avoided: over a 10–20 year horizon, most of the life-saving work is on the exposure and vulnerability side, not the emissions side. Not because mitigation does not matter, but because mitigation operates with a longer lag than the lifespan of a household on a riverbank.

::: {.box title="Box 1.1 — Three terms, one event" kind="note"}
The Pakistan floods of 2022, read through the framework.

**Hazard** — monsoon rainfall amplified by a warmer atmosphere, lasting eight weeks. Attribution analysis found that climate change made rainfall of this severity more likely (@otto2022).

**Exposure** — 33 million people affected, living on the Indus plain, in the provinces of Sindh and Balochistan (@pakistan2022). This part is explained by settlement history.

**Vulnerability** — 1,739 deaths recorded by the NDMA, one-third of them children, a toll lower than it might have been because warnings existed. But 2.2 million houses were damaged or destroyed and eight million people were displaced (@pakistan2022).

One disaster, three explanations, three different kinds of repair.
:::

## Exposure: a decision already made

Exposure is the residue of accumulated decisions. When a city expands onto a floodplain, exposure rises — not because the climate changed, but because the city chose its footprint. When agriculture moves into land that was previously too dry, exposure to drought rises.

The global trend is consistent. The number of people living in flood-exposed areas keeps rising, not because floods are spreading, but because construction moves toward water (@undrr2022). The same assessment records that while deaths per event have fallen in many places, economic losses keep rising — the signature of exposure growing faster than protection.

What makes exposure hard to reduce is that it is often a rational economic decision at the moment it is taken. Land on the floodplain is cheap precisely because it floods. For a household with no alternative, that is not a mistake; it is the only decision available. Reducing exposure is therefore almost always a question of providing alternatives rather than prohibiting choices.

## Vulnerability: the part that can be repaired

Vulnerability is where this book spends most of its time, because it explains most of the variation in death tolls between places.

Consider two heat events. In Europe, the 2003 summer killed an estimated 70,000 people (@robine2008). The 2022 summer killed 61,672 despite being hotter (@ballester2023). Most of that difference is not a change in hazard. It is a change in vulnerability: heat warning systems, municipal response plans, public awareness, and wider access to cooling. Not enough — but enough to move the number.

Compare a place without such plans. In Ahmedabad, India, a May 2010 heatwave killed more than a thousand people within days. After the city adopted a heat action plan in 2013 — a register of vulnerable residents, shifted working hours, free drinking water at bus stops, and SMS alerts — a pilot evaluation found lower mortality in a subsequent heatwave even though temperatures were not lower (@hess2018).

This is not a story about a rich city buying technology. It is a story about a city that decided to write down who was most likely to die, and then called them. The cost was small. The expensive part was deciding to do it.

## Vulnerability is a property of situations, not of people

A misunderstanding worth dismantling early: vulnerability is not an attribute of a group. It is the product of a configuration — physical, social, economic, institutional — and configurations can change (@adger2006).

An older person living alone in an apartment without cooling is highly vulnerable to heat. But the vulnerability is not caused by age. It comes from the combination of age, isolation, and building. In British Columbia, 619 people died during the June 2021 heat dome. About two-thirds were 70 or older, 56 per cent lived alone, and 98 per cent died indoors (@bccoroners2022). If a neighbour checks twice a day, vulnerability falls. If a cooling centre is within walking distance, it falls further. If there is transport to that centre, further still.

Effective intervention is therefore rarely one large act. It is several small acts that close on each other. This is also why large post-disaster donations often accomplish less than small pre-disaster investment: what is needed is not one field hospital but a hundred home visits that never get photographed.

## The number that changes how you read

Deaths from weather-related disasters have fallen sharply over the past century, not risen. Global mortality from such events declined by more than 90 per cent since the 1920s, while population roughly quadrupled (@cred2023). The decline is real, and it is routinely abused by two sides: those who conclude there is no problem, and those who conclude the statistics cannot be trusted.

The truth is more specific. The fall is driven mainly by the disappearance of famine deaths from drought — the dominant killer of the 1920s — and by better warnings, shelters, and response (@cred2023). Meanwhile economic losses rise, because exposed assets multiply and because extreme events themselves become more frequent (@undrr2022). A falling death toll is therefore not evidence that risk is falling. It is evidence that one kind of vulnerability was reduced while another kind grew.

Bangladesh is the clearest case. Cyclone Bhola in 1970 killed 300,000 people, with estimates reaching 500,000. Cyclone Amphan in 2020, of comparable strength, killed 26 people in Bangladesh after about 2.5 million were evacuated (@sammonds2021). What changed was not the storm. It was the system: 76,000 volunteers across coastal villages, raised shelters, warnings in local languages, and drills that made evacuation routine (@wmo2022).

The pair is often cited as a success story, and it is — but a success with a price. Adaptation costs in Bangladesh rise every year, and economic damage rises even as deaths fall. Success in reducing mortality is not the same as success in reducing risk.

## The framework this book uses

The three terms produce a working frame applied consistently throughout:

**Hazard** is set by physics and past emissions. It cannot be negotiated in the short run, and every chapter that discusses it names its data source.

**Exposure** is set by the history of development. It can be changed, but slowly, expensively, and usually against entrenched economic interest.

**Vulnerability** is set by social arrangement: who has information, money, transport, adequate housing, and someone who will check on them. It is the most changeable in the short run, and the most often ignored because its improvements are not photogenic.

The frame also explains why this book does not open with a temperature graph. The graph matters, but if a reader wants to know why two places with the same weather suffer different death tolls, the answer lies in the last two terms, not the first.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that risk is the product of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, and that in the short run vulnerability is the most productive place to work.

The argument fails if, once data are assembled across many countries, deaths per event turn out to be determined almost entirely by the magnitude of the physical hazard, with social variables contributing a remainder of no consequence. If that were true, the reason to write Chapters 8 to 11 — on money, infrastructure, social capital, and knowledge — would disappear.

So far the evidence runs the other way. Storms of comparable strength produce death tolls that differ by two orders of magnitude in different places. Wind speed does not account for the difference.

## Further reading

- **IPCC (2022a)**, *AR6 Working Group II*, especially Chapter 1 on the risk framework. The canonical statement of the hazard–exposure–vulnerability triad.
- **UNDRR (2022)**, *Global Assessment Report 2022*. The clearest recent account of why losses rise while deaths fall.
- **Adger (2006)**, "Vulnerability." Short, and still the best conceptual map of the term.
- **Sammonds et al. (2021)** on Bangladesh. The Bhola-to-Amphan comparison with the underlying data.
- **CRED (2023)**, *EM-DAT*. Read the definitions before using any disaster death figure; they differ from what most readers assume.
