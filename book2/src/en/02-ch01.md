# Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability

::: lead
Three terms are used interchangeably in public argument and separately in the literature. The most common error in climate discussion is not a wrong number. It is a right number attached to the wrong term.
:::


## Why the distinction is not pedantic

A climate disaster begins with a hazard. That part is described by physics, and it is the part most easily blamed. The middle position often taken — "this is not a natural disaster, it is a social one" — is correct as a correction, but it stops too early. It merges three things that have to be separated if we want to know what can be repaired.

Hazard is the physical event: extreme heat, extreme rainfall, cyclone wind, drought. It is measured in physical units — degrees, millimetres per hour, metres per second. Exposure is the presence of people, buildings, and assets where the hazard occurs; it is measured in people or in value. Vulnerability is how easily those things break when the hazard arrives [@undrr2022; @ipcc2022a].

The working relationship in the disaster literature is usually written as **Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability**. This is not a physical equation. It is a frame, and its value is that it shows risk can fall without the hazard falling [@ipcc2022a].

Using it as arithmetic requires care, and the arithmetic is worth doing once because it disciplines the intuition. If hazard doubles while exposure and vulnerability are each reduced by a third, the product is 2 × 0.67 × 0.67 ≈ 0.89: net risk falls by about a tenth. Halving exposure and vulnerability instead gives 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.5, and risk falls by half. Two conclusions follow, and both are load-bearing for the rest of this book. A hazard that doubles can be absorbed by a modest reduction in the social terms — but only if the reduction is real, and only if it is applied to both terms rather than one. And the formula rewards precision it does not deserve: exposure and vulnerability are not measured on a common scale, so the numbers above illustrate a direction, not a magnitude [@kelman2012; @birkmann2013].

The distinction also decides what counts as an answer. If damage is treated as an act of nature, the only coherent policy is to wait and rebuild. If damage is treated as the product of social arrangement, there is a list of decisions that can be changed now.

## Where the vocabulary came from

The three-term frame is not a recent invention, and its history explains why it is still contested.

Before the 1970s, disaster research was dominated by a physicalist account: disasters were acts of
nature, and the social response was relief. The corrective came from a group of geographers and
sociologists who noticed that the same hazard produced wildly different outcomes in comparable
societies, and that the difference tracked patterns of land tenure, employment, and housing rather
than the weather [@okeefe1976].

Their argument, published in *Nature* in 1976 under the title "Taking the naturalness out of natural
disasters," was deliberately blunt: there is no such thing as a natural disaster, because the
conditions that convert a hazard into a catastrophe are produced by human decisions. The paper was
contested at the time and is now close to orthodoxy in the field.

The framework that followed, and that still organises most vulnerability research, is the Pressure and
Release model [@wisner2004]. It describes a chain running from **root causes** — economic systems,
governance structures, ideology — through **dynamic pressures** — rapid urbanisation, deforestation,
declining public services — to **unsafe conditions** — fragile houses on marginal land, livelihoods
exposed to a single hazard, populations without warning. The hazard applies pressure at the end of
that chain; the chain determines how much damage the pressure produces.

The model's contribution is that it makes vulnerability traceable. A death on a floodplain is not
explained by rainfall; it is explained by why the household was on the floodplain, which is explained
by land markets, which are explained by policy. The chain can be followed, and each link can be
intervened in.

The IPCC adopted a version of this framing in its risk assessments, formalising risk as a function of
hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, with adaptation and mitigation acting on the components rather
than on the risk directly [@ipcc2022a]. That formalisation is what makes the framework usable in policy:
if risk is a product of three terms, an intervention that reduces any one of them reduces risk, and
the terms can be compared for cost.

::: {.box title="Box 1.1 — How the risk framework is assembled" kind="note"}
The three-term frame is not a measurement; it is a decomposition. Its value depends on how each term is operationalised.

**Hazard** is measured in physical units [@ipcc2022a] and is the best-observed term. Global temperature, rainfall intensity, and cyclone wind are instrumentally recorded, with defined baselines.

**Exposure** is measured by people or asset value in a defined area, and the area is the contested part. A flood hazard map drawn at a 1-in-100-year return period will show a smaller exposed population than one drawn at 1-in-30.

**Vulnerability** has no single metric. Studies use income, housing quality, age structure, access to services, or composite indices, and the choice changes the ranking of places.

This is why the same event can produce different "exposure" estimates from different agencies: the frame is shared, the operationalisation is not.
:::
::: {.box title="Box 1.2 — Three terms, one event" kind="note"}
The Pakistan floods of 2022, read through the framework.

**Hazard** — monsoon rainfall amplified by a warmer atmosphere, lasting eight weeks. Attribution analysis found that climate change made rainfall of this severity more likely [@otto2022].

**Exposure** — 33 million people affected, living on the Indus plain, in the provinces of Sindh and Balochistan [@pakistan2022]. This part is explained by settlement history.

**Vulnerability** — 1,739 deaths recorded by the NDMA, one-third of them children, a toll lower than it might have been because warnings existed. But 2.2 million houses were damaged or destroyed and eight million people were displaced [@pakistan2022].

One disaster, three explanations, three different kinds of repair.
:::

## Two neighbourhoods, one afternoon

The clearest demonstration that vulnerability is socially produced comes not from a poor country but
from a rich one, and not from a flood but from heat.

In July 1995, a heat wave settled over Chicago. Over five days, the city recorded 739 excess deaths.
The mortality was not distributed evenly. It was concentrated in a small number of neighbourhoods on
the South and West Sides, and within those neighbourhoods among older people living alone
[@klinenberg2002].

The sociologist Eric Klinenberg, who reconstructed the event, found that the difference between the
neighbourhoods that died and the neighbourhoods that did not was not temperature. It was the
structure of everyday life: whether people knew their neighbours, whether the streets were busy or
empty, whether there were shops and churches and parks where someone would notice an absence.

Two adjacent neighbourhoods with similar housing stock and similar income levels had very different
death tolls, because one had a functioning public life and the other had lost it. The mechanism was
not physiological but social: in the neighbourhoods with dense networks, someone knocked on the door.
In the neighbourhoods without, nobody did [@klinenberg2002].

The finding has held up across subsequent heat events, including the British Columbia heat dome in
2021, where 56 per cent of the dead lived alone [@bccoroners2022]. It is the strongest single argument
in this book: a variable that appears in no engineering model — whether someone will check on you —
predicts survival better than the physical exposure does.

And it has an uncomfortable corollary. If social isolation is a cause of death in a heat wave, then
the policies that produce social isolation — housing that disperses communities, public space that is
allowed to decay, services that are withdrawn — are health policies, whether or not anyone calls them
that.

## Hazard: what can and cannot be changed


Hazard has a property that matters: it does not respond to policy on a short timescale.

Global mean surface temperature in 2024 was about 1.55 °C above the 1850–1900 baseline; in 2025 it was about 1.43 °C (@wmo2025). The 2025 decline is not recovery. It is the dissipation of El Niño, not the effect of policy. Atmospheric CO₂ reached about 422.8 ppm in 2024 (@noaa2025), and annual emissions remain near 42 billion tonnes of CO₂ (@forster2023).

Because carbon dioxide persists, the hazard of this decade was set by emissions of previous decades. No policy can cancel this year's heatwave. What policy can do is slow the rise of hazard for the next decade while exposure and vulnerability are reduced for the present one.

The practical conclusion is uncomfortable and is often avoided: over a 10–20 year horizon, most of the life-saving work is on the exposure and vulnerability side, not the emissions side. Not because mitigation does not matter, but because mitigation operates with a longer lag than the lifespan of a household on a riverbank.


## Exposure: a decision already made

Exposure is the residue of accumulated decisions. When a city expands onto a floodplain, exposure rises — not because the climate changed, but because the city chose its footprint. When agriculture moves into land that was previously too dry, exposure to drought rises.

The global trend is consistent. The number of people living in flood-exposed areas keeps rising, not because floods are spreading, but because construction moves toward water (@undrr2022). The same assessment records that while deaths per event have fallen in many places, economic losses keep rising — the signature of exposure growing faster than protection.

What makes exposure hard to reduce is that it is often a rational economic decision at the moment it is taken. Land on the floodplain is cheap precisely because it floods. For a household with no alternative, that is not a mistake; it is the only decision available. Reducing exposure is therefore almost always a question of providing alternatives rather than prohibiting choices.

## Vulnerability: the part that can be repaired

Vulnerability is where this book spends most of its time, because it explains most of the variation in death tolls between places.

Consider two heat events. In Europe, the 2003 summer killed an estimated 70,000 people (@robine2008). The 2022 summer killed 61,672 despite being hotter (@ballester2023). Most of that difference is not a change in hazard. It is a change in vulnerability: heat warning systems, municipal response plans, public awareness, and wider access to cooling. Not enough — but enough to move the number.

Compare a place without such plans. In Ahmedabad, India, a May 2010 heatwave killed more than a thousand people within days. After the city adopted a heat action plan in 2013 — a register of vulnerable residents, shifted working hours, free drinking water at bus stops, and SMS alerts — a pilot evaluation found lower mortality in a subsequent heatwave even though temperatures were not lower (@hess2018).

This is not a story about a rich city buying technology. It is a story about a city that decided to write down who was most likely to die, and then called them. The cost was small. The expensive part was deciding to do it.

## Vulnerability is a property of situations, not of people

A misunderstanding worth dismantling early: vulnerability is not an attribute of a group. It is the product of a configuration — physical, social, economic, institutional — and configurations can change (@adger2006).

An older person living alone in an apartment without cooling is highly vulnerable to heat. But the vulnerability is not caused by age. It comes from the combination of age, isolation, and building. In British Columbia, 619 people died during the June 2021 heat dome. About two-thirds were 70 or older, 56 per cent lived alone, and 98 per cent died indoors (@bccoroners2022). If a neighbour checks twice a day, vulnerability falls. If a cooling centre is within walking distance, it falls further. If there is transport to that centre, further still.

Effective intervention is therefore rarely one large act. It is several small acts that close on each other. This is also why large post-disaster donations often accomplish less than small pre-disaster investment: what is needed is not one field hospital but a hundred home visits that never get photographed.

## The number that changes how you read

Deaths from weather-related disasters have fallen sharply over the past century, not risen. Global mortality from such events declined by more than 90 per cent since the 1920s, while population roughly quadrupled (@cred2023). The decline is real, and it is routinely abused by two sides: those who conclude there is no problem, and those who conclude the statistics cannot be trusted.

The truth is more specific. The fall is driven mainly by the disappearance of famine deaths from drought — the dominant killer of the 1920s — and by better warnings, shelters, and response (@cred2023). Meanwhile economic losses rise, because exposed assets multiply and because extreme events themselves become more frequent (@undrr2022). A falling death toll is therefore not evidence that risk is falling. It is evidence that one kind of vulnerability was reduced while another kind grew.

Bangladesh is the clearest case. Cyclone Bhola in 1970 killed 300,000 people, with estimates reaching 500,000. Cyclone Amphan in 2020, of comparable strength, killed 26 people in Bangladesh after about 2.5 million were evacuated (@sammonds2021). What changed was not the storm. It was the system: 76,000 volunteers across coastal villages, raised shelters, warnings in local languages, and drills that made evacuation routine (@wmo2022).

The pair is often cited as a success story, and it is — but a success with a price. Adaptation costs in Bangladesh rise every year, and economic damage rises even as deaths fall. Success in reducing mortality is not the same as success in reducing risk.

## Exposure is a political variable

If exposure is the residue of past decisions, it follows that exposure is a political variable, and
the politics is usually invisible because the decisions were made incrementally and by different
people.

Three mechanisms recur across countries.

**Land markets push the poor toward hazard.** Floodplains, steep slopes, and the edges of industrial
sites are cheap for the reason that they are dangerous. A household with no choice is not making an
error when it builds there; it is choosing the only option the market offered.

**Infrastructure attracts development, and development raises exposure.** A levee, a road, a
drainage scheme — each makes a place more attractive, and each is followed by construction behind it.
The engineering reduces the probability of damage per building while increasing the number of
buildings that can be damaged.

**Subsidies and insurance can hold exposure in place.** A compensation scheme that rebuilds in the
same place after every flood, at public expense, is a scheme that subsidises the decision to remain
exposed. The household is not irrational; the incentive is.

None of these is a market failure in the textbook sense. Each is a rational response to the
incentives that policy created. Which is why reducing exposure is rarely a matter of telling people
to move, and almost always a matter of changing what the alternatives cost.

## Three cases, one framework

The framework earns its place if it explains cases that look unrelated. Three, from different
continents and different hazards.

**Pakistan, 2022.** A monsoon amplified by a warmer atmosphere fell on a floodplain settled by
smallholders with insecure tenure, in a country whose drainage and embankment system had been
under-maintained for years. The hazard was exceptional; the exposure was historical; the
vulnerability was institutional. About 33 million people were affected and 1,739 died
[@pakistan2022].

**Texas, 2021.** A polar vortex — rare but not unprecedented — hit a state whose grid had been
deliberately isolated from national interconnection and whose gas infrastructure had never been
winterised. The hazard was meteorological; the exposure was a design choice; the vulnerability was
regulatory. More than 200 people died, most of them indoors [@ferc2021].

**Europe, 2003 and 2022.** Two heat waves of comparable magnitude, twenty years apart. The first
killed an estimated 70,000 [@robine2008]; the second killed 61,672 on a hotter event [@ballester2023].
The hazard increased; the vulnerability fell; the toll fell slightly. That is what adaptation looks
like when it works — not a solved problem, a race.

In each case, the physical event is the least interesting variable. The interesting variable is the
one that policy touched, or failed to touch, before the event.

## The framework this book uses

The three terms produce a working frame applied consistently throughout:

**Hazard** is set by physics and past emissions. It cannot be negotiated in the short run, and every chapter that discusses it names its data source.

**Exposure** is set by the history of development. It can be changed, but slowly, expensively, and usually against entrenched economic interest.

**Vulnerability** is set by social arrangement: who has information, money, transport, adequate housing, and someone who will check on them. It is the most changeable in the short run, and the most often ignored because its improvements are not photogenic.

The frame also explains why this book does not open with a temperature graph. The graph matters, but if a reader wants to know why two places with the same weather suffer different death tolls, the answer lies in the last two terms, not the first.

## Why the distinction decides the budget

A framework is only useful if it changes what someone does on Monday morning. This one changes three
budget lines.

**Emergency response** is spending that acts on the consequences. It is the easiest to justify
politically, because it is visible and it happens after a photograph. It reduces neither hazard nor
exposure nor vulnerability; it reduces the duration of suffering.

**Adaptation** is spending that acts on exposure and vulnerability before the event. Its benefits are
invisible, which is why it is underfunded relative to response in almost every budget in the world.
An avoided death does not appear in a press release.

**Mitigation** is spending that acts on the hazard itself, with a lag of decades. It is the only
category that changes the physical baseline, and the only one whose benefits fall outside the
electoral horizon of the people who authorise it.

The three-term frame makes the trade-off explicit. A government that spends 95 per cent of its
climate budget on response has decided to accept the death toll and pay for the funerals. That is a
choice, and it is defensible only if stated as one.

## Where the framework is weakest

Intellectual honesty requires naming the places where the three-term decomposition does damage as well
as good.

**It can become a checklist that stops thought.** A report that assigns a number to hazard, exposure,
and vulnerability has not explained anything; it has restated the problem in three columns. The
framework is a scaffold for causal argument, not a substitute for one.

**The terms are not independent.** Vulnerability and exposure are correlated, because the same
households that are exposed are often the ones that are fragile. Multiplying the terms, as the
formula suggests, can therefore overstate the combined effect. In practice the decomposition is used
qualitatively rather than arithmetically, and should be.

**The global models that aggregate the three terms disagree with each other.** A review of global
multi-hazard risk assessments found that different models, applied to the same hazard, produce exposed
population estimates that differ by factors of two or more, largely because of where the exposure layers
come from and how vulnerability is proxied [@ward2020]. The framework survives this; the numbers built
on top of it should be read with the disagreement in view.

**It can depoliticise by technicalising.** Assigning vulnerability a score makes it look like a
property of a place that can be measured and then addressed with a programme, when it is often a
property of a relationship — who owns the land, who sets the rent, who gets the permit — that no
programme addresses.

**It struggles with cascades.** A framework built to explain one hazard at one moment is awkward when
the disaster is a chain, which is the subject of Chapter 2.

These weaknesses are survivable, and the alternative frameworks — a pure physical account, or a purely
political one — are worse. But a reader who uses the framework should know where it bends.

The strongest version of the criticism comes from inside disaster studies, and it is worth stating in
its own terms rather than defusing it. Gaillard argues that the field's central distinction — natural
versus social — has become a way of avoiding the question of who holds power, because a framework that
locates cause in "social arrangements" can describe those arrangements without naming the people who
maintain them [@gaillard2019]. Kelman, Gaillard and Mercer make a related point about the word
"disaster" itself: what counts as a disaster is defined by whoever is doing the counting, and the
definition carries an implicit judgement about whose losses matter [@kelman2012]. A framework that
treats hazard, exposure, and vulnerability as three neutral columns can absorb both criticisms without
answering either.

The alternative framework in widest use, the MOVE framework, keeps the components but insists that
vulnerability be defined by the characteristics of the exposed system rather than by outcome, and that
the political and economic drivers be named as part of the model rather than as background
[@birkmann2013]. It is a more cumbersome instrument, and this book does not adopt it wholesale — but a
reader who finds the three-term frame too bloodless should know that a more explicit alternative exists,
and that the choice between them is not settled.

## A note on language

The words used for these three terms carry assumptions, and the assumptions matter.

"Hazard" is neutral: a physical phenomenon with a probability and a magnitude. "Natural disaster" is not,
because it bundles the hazard with the outcome. "Catastrophe" is stronger still, implying a scale of loss
that no arrangement could have prevented.

The vocabulary chosen by an institution reveals its theory. An agency that speaks of "natural disasters"
is likely to fund relief and engineering. An agency that speaks of "disaster risk" is likely to fund
preparedness and land use.

The shift in official language over the past two decades — from "natural disaster" to "disaster risk
reduction" — is not cosmetic. It accompanied a shift in budgets, and it was resisted by institutions whose
mandate was relief.

## The 1931 China floods and the limit of the framework

A case that tests the framework's limit, and should be included because the framework is sometimes used to
explain more than it can.

The 1931 floods on the Yangtze and Huai rivers killed an estimated one to four million people, depending on
the source. The range is enormous because the records are incomplete, and the uncertainty is itself part of
the case.

Applying the three terms: the hazard was extreme rainfall and snowmelt; the exposure was a densely populated
floodplain with no flood control; the vulnerability was a rural population with no warning, no shelter, no
relief system, and a government in the middle of a civil war and an invasion.

The framework explains the toll, and it also shows its limit. At a certain scale, the social variables are
not independent levers that a policy could move — they are the structure of a state that cannot act. The
framework says where the deaths came from; it does not say that anyone in 1931 could have prevented them.

That is an important qualification for a book that recommends interventions. The recommendation applies
where there is a state with capacity and a society with resources. Where there is not, the framework is a
diagnosis without a prescription, and the honest response is to say so.

## The reader's own case

A practical exercise that the book recommends rather than sets.

Take a disaster you know — from the news, from your own country, from your family's history — and apply the
three terms.

**Hazard.** What was the physical event, in physical units? What is the source?

**Exposure.** Who and what was in its path, and why were they there? What decisions put them there?

**Vulnerability.** Who died, who lost, and who recovered? What determined the difference?

The exercise is revealing because the answers are rarely available in the coverage, and the gaps are
instructive. A report that gives the rainfall and the death toll and nothing in between has skipped the
interesting part.

The book's chapters are, in effect, worked examples of the exercise. The reader who can perform it on a new
case has learned what the book has to teach.

## A note on the numbers in this chapter

The figures used here are the ones that survived verification, and it is worth explaining what that means
in practice.

Global temperature, emissions, and sea level come from institutional datasets with published methods
[@wmo2025; @noaa2025; @ipcc2021]. They are the least contested numbers in the book.

Death tolls come from sources of varying quality, and the variation is stated: the Pakistan figure is from
the national disaster authority [@pakistan2022]; the European figures are excess mortality estimates
[@robine2008; @ballester2023]; the British Columbia figure is from the provincial coroner
[@bccoroners2022].

The framework itself — hazard, exposure, vulnerability — is a conceptual scheme with a literature, not a
measurement. It cannot be verified in the same way, and it should not be presented as if it could.

The distinction between a measured number, an estimated number, and a framework is the discipline that
Chapter 17 turns into a set of tests.

## Two terms that this book avoids

A note on vocabulary, because two words do most of the damage in this field.

**"Natural disaster"** is avoided in this book except where quoting. It bundles a physical event with a
social outcome, and the bundling is the error the framework is designed to undo.

**"Resilience"** is used sparingly, and defined when used. The word has acquired so many meanings — the
ability to bounce back, the ability to absorb, the ability to adapt, a synonym for strength — that it
functions as a compliment rather than a concept. A resilient system in one sense is a system that returns
to its previous state; in another, it is a system that changes. The two are different, and confusing them
leads to policy that rebuilds the vulnerability it just experienced.

Where the book means a specific thing, it says so: absorption, adaptation, or transformation, and which
one.

## What the framework cannot tell you

A note on the limits of the framework, stated directly so the book does not oversell it.

The framework cannot tell you **which** intervention to choose. It says where the deaths come from; it does
not compare a drainage scheme with a heat plan, because the comparison requires local costs and local
values.

It cannot tell you **how much** protection is enough. That is a question about acceptable risk, which is
political.

It cannot tell you **who** should pay. That is the subject of Chapter 13.

What it can do is tell you where to look, which is more than most frameworks manage.

## A final methodological note

The three terms are often treated as a taxonomy, and they are better treated as a set of questions.

Ask of any claim: what is the hazard, and how is it measured? Who is exposed, and why are they there? Who is
vulnerable, and what makes them so?

The questions produce answers that are usually partial, and the partiality is informative. A claim that can
answer the hazard question and not the other two is a claim about physics. A claim that can answer all three
is a claim about a disaster.

The book uses this procedure in every chapter, and the reader can use it on any case.

## What would prove this chapter wrong


This chapter argues that risk is the product of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, and that over a 10–20 year horizon vulnerability is the most productive place to work.

The falsifier is a variance decomposition with a stated threshold. Take a sample of events with recorded hazard intensity, recorded exposure (people and value in the affected area), and recorded vulnerability proxies (income, housing quality, age structure, access to services). If hazard intensity alone explains more than 90 per cent of the variance in death rates, and the social terms together add less than five percentage points, the chapter is wrong and the argument of Chapters 8 to 11 collapses.

A weaker version is available with existing data and has not come out the other way: cyclones of comparable strength produce death tolls that differ by two orders of magnitude, and the difference tracks income and shelter rather than wind speed [@sammonds2021; @undrr2022]. A dataset in which that relationship vanished would refute the claim.

The budget implication is falsified by outcome, not by argument. If countries that spent the largest share of their climate finance on response had the same mortality trend as countries that spent it on adaptation, the claim that the allocation matters would fail. That comparison is possible with the finance data and the mortality series, and the fact that it is rarely made is part of why the claim survives.
