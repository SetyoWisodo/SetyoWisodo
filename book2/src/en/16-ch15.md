# Households: What Can Be Done This Week

::: lead
Household adaptation is usually sold as a shopping list. The list is mostly wrong: it optimises for the wrong hazards and it ignores the one intervention that matters most, which costs nothing and is the hardest to do.
:::

## The honest starting point

The first thing to say about household adaptation is that the largest determinants of a household's risk are not within its control. Whether the drainage is maintained, whether the building code is enforced, whether the warning arrives, and whether the neighbourhood is protected are all public decisions.

This matters because the genre of household preparedness advice often functions as a way of transferring public responsibility onto individuals. A checklist that tells a household to stock three days of water is not a substitute for a functioning water system.

That said, there are things a household can do that measurably reduce risk, and they are worth separating from the things that merely feel like preparation.

## Why the advice usually fails

Household preparedness advice has a poor record of changing behaviour, and the reasons are known.

**It is generic.** A leaflet listing hazards that do not apply to the reader is ignored, correctly.

**It assumes resources.** The advice to store three days of water assumes containers and space. The
advice to keep documents in a waterproof bag assumes documents.

**It assumes a household that is already organised.** A single parent working two jobs is not
underprepared out of ignorance.

**It is delivered as a product.** Brochures, kits, and campaigns are the standard instruments, and they
produce awareness rather than action. The evidence on behaviour change consistently finds that a
conversation with a trusted person outperforms a leaflet.

The alternative that works is specific, local, and social: a person who visits, a list that names the
household, and an action that is small enough to do this week.

## What the evidence says about kits

The commercial preparedness industry sells a product that is difficult to evaluate, because the outcome
it prevents is rare.

The studies that exist find three things.

**Kits correlate with preparedness but do not cause it.** Households that buy kits are already the kind
of household that prepares; the kit is a symptom, not the intervention.

**The items most used are the cheapest.** Torch, batteries, water, and documents account for most of the
value. The expensive items — generators, water filters, satellite communicators — are rarely used and
often fail when they are.

**Training matters more than equipment.** A household that has practised using what it has performs
better than a household with better equipment and no practice.

The practical implication is that the marginal dollar is better spent on a conversation than on a kit,
and that the best kit is the one the household already owns plus the knowledge of how to use it.

## What actually reduces risk

**Knowing the hazard you actually face.** Not the generic hazard, the specific one. A household in a floodplain faces flooding, not wildfire. A household in a top-floor apartment in a heat island faces heat, not flooding. Preparedness should be hazard-specific, and most of it is not.

**Social contact.** The single most effective household-level intervention for heat and for many other hazards is having someone who will check. In the British Columbia heat dome, 56 per cent of the people who died lived alone (@bccoroners2022). A daily call during a heat event is a more effective intervention than most equipment.

**Documents and access.** Identity documents, property papers, medical records, and insurance details, stored in a form that survives water and is accessible if the house does not. This is unglamorous and disproportionately valuable, because the inability to prove ownership is a barrier to every form of assistance.

**Money that is reachable.** A small reserve that can be accessed without a functioning bank branch, and an understanding of what insurance actually covers. Many households discover after an event that their policy excludes the peril that occurred.

**A plan that has been discussed.** Where to go, how to get there, who is responsible for whom, and what to do if separated. The value is not in the document but in the conversation, which surfaces the assumptions that would otherwise fail under stress.

## What is mostly sold and mostly useless

**The preparedness kit as a commercial product.** A pre-packaged bag of items is not harmful, but its marginal value over a household's existing supplies is small, and the purchase can substitute for the planning that matters.

**Standby generators without fuel management.** A generator that cannot be fuelled after three days is a generator for three days. Fuel storage, rotation, and safe operation matter more than the machine.

**Sandbags without a plan for where the water goes.** Sandbags divert water. If the diversion is into a neighbour's property or into a stairwell, the intervention has moved the problem rather than solved it.

**Insurance purchased as a substitute for risk reduction.** Insurance transfers financial loss; it does not prevent physical harm. A household that is insured but not prepared is financially protected and physically exposed.

## The heat case, in detail

Because heat is the hazard where household action has the clearest evidence, it is worth being specific.

The people who die in heat are disproportionately old, living alone, and indoors. The interventions that reach them are social.

**Check on neighbours, twice a day, during a heat event.** Particularly older people living alone, people with mobility limitations, and people with cardiovascular or respiratory conditions.

**Open windows at night, close them during the day.** The most effective passive cooling strategy in a building without air conditioning, and the opposite of what many people do because they were told to keep windows closed for security.

**Use the coolest room.** In many apartments, one room — usually north-facing, lower floor, or with cross-ventilation — is materially cooler. Sleeping in it during a heat event is a real intervention.

**Drink before thirst.** Thirst is a late signal, particularly in older people, whose thirst response is blunted.

**Know the cooling options.** A library, a mosque, a shopping centre, a relative's house. The option needs to be identified before the event, and it needs to be reachable.

## The flood case

**Know the water's route.** Not the flood map's zone, but the actual path water takes through the street. This is learned from neighbours who have been there longer, and it is more accurate than the official map.

**Move valuables up, not out.** In a shallow flood, the difference between ground floor and first floor is the difference between loss and no loss. This requires a plan made before the water arrives.

**Do not drive through water.** The most common cause of flood death in vehicles is misjudging depth. Thirty centimetres of moving water can move a car.

**Keep the drains clear.** A blocked drain on a street is a collective problem with a household-scale intervention, and it is usually nobody's job.

## The household in a collective action problem

There is a structural reason why household adaptation cannot substitute for public action, and it is
worth stating precisely because it explains a persistent policy failure.

Many of the most effective household measures are only effective if neighbours do them too.

**Drain clearance.** One household clearing its drain does little if the street's drains are blocked
downstream.

**Roof strapping.** A household that straps its roof reduces its own damage; the household next door's
roof becomes debris that damages it.

**Evacuation.** A household that evacuates reduces its own risk; if its neighbours do not, the emergency
services are overwhelmed and the roads are blocked.

**Heat checks.** A household checking on its neighbours is effective; a street where nobody does is not.

These are collective action problems, and collective action problems are not solved by individual
choice. They are solved by coordination — a street association, a municipal programme, a norm.

The policy implication is that household programmes should be designed as collective programmes: a
street-level drill, a neighbourhood register, a shared plan. The unit of preparedness is not the
household but the block.

## The limits, stated plainly

Household adaptation cannot compensate for a house in the wrong place, a drainage system that does not work, or a warning system that does not reach. When those fail, the household measures buy time and reduce harm; they do not substitute.

The correct framing is that household action is the last line, not the first. A society that relies on it has decided to accept a higher death toll.

::: {.box title="Box 15.1 — A week's worth of preparation" kind="note"}
Not a shopping list. A sequence of conversations and small actions.

**Day 1.** Identify the specific hazard for this address, and the specific route water or heat takes.
**Day 2.** Write down the names and numbers of three neighbours, one of whom is older or lives alone. Agree to check on each other.
**Day 3.** Photograph documents and store them somewhere that is not the house.
**Day 4.** Read the insurance policy and find the exclusion that matters.
**Day 5.** Identify two places to go — one for heat, one for flood — and how to get there without a car.
**Day 6.** Fix one thing: a drain, a window, a loose roof sheet, a tree branch.
**Day 7.** Talk to the household about who does what. The conversation is the deliverable.
:::

## The counter-case: where household preparation was decisive

There are settings where household-level action genuinely made the difference, and they are worth naming to avoid overcorrecting into fatalism.

In the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, households that had participated in routine evacuation drills responded faster, and the difference was measurable in survival. In communities with strong neighbourhood associations, the initial response — before any agency arrived — was organised by residents.

In heat events, the households that survived were not necessarily the ones with air conditioning; they were the ones with social contact.

The pattern is that household action matters most in the first hours and for the most marginal people, and least as a substitute for public systems.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that household preparation is valuable but limited, that social contact is the highest-value household intervention, and that the commercial preparedness model misallocates attention.

It would be wrong if households with comprehensive equipment but no social contact had better outcomes than households with social contact and modest equipment, controlling for hazard. The heat mortality evidence points the other way.
