# Food: When One Harvest Becomes a Price Somewhere Else

::: lead
No single country feeds the world, and no single harvest failure starves it. But the global food system is thinner than it looks, and a thin market converts a local weather event into a price that a household four thousand kilometres away cannot pay.
:::

## The 2023 rice episode

In July 2023, India restricted rice exports. The country accounts for about 40 per cent of global rice exports, and the varieties affected represented roughly a tenth of the global market (@fao2023).

Within a month, the FAO All Rice Price Index rose about 10 per cent, reaching a fifteen-year nominal high (@fao2023). Thai white rice, the regional benchmark, rose faster. Import-dependent countries in West Africa and the Philippines scrambled to secure cargoes.

El Niño was developing, and every importer could see it. The restriction was a weather-driven policy response to a weather-driven risk, and it produced a price shock that fell hardest on countries with the least capacity to pay.

The important feature of this episode is not the size of the price move. It is the **thinness** of the market. Rice is the staple of half the world, but only about 10 per cent of global production is traded internationally (@fao2023). A small share of countries supplies that trade. When one of them restricts exports, the marginal supply available to importers can fall sharply even though global production has barely changed.

That is the mechanism. A harvest failure in one place becomes a food crisis in another not because there is not enough food, but because there is not enough food *for sale* at a price the poorest buyers can reach.

## Why exporters restrict, and why it makes things worse

Export restrictions are individually rational. A government facing rising domestic prices can lower them by keeping grain at home. The political benefit is immediate; the international cost is diffuse.

The collective result is well documented: when several exporters restrict simultaneously, world prices rise further than any single restriction would imply, and the countries that suffer most are those that import and cannot pay (@fao2022). The 2007–2008 food price crisis followed this pattern, and the 2023 rice episode was a smaller version of it.

The policy lesson is not that governments should be altruistic. It is that the international system has no mechanism to prevent a rational individual action from producing a collectively worse outcome — and that the cost is paid by people who had no part in the decision.

## Who absorbs the loss

In most low-income countries, food insecurity is concentrated among smallholder farmers and the rural landless. Smallholders are both producers and consumers, and the two roles are not equally protected.

When a harvest fails, the farmer loses income and must buy food at higher prices. When a harvest succeeds, the farmer faces lower prices for the surplus. The smallholder is on the wrong side of the price movement in both directions, which is why agricultural price volatility is a more reliable predictor of rural poverty than average rainfall (@fao2022).

The landless rural poor have no harvest to sell at all. They buy all their food, and they are the first to reduce meals when prices rise. The mechanism is not famine in the classic sense; it is a quiet reduction in quantity and quality that shows up in child stunting years later.

## Post-harvest loss: the food that is grown and never eaten

Roughly 14 per cent of food produced globally is lost between harvest and retail, and a larger share is wasted by consumers in rich countries (@fao2019).

The losses are not evenly distributed. In low-income countries, the losses are concentrated near the farm: poor storage, inadequate drying, pests, and the absence of a cold chain. In high-income countries, they are concentrated at the consumer end.

The policy implication is different for each. In low-income settings, the highest-return investment is often storage — a hermetic bag, a metal silo, a drying floor — which is cheap, decentralised, and does not require a functioning international market. In rich countries, the returns come from retail standards and consumer behaviour, which are harder to legislate and easier to ignore.

The 14 per cent figure is often quoted as though it were a global average that applies everywhere. It is not. A farmer in the Sahel loses a much larger share of the harvest than a farmer in Iowa, and the loss happens before the food ever enters a market.

## Anticipatory action: paying before the harvest fails

The standard humanitarian model responds after a crisis is visible: assess, appeal, deliver. That sequence reliably arrives late, because the assessment takes weeks and the appeal takes months.

Anticipatory action inverts the sequence. A forecast triggers a pre-agreed release of funds before the shock, based on a threshold — rainfall below a defined percentile, a temperature forecast, a price index — with the money going to households identified in advance (@ifrc2022).

The evidence base is still small, but the logic is sound: a household that receives cash before a drought sells fewer assets and keeps its children in school. The same money spent six months later buys less, because the assets have already been sold at distress prices.

The obstacle is institutional rather than technical. Anticipatory systems require an agency to spend money on the basis of a forecast that may be wrong. That is politically difficult, because a false alarm is visible and the avoided disaster is not.

::: {.box title="Box 6.1 — How the rice price figures are made" kind="note"}
The FAO All Rice Price Index is a trade-weighted index of international rice prices, expressed relative to a 2014–2016 base. It measures prices in export markets, not retail prices in importing countries.

Three cautions. First, the index does not reflect what a household pays; transport, tariffs, and local market power intervene. Second, the index is nominal — a fifteen-year nominal high is not necessarily a fifteen-year real high. Third, the +10 per cent monthly move is an average across varieties; specific varieties moved much more.

The World Bank's own food security reporting put the rise in some benchmark prices at roughly 20 per cent from the pre-ban level, depending on the variety and the reference date. Both figures are legitimate; they answer different questions.
:::

## The counter-case: when the system absorbed the shock

The 2023 rice episode could have been much worse, and the reason is worth recording.

Global rice stocks were adequate. The restriction affected a small share of traded volume, and other exporters — Thailand, Vietnam, Pakistan — increased shipments. Prices rose, then partially retreated. There was no repeat of 2008, when rice prices more than doubled and riots followed in several countries.

Part of the difference is stock levels. Part is that importing countries had learned to hold buffer stocks and to diversify suppliers after 2008. The system did not fail; it bent.

That is an argument for reserves and diversification, not for complacency. The system bent because the shock was moderate. A simultaneous failure of two major exporters, or a strong El Niño coinciding with a restriction, would have tested it differently.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that food crises are produced by market structure and policy response as much as by harvest outcomes, and that the most effective interventions are often storage, market access, and anticipatory finance rather than yield increases.

It would be wrong if global food crises consistently followed production shortfalls, with prices and hunger tracking harvests closely and policy playing no independent role. The 2007–2008 and 2023 episodes both show prices moving far more than production, which is the signature of a policy-driven shock.
