# Food: When One Harvest Becomes a Price Somewhere Else

::: lead
No single country feeds the world, and no single harvest failure starves it. But the global food system is thinner than it looks, and a thin market converts a local weather event into a price that a household four thousand kilometres away cannot pay.
:::

## The 2023 rice episode

In July 2023, India restricted rice exports. The country accounts for about 40 per cent of global rice exports, and the varieties affected represented roughly a tenth of the global market (@fao2023).

Within a month, the FAO All Rice Price Index rose about 10 per cent, reaching a fifteen-year nominal high (@fao2023). Thai white rice, the regional benchmark, rose faster. Import-dependent countries in West Africa and the Philippines scrambled to secure cargoes.

El Niño was developing, and every importer could see it. The restriction was a weather-driven policy response to a weather-driven risk, and it produced a price shock that fell hardest on countries with the least capacity to pay.

The important feature of this episode is not the size of the price move. It is the **thinness** of the market. Rice is the staple of half the world, but only about 10 per cent of global production is traded internationally (@fao2023). A small share of countries supplies that trade. When one of them restricts exports, the marginal supply available to importers can fall sharply even though global production has barely changed.

That is the mechanism. A harvest failure in one place becomes a food crisis in another not because there is not enough food, but because there is not enough food *for sale* at a price the poorest buyers can reach.

## Why exporters restrict, and why it makes things worse

Export restrictions are individually rational. A government facing rising domestic prices can lower them by keeping grain at home. The political benefit is immediate; the international cost is diffuse.

The collective result is well documented: when several exporters restrict simultaneously, world prices rise further than any single restriction would imply, and the countries that suffer most are those that import and cannot pay (@fao2022). The 2007–2008 food price crisis followed this pattern, and the 2023 rice episode was a smaller version of it.

The policy lesson is not that governments should be altruistic. It is that the international system has no mechanism to prevent a rational individual action from producing a collectively worse outcome — and that the cost is paid by people who had no part in the decision.

## 2007–2008: the rehearsal

The 2023 episode had a predecessor that was much worse, and the comparison is worth setting out because
it shows how a moderate shock becomes a crisis.

In 2007 and 2008, a combination of drought in Australia, poor harvests in Europe, low global stocks,
high oil prices, and — crucially — export restrictions by several major producers produced a spike in
staple food prices. Rice prices more than doubled. Wheat and maize rose sharply. Food riots occurred in
more than thirty countries, and governments fell in several [@fao2022].

The production shortfall alone did not explain the price movement. Global cereal production in 2007 was
only slightly below trend. What turned a modest shortfall into a crisis was the policy response: when
India, Vietnam, and others restricted exports, importers panicked and bought, which drove prices
further, which justified further restrictions.

The episode established a pattern that has been repeated since: **the food system's fragility is
concentrated in the policy response, not in the harvest.** A world with adequate stocks and a panic is
a world with a food crisis.

## The mechanics of a thin market

It is worth being precise about why a 10 per cent restriction in exports can produce a much larger
price movement, because the mechanism is not intuitive.

Rice is the staple of about half the world's population, but only around a tenth of global production
crosses a border. That traded share is the market's margin between surplus and deficit regions. When a
major exporter restricts, the margin shrinks, and the price has to rise enough to ration the remaining
supply among importers who all need it at the same time.

Three factors amplify the effect.

**Stock behaviour.** Importers that hold buffer stocks release them, which cushions the shock. Importers
without stocks must buy at any price. The distribution of stocks therefore determines who is hurt.

**Contract structure.** Most rice trade is on short-term contracts, so a restriction can bite within
weeks. Wheat and maize, with deeper futures markets, adjust more slowly.

**Thinness of substitutes.** Rice is not easily substituted in consumption, particularly in Asia, so
consumers cannot switch to another grain when the price rises. Demand is inelastic, which means the
price must move further to clear the market.

The policy implication is that buffer stocks and diversified suppliers are not relics of a
planned-economy past. They are the instruments that convert a thin market into a survivable one.

## Who absorbs the loss

In most low-income countries, food insecurity is concentrated among smallholder farmers and the rural landless. Smallholders are both producers and consumers, and the two roles are not equally protected.

When a harvest fails, the farmer loses income and must buy food at higher prices. When a harvest succeeds, the farmer faces lower prices for the surplus. The smallholder is on the wrong side of the price movement in both directions, which is why agricultural price volatility is a more reliable predictor of rural poverty than average rainfall (@fao2022).

The landless rural poor have no harvest to sell at all. They buy all their food, and they are the first to reduce meals when prices rise. The mechanism is not famine in the classic sense; it is a quiet reduction in quantity and quality that shows up in child stunting years later.

## Post-harvest loss: the food that is grown and never eaten

Roughly 14 per cent of food produced globally is lost between harvest and retail, and a larger share is wasted by consumers in rich countries (@fao2019).

The losses are not evenly distributed. In low-income countries, the losses are concentrated near the farm: poor storage, inadequate drying, pests, and the absence of a cold chain. In high-income countries, they are concentrated at the consumer end.

The policy implication is different for each. In low-income settings, the highest-return investment is often storage — a hermetic bag, a metal silo, a drying floor — which is cheap, decentralised, and does not require a functioning international market. In rich countries, the returns come from retail standards and consumer behaviour, which are harder to legislate and easier to ignore.

The 14 per cent figure is often quoted as though it were a global average that applies everywhere. It is not. A farmer in the Sahel loses a much larger share of the harvest than a farmer in Iowa, and the loss happens before the food ever enters a market.

## Anticipatory action: paying before the harvest fails

The standard humanitarian model responds after a crisis is visible: assess, appeal, deliver. That sequence reliably arrives late, because the assessment takes weeks and the appeal takes months.

Anticipatory action inverts the sequence. A forecast triggers a pre-agreed release of funds before the shock, based on a threshold — rainfall below a defined percentile, a temperature forecast, a price index — with the money going to households identified in advance (@ifrc2022).

The evidence base is still small, but the logic is sound: a household that receives cash before a drought sells fewer assets and keeps its children in school. The same money spent six months later buys less, because the assets have already been sold at distress prices.

The obstacle is institutional rather than technical. Anticipatory systems require an agency to spend money on the basis of a forecast that may be wrong. That is politically difficult, because a false alarm is visible and the avoided disaster is not.

::: {.box title="Box 6.1 — How the rice price figures are made" kind="note"}
The FAO All Rice Price Index is a trade-weighted index of international rice prices, expressed relative to a 2014–2016 base. It measures prices in export markets, not retail prices in importing countries.

Three cautions. First, the index does not reflect what a household pays; transport, tariffs, and local market power intervene. Second, the index is nominal — a fifteen-year nominal high is not necessarily a fifteen-year real high. Third, the +10 per cent monthly move is an average across varieties; specific varieties moved much more.

The World Bank's own food security reporting put the rise in some benchmark prices at roughly 20 per cent from the pre-ban level, depending on the variety and the reference date. Both figures are legitimate; they answer different questions.
:::

## The 2022 food crisis: when the shock came from outside the climate

Not every food crisis is climate-driven, and the 2022 episode is a useful control case.

The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 removed a large share of the world's wheat, maize, and
sunflower oil exports from the market, blocked Black Sea shipping, and drove up fertiliser and energy
prices. Food prices reached record levels, and the number of people facing acute food insecurity rose
sharply [@fao2022].

The climate contribution was secondary. Some of the affected regions were also experiencing drought,
but the dominant shock was conflict and the policy response to it.

Why this belongs in a climate book: because the food system does not distinguish between shocks. A
household facing a 40 per cent price rise does not care whether the cause was a blocked port or a
failed monsoon. The vulnerability is the same, and the instruments that protect against one protect
against the other.

The lesson for anticipatory action is that the trigger should not be climate-specific. A price index, a
market-access indicator, or a conflict alert can trigger the same pre-agreed finance as a rainfall
forecast, and the household outcome is similar.

## Why famines are now rare, and where they still happen

The decline in famine mortality over the past century is one of the great unremarked achievements, and
its mechanism matters for the argument of this book.

Famines in the classic sense — mass starvation caused by a harvest failure — have become rare in most
of the world. The reasons are agricultural (improved varieties, fertiliser, irrigation), economic
(trade, storage, and the ability to move grain), and political (early warning systems, humanitarian
access, and the fact that famine is now treated as a political failure rather than an act of God).

Where famine still occurs, it is almost always in places where one of those conditions has failed. The
2011 Somalia famine occurred in territory controlled by an armed group that restricted humanitarian
access. The 2017 famine in South Sudan occurred during a civil war. The common feature is not the
weather; it is the collapse of the systems that convert food availability into food access
[@checchi2013].

This is the food version of the book's central argument. The hazard — drought — is real. The famine is
produced by the failure of markets, states, and access. A world that has enough food and still has
famines is a world with a distribution problem, and distribution problems are political.

## What a drought does to a household, in sequence

The aggregate figures on food crises conceal a sequence of decisions that a household makes, and the
sequence determines whether the crisis is temporary or permanent.

**Month 1–2: borrowing.** The household borrows from relatives or a local lender to buy food. The
interest is often high and the terms informal.

**Month 3–4: selling productive assets** [@fao2022]. Livestock first, then tools, then land. The sequence is
rational — the household sells what it can replace most easily — but each sale reduces future income.

**Month 5–6: reducing consumption.** Fewer meals, cheaper food, and the adults eat less so that the
children can eat. This is the stage at which malnutrition begins, and it is often invisible because there
is still food in the house.

**Month 7–12: migration.** One or more members leave to find work. The household splits, which reduces
the immediate food burden and creates a new vulnerability: the migrant is exposed to a different set of
risks, and the household loses labour.

**Year 2 and beyond: recovery, or a new equilibrium at a lower level.** Households that kept their land
and livestock recover. Households that sold them do not, and their children are more likely to be
stunted, less likely to complete school, and more likely to be poor as adults.

The point of the sequence is that the intervention window is narrow and early. A cash transfer in month
two prevents the asset sales; the same transfer in month eight buys food and does not restore the
assets.

## The counter-case: when the system absorbed the shock

The 2023 rice episode could have been much worse, and the reason is worth recording.

Global rice stocks were adequate. The restriction affected a small share of traded volume, and other exporters — Thailand, Vietnam, Pakistan — increased shipments. Prices rose, then partially retreated. There was no repeat of 2008, when rice prices more than doubled and riots followed in several countries.

Part of the difference is stock levels. Part is that importing countries had learned to hold buffer stocks and to diversify suppliers after 2008. The system did not fail; it bent.

That is an argument for reserves and diversification, not for complacency. The system bent because the shock was moderate. A simultaneous failure of two major exporters, or a strong El Niño coinciding with a restriction, would have tested it differently.

## The Indian farmer who switched crops

At the household scale, adaptation to a changing rainfall regime is visible in cropping decisions, and the
evidence from India is unusually detailed.

Farmers in rainfed areas have been shifting from water-intensive crops to pulses and oilseeds, earlier
sowing, shorter-duration varieties, and in some districts a return to millets that had been abandoned when
irrigation arrived. The shift is driven by the observed failure of the monsoon, not by a policy.

Three things determine whether the shift succeeds.

**Seed access.** A farmer who cannot obtain seed for the new crop cannot make the change. Public
distribution systems that stock only the old varieties lock the farmer in.

**Market access.** A crop that cannot be sold is not an adaptation. Millets have a thin market, and a
farmer who grows them without a buyer has taken a risk without a return.

**Insurance.** A new crop is a new risk. Index insurance that covers the old crop does not cover the new
one, and the farmer who has no cover is exposed.

The pattern is general: household adaptation is constrained by the systems around the household, and the
systems are the policy's business.

## The seed that is not in the bank

A specific and under-discussed vulnerability: crop diversity is concentrated in a small number of
varieties, and the varieties that are not grown are not kept.

Genebanks hold a fraction of the diversity that exists in farmers' fields, and the fraction that is held
is unevenly distributed. The varieties that are most likely to be useful under a future climate — landraces
adapted to marginal conditions — are the ones most likely to be lost, because the farmers who grow them
are the ones under pressure to switch.

The loss is irreversible. A variety that disappears from cultivation and from a genebank is gone, and its
traits cannot be recovered by breeding.

The policy implication is that supporting the farmers who maintain diversity is a climate adaptation
measure, even though it appears in no adaptation budget.

## What a country can actually do

The chapter would be counselled if it ended in diagnosis without a menu, so here is one, ordered by
what the evidence supports.

**Hold stocks, and hold them transparently.** A publicly announced buffer stock stabilises expectations
as well as supply. The announcement is half the policy.

**Diversify suppliers.** A country that buys rice from three regions is less exposed than one that buys
from one, even at a slightly higher average price. The premium is insurance.

**Invest in storage at the farm level.** Hermetic bags and metal silos reduce post-harvest loss at a
cost per tonne far below the cost of importing the lost grain.

**Use anticipatory finance.** Pre-agreed, forecast-triggered transfers are cheaper than emergency
appeals and reach households before they sell assets.

**Keep trade open, and coordinate the closure.** The most damaging policy in a food crisis is a
unilateral export restriction. Regional coordination — even imperfect — is better than none.

**Measure the right thing.** A country that tracks production but not prices, stocks, and household
access is flying blind in exactly the conditions that produce a crisis.

None of these is expensive relative to the cost of a crisis. All of them are underprovided, because
the crisis they prevent is invisible.

## The supermarket as a climate institution

A final observation that connects the food chapter to the rest of the book.

In rich countries, the food system's climate vulnerability is concentrated in a small number of
institutions: the port, the distribution centre, the cold chain, and the retail network. A disruption to
any of them produces empty shelves within days, as the early weeks of the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated
— not because there was no food, but because the system that moved it was optimised for a different
pattern of demand.

The households most exposed to a food system failure are not the poorest in the world but the poorest in
rich countries: those without savings, without storage, and without the ability to buy in bulk when
supplies are disrupted.

This is the chain logic of Chapter 2 applied to food, and it is the reason food resilience is not only a
developing-country issue.

## The famine that was declared early

A case that shows anticipatory action can work at scale: the 2017 famine warning in the Horn of Africa.

In early 2017, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network and its partners projected that parts of Somalia,
South Sudan, Nigeria, and Yemen would face famine without a large and rapid response. The projection was
made months before the threshold was met, and it triggered a large-scale humanitarian mobilisation.

Somalia did not experience famine in 2017, though the drought was comparable to 2011. The number of deaths
was far lower than in the earlier event.

The attribution is contested, and the counterfactual is unobservable. What is documented is that the
response was faster and larger than in 2011, because the warning was issued earlier and was accepted.

The case supports the chapter's argument about timing. It does not prove that anticipatory action works in
general; it shows that early warning plus early finance can change an outcome, which is the claim that
matters.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that food crises are produced by market structure and policy response as much as by harvest outcomes, and that the most effective interventions are often storage, market access, and anticipatory finance rather than yield increases.

It would be wrong if global food crises consistently followed production shortfalls, with prices and hunger tracking harvests closely and policy playing no independent role. The 2007–2008 and 2023 episodes both show prices moving far more than production, which is the signature of a policy-driven shock.

## Smallholders are not a category

The argument that smallholders absorb the loss is true on average and misleading in detail, because
the category contains very different positions.

**Subsistence farmers with secure land** lose a harvest and eat less, but keep the land and can replant.

**Subsistence farmers with insecure tenure** lose the harvest and may lose the land, if the failure
forces a sale or a default on a loan secured against it.

**Tenant farmers** lose the harvest and the rent is still owed. This is the most fragile position in
agriculture and the least visible in statistics, which count land rather than contracts.

**Landless labourers** lose wages when the harvest fails, and face higher food prices at the same time.
They are the most exposed group and are usually outside agricultural policy entirely.

A policy that targets "smallholders" therefore reaches some of the vulnerable and misses others. The
distinction that matters is not farm size but the combination of tenure, contract, and alternative
income — which is why the most effective interventions in food crises are often cash transfers rather
than agricultural programmes.

## The food bank and the price spike

A small illustration of how price shocks reach households in rich countries, which is where most readers
live and which the chapter has mostly discussed at a distance.

A 10 per cent rise in staple prices is absorbed by a household with a stable income and a car. It is not
absorbed by a household already using a food bank, because food banks do not index their supply to prices
and their donors are themselves affected.

The evidence from the 2022 inflation episode in rich countries is that food bank use rose sharply, that
the composition of users shifted toward employed households, and that the institutions were not designed
for the increase.

The mechanism is identical to the one described for low-income countries: a price shock transmits through
the household budget, the coping strategies run out in a fixed order, and the last resort is charity. The
difference is the income level at which the sequence begins.

## The price that is not the price

A final mechanism: the price a household pays is not the international price, and the gap between them is
where the harm happens.

**Transport.** A landlocked country pays more for imported grain, and the cost rises with distance and
with the quality of the road.

**Tariffs and taxes.** A government that taxes food imports to protect domestic farmers raises the price
for consumers, and the poorest spend the largest share of their income on food.

**Market power.** A small number of importers, millers, and retailers can capture a large share of a price
rise, and the household pays it.

**Currency.** A depreciation in the exchange rate raises the local price of imports immediately, which is
why a food crisis often coincides with a currency crisis.

The policy implication is that the international price is only the first of several numbers, and that the
domestic instruments — tariffs, competition policy, exchange rate management — matter as much as the trade
regime.
