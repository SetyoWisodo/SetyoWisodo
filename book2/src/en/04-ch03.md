# Indonesia as Reader, Not Example

::: lead
Indonesia is usually introduced in climate writing as a warning — a country that will be hit hard. That framing is both true and unhelpful. Indonesia is more useful as a reader of the framework: a place where the three terms of Chapter 1 come apart most visibly, and where the most dangerous hazard is partly manufactured by the people living on it.
:::

## Why the archipelago is a hard test

A framework that only works in rich, temperate, well-governed places is not a framework. It is a description of those places. Indonesia is a useful test because it violates several assumptions at once.

It is an archipelago of more than 17,000 islands, with 99,093 kilometres of coastline according to the national geospatial agency — up from 95,181 kilometres in the 2008 UN count and 81,000 in earlier estimates (@big2013). The figure changed not because the coast grew but because the method changed: the definition of an island, the scale of the map, the tidal datum. That is a useful reminder at the outset. Even the most basic physical quantity in this book depends on a decision about how to measure.

Roughly two-thirds of the country is sea. Most of the population lives on the coastal plain or in lowland river basins. There are more than 500 district governments, each with its own land-use authority, budget, and capacity. And the country sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, so the hazard profile is not only climatic: earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions share the same exposed population and often the same institutions.

::: {.box title="Box 3.1 — The four things that make Indonesia a distinct case" kind="note"}
1. **Subsidence, not just sea-level rise.** In parts of Jakarta the ground is sinking faster than the sea is rising, so the relative rate is what matters — and it is set by groundwater extraction, not by the atmosphere (@abidin2011).
2. **Three-way flooding.** River floods, tidal flooding (rob), and rainfall-driven flash floods arrive separately and sometimes together. A levee built for one does not protect against another.
3. **Peat and fire.** Drained peatland oxidises, subsides, and burns. The resulting smoke is a transboundary health hazard that no single district can manage (@worldbank2016).
4. **Decentralisation.** Authority over land use, drainage, and permits sits with hundreds of local governments. National adaptation policy is only as good as the weakest local permit office.
:::

## Subsidence: a hazard made by people

Chapter 1 defined hazard as the physical event. Indonesia complicates that definition in a useful way, because in Jakarta part of the hazard is manufactured.

North Jakarta is sinking. GPS campaigns running from 1997 to 2010 recorded rates of 1 to 15 centimetres per year, and up to 20–28 centimetres per year at specific locations (@abidin2011). Cumulatively, parts of the north of the city have dropped more than three metres. Over the same period, global sea level rose about twenty centimetres (@ipcc2021).

The mechanism is straightforward. Groundwater extraction removes water from the pores of the sediment, the sediment compacts, and the surface drops. The pumping is not irrational: for many households and businesses, the aquifer is the only reliable supply, because the piped network does not reach them, or reaches them intermittently. Subsidence is therefore not a natural process that happens to a city. It is the physical consequence of a service-delivery failure.

This produces an important analytical consequence. If the relative sea-level rate is set substantially by subsidence, then a policy that builds sea walls while continuing to permit deep groundwater extraction is not adaptation. It is expenditure that will be outrun by the ground it is built on. The cheaper intervention — metering, enforcement, and a reliable piped supply — is institutional, not engineering.

The same pattern appears in Semarang, on the north coast of Java, where subsidence rates of 14 to 19 centimetres per year have been recorded. Both cities illustrate the same point: **the most effective climate adaptation in a subsiding delta is usually water management, not coastal engineering.**

## Three kinds of flood, three different defences

Indonesia is often described as flood-prone, as if flooding were one thing. It is at least three.

**River flooding** comes from rainfall concentrated upstream, arriving through a channel that may have been narrowed, straightened, or built over. The defence is catchment management: retention, reforestation, floodplain storage, and the willingness to keep land out of the channel's path. Jakarta's rivers are heavily modified, and the modification has generally been to move water downstream faster — which protects upstream property and increases the peak downstream.

**Tidal flooding (rob)** comes from the sea, driven by high tides, storm surge, and the seasonal monsoon. The defence is a combination of coastal protection, drainage that can discharge against a high sea, and — critically — not building in the areas that flood at spring tide. North Jakarta's seawall is a partial answer; during the 2007 and 2013 tidal floods, the wall was overtopped or bypassed in places, and the water stayed for days.

**Rainfall-driven flash flooding** comes from short, intense storms that overwhelm local drainage. The defence is permeability, retention ponds, and drainage designed for the storm intensities of the current decade rather than the 1970s. This is the flood type that most often produces the viral photographs, and the one most tractable to local government.

The three interact. A subsided neighbourhood floods on a moderate tide; if that same week brings a river flood, the drainage system has nowhere to discharge; if the river flood coincides with heavy rain, the system fails in a way that none of its designers modelled. This is the chain logic of Chapter 2, applied at city scale.

## Peat, fire, and smoke as infrastructure failure

In 2015, fires on drained peatland in Sumatra and Kalimantan burned for months. The smoke crossed national borders, closed schools in Malaysia and Singapore, and produced an estimated 100,000 premature deaths across the region (@worldbank2016). The World Bank put the cost to Indonesia at more than US$16 billion, roughly twice the cost of the 2004 tsunami reconstruction (@worldbank2016).

The fire is not a natural hazard in any meaningful sense. Peat accumulates over thousands of years and stores carbon and water. Drain it for plantation, and it oxidises, subsides, and becomes flammable. Once burning, it smoulders underground and reignites, which is why peat fires resist conventional firefighting.

This is an unusually clear case of a hazard created by land-use policy. The decision to drain was made for agricultural development, and the harm was displaced onto public health systems across a region. It also has a sharp equity dimension: the people who bear the smoke are not the people who profited from the drainage.

## Reefs and mangroves as infrastructure

The Coral Triangle — the waters around Indonesia, the Philippines, and Papua New Guinea — contains the highest marine biodiversity on earth, and Indonesian reefs support the livelihoods of millions of coastal people.

It is analytically useful to describe reefs and mangroves not as nature but as infrastructure. Mangroves reduce wave energy and stabilise sediment; a healthy reef flat dissipates swell before it reaches the shore. When they are lost — to aquaculture, to blast fishing, to coastal construction — the effect is exactly the effect of removing a sea wall, except that no budget line records the loss.

The policy implication is uncomfortable for accounting systems. Coastal protection built from concrete appears in a capital budget and can be procured. Coastal protection provided by a mangrove belt appears in no budget, is rarely counted as an asset, and is therefore the first thing traded away when land is needed. This is one of the ways in which public accounting systematically undervalues the cheapest form of adaptation.

## Decentralisation and the weakest permit office

Indonesia's post-1999 decentralisation gave district governments substantial authority over spatial planning, building permits, and local infrastructure. That is a democratic gain and an adaptation problem.

National adaptation planning can set standards. Implementation depends on more than 500 district governments, each with different fiscal capacity, technical staff, and political constraints. A district that permits construction in a floodplain to attract investment will generate local revenue and pass the flood risk downstream — literally and politically.

This is not unique to Indonesia; it is the general shape of the problem in any decentralised state. But Indonesia's scale makes it visible. The relevant question for adaptation is not whether a national plan exists but whether the permit office three levels down has the incentive, the data, and the technical capacity to refuse.

::: {.box title="Box 3.2 — How the subsidence numbers are made" kind="note"}
The Jakarta figures come from repeat GPS campaign measurements at monuments across the city, compared across survey epochs from 1997 to 2010 (@abidin2011).

Three limits matter when using them. First, GPS measures at points; interpolation between monuments can hide local variation, particularly where pumping is concentrated. Second, the rates are averages over the interval, not a current rate — extraction policy has changed since 2010. Third, "up to 28 cm/yr" describes specific locations, not the city; quoting it as a citywide rate overstates the average.

Satellite radar interferometry (InSAR) now provides far denser coverage, and independent InSAR studies broadly confirm the pattern, though with different local maxima.
:::

## What Indonesia teaches the framework

Four lessons transfer beyond the archipelago.

**Hazard can be manufactured.** Subsidence and peat fire show that the physical hazard term is not always exogenous. Where a hazard has a policy cause, adaptation spending that ignores the cause is likely to be wasted.

**Relative rates are what matter.** Sea-level rise is a global number; flooding is a local one. A city sinking at 15 centimetres a year is experiencing a relative sea-level rise of roughly fifteen times the global average, and its defence budget should be sized accordingly.

**Infrastructure is not only concrete.** Reefs, mangroves, and peat are infrastructure with no capital budget line, which is precisely why they are undervalued.

**Governance capacity is a risk term.** Decentralisation is not a flaw to be fixed; it is the operating environment. Adaptation that assumes centralised implementation will not survive contact with 500 district offices.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that Indonesia's distinctive contribution is to show hazard, exposure, and vulnerability being produced together — and that the most productive interventions are often institutional rather than engineering.

The argument would fail if, once subsidence is controlled for, Jakarta's flood outcomes turned out to be explained almost entirely by rainfall and sea level, with governance variables adding nothing measurable. That would reduce the Indonesian case to a standard coastal-city story with a geological complication.

The evidence so far suggests the opposite: districts with comparable exposure and rainfall show different outcomes, and the differences track drainage maintenance, permit enforcement, and pumping control — all institutional variables.

## Further reading

- **Abidin et al. (2011)** on Jakarta subsidence. The primary source for the rates, and a model of how to report measurement uncertainty.
- **World Bank (2016)**, *The Cost of Fire: An Economic Analysis of Indonesia's 2015 Fire Crisis*. The best-documented account of a policy-made hazard.
- **BIG (2013)** on the coastline count — read alongside earlier UN figures to see how method drives the number.
- **IPCC (2021)** on sea level, for the global rate against which local relative rates should be compared.
