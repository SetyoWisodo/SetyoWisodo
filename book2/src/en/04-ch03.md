# Indonesia as Reader, Not Example

::: lead
Indonesia is usually introduced in climate writing as a warning — a country that will be hit hard. That framing is both true and unhelpful. Indonesia is more useful as a reader of the framework: a place where the three terms of Chapter 1 come apart most visibly, and where the most dangerous hazard is partly manufactured by the people living on it.
:::

## Why the archipelago is a hard test

A framework that only works in rich, temperate, well-governed places is not a framework. It is a description of those places. Indonesia is a useful test because it violates several assumptions at once.

It is an archipelago of more than 17,000 islands, with 99,093 kilometres of coastline according to the national geospatial agency — up from 95,181 kilometres in the 2008 UN count and 81,000 in earlier estimates (@big2013). The figure changed not because the coast grew but because the method changed: the definition of an island, the scale of the map, the tidal datum. That is a useful reminder at the outset. Even the most basic physical quantity in this book depends on a decision about how to measure.

Roughly two-thirds of the country is sea. Most of the population lives on the coastal plain or in lowland river basins. There are more than 500 district governments, each with its own land-use authority, budget, and capacity. And the country sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, so the hazard profile is not only climatic: earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions share the same exposed population and often the same institutions.

::: {.box title="Box 3.1 — The four things that make Indonesia a distinct case" kind="note"}
1. **Subsidence, not just sea-level rise.** In parts of Jakarta the ground is sinking faster than the sea is rising, so the relative rate is what matters — and it is set by groundwater extraction, not by the atmosphere (@abidin2011).
2. **Three-way flooding.** River floods, tidal flooding (rob), and rainfall-driven flash floods arrive separately and sometimes together. A levee built for one does not protect against another.
3. **Peat and fire.** Drained peatland oxidises, subsides, and burns. The resulting smoke is a transboundary health hazard that no single district can manage (@worldbank2016).
4. **Decentralisation.** Authority over land use, drainage, and permits sits with hundreds of local governments. National adaptation policy is only as good as the weakest local permit office.
:::

## Subsidence: a hazard made by people

Chapter 1 defined hazard as the physical event. Indonesia complicates that definition in a useful way, because in Jakarta part of the hazard is manufactured.

North Jakarta is sinking. GPS campaigns running from 1997 to 2010 recorded rates of 1 to 15 centimetres per year, and up to 20–28 centimetres per year at specific locations (@abidin2011). Cumulatively, parts of the north of the city have dropped more than three metres. Over the same period, global sea level rose about twenty centimetres (@ipcc2021).

The mechanism is straightforward. Groundwater extraction removes water from the pores of the sediment, the sediment compacts, and the surface drops. The pumping is not irrational: for many households and businesses, the aquifer is the only reliable supply, because the piped network does not reach them, or reaches them intermittently. Subsidence is therefore not a natural process that happens to a city. It is the physical consequence of a service-delivery failure.

This produces an important analytical consequence. If the relative sea-level rate is set substantially by subsidence, then a policy that builds sea walls while continuing to permit deep groundwater extraction is not adaptation. It is expenditure that will be outrun by the ground it is built on. The cheaper intervention — metering, enforcement, and a reliable piped supply — is institutional, not engineering.

The same pattern appears in Semarang, on the north coast of Java, where subsidence rates of 14 to 19 centimetres per year have been recorded. Both cities illustrate the same point: **the most effective climate adaptation in a subsiding delta is usually water management, not coastal engineering.**

## Semarang: the same mechanism, a different economy

Jakarta gets the attention, but Semarang is the more revealing case, because it is smaller, poorer, and
the mechanism is visible in a shorter time.

The north coast of Central Java has been subsiding for decades, at recorded rates of roughly 14 to 19
centimetres a year in the worst-affected districts. The cause is the same as Jakarta's: groundwater
extraction by households and industry where the piped network is unreliable, plus the consolidation of
soft sediments under the weight of construction.

What makes Semarang different is the composition of the exposed population. North Semarang is a
working port and fishing district, with kampungs built directly behind the seawall and houses whose
ground floors are now below the level of the highest tides. The households that flood first are the
ones with the least capacity to move and the most dependence on the water they are being flooded by.

Two consequences follow.

**The adaptation budget is smaller.** Jakarta can contemplate a sea wall costing billions. Semarang's
municipal budget cannot, which means the intervention has to be cheaper and more distributed: drainage
rehabilitation, pumping, raising of roads and houses, and — critically — a managed reduction in
groundwater extraction that is enforced rather than announced.

**The retreat question arrives earlier.** In some districts, the practical choice is not whether to
retreat but how to manage a partial retreat that is already happening, household by household, as
properties become uninhabitable and are sold to those with even fewer options.

This is a pattern that repeats across the region, and it is the reason the book treats subsidence as a
governance problem rather than an engineering one. A city that can meter water and enforce the meter
has a slower problem than a city that cannot.

## The 2007 and 2013 Jakarta floods: a chain in one week

Two flood events in Jakarta show how the three flood types combine, and how the chain of Chapter 2
operates at city scale.

In February 2007, a period of heavy monsoon rain coincided with a high tide. The city's drainage could
not discharge into a high sea, the rivers were already full, and the pumps that might have moved water
had nowhere to move it. Roughly 70 per cent of the city was inundated at some point, and hundreds of
thousands of people were displaced. The economic loss was estimated in the billions of rupiah, and the
event prompted the construction of the eastern flood canal and a series of pumping stations.

In January 2013, a similar coincidence produced a similar outcome. The sea wall that had been built in
the interim was overtopped or bypassed in places, and the water remained for days. The lesson drawn by
the city was that the wall needed to be higher; the lesson the evidence supports is that a wall built
on subsiding ground, with a drainage system that depends on pumping, has a design problem that height
alone does not solve.

The two events together illustrate a general point about flood infrastructure: it is a system, and the
weakest component sets the performance. A wall without drainage is a bathtub. Drainage without pumping
is a pipe to nowhere. Pumping without power is a machine that stops in the storm that most needs it.

## Three kinds of flood, three different defences

Indonesia is often described as flood-prone, as if flooding were one thing. It is at least three.

**River flooding** [@ipcc2021] comes from rainfall concentrated upstream, arriving through a channel that may have been narrowed, straightened, or built over. The defence is catchment management: retention, reforestation, floodplain storage, and the willingness to keep land out of the channel's path. Jakarta's rivers are heavily modified, and the modification has generally been to move water downstream faster — which protects upstream property and increases the peak downstream.

**Tidal flooding (rob)** comes from the sea, driven by high tides, storm surge, and the seasonal monsoon. The defence is a combination of coastal protection, drainage that can discharge against a high sea, and — critically — not building in the areas that flood at spring tide. North Jakarta's seawall is a partial answer; during the 2007 and 2013 tidal floods, the wall was overtopped or bypassed in places, and the water stayed for days.

**Rainfall-driven flash flooding** comes from short, intense storms that overwhelm local drainage. The defence is permeability, retention ponds, and drainage designed for the storm intensities of the current decade rather than the 1970s. This is the flood type that most often produces the viral photographs, and the one most tractable to local government.

The three interact. A subsided neighbourhood floods on a moderate tide; if that same week brings a river flood, the drainage system has nowhere to discharge; if the river flood coincides with heavy rain, the system fails in a way that none of its designers modelled. This is the chain logic of Chapter 2, applied at city scale.

## Peat, fire, and smoke as infrastructure failure

In 2015, fires on drained peatland in Sumatra and Kalimantan burned for months. The smoke crossed national borders, closed schools in Malaysia and Singapore, and produced an estimated 100,000 premature deaths across the region (@worldbank2016). The World Bank put the cost to Indonesia at more than US$16 billion, roughly twice the cost of the 2004 tsunami reconstruction (@worldbank2016).

The fire is not a natural hazard in any meaningful sense. Peat accumulates over thousands of years and stores carbon and water. Drain it for plantation, and it oxidises, subsides, and becomes flammable. Once burning, it smoulders underground and reignites, which is why peat fires resist conventional firefighting.

This is an unusually clear case of a hazard created by land-use policy. The decision to drain was made for agricultural development, and the harm was displaced onto public health systems across a region. It also has a sharp equity dimension: the people who bear the smoke are not the people who profited from the drainage.

## Reefs and mangroves as infrastructure

The Coral Triangle — the waters around Indonesia, the Philippines, and Papua New Guinea — contains the highest marine biodiversity on earth, and Indonesian reefs support the livelihoods of millions of coastal people.

It is analytically useful to describe reefs and mangroves not as nature but as infrastructure. Mangroves reduce wave energy and stabilise sediment; a healthy reef flat dissipates swell before it reaches the shore. When they are lost — to aquaculture, to blast fishing, to coastal construction — the effect is exactly the effect of removing a sea wall, except that no budget line records the loss.

The policy implication is uncomfortable for accounting systems. Coastal protection built from concrete appears in a capital budget and can be procured. Coastal protection provided by a mangrove belt appears in no budget, is rarely counted as an asset, and is therefore the first thing traded away when land is needed. This is one of the ways in which public accounting systematically undervalues the cheapest form of adaptation.

## Decentralisation and the weakest permit office

Indonesia's post-1999 decentralisation gave district governments substantial authority over spatial planning, building permits, and local infrastructure. That is a democratic gain and an adaptation problem.

National adaptation planning can set standards. Implementation depends on more than 500 district governments, each with different fiscal capacity, technical staff, and political constraints. A district that permits construction in a floodplain to attract investment will generate local revenue and pass the flood risk downstream — literally and politically.

This is not unique to Indonesia; it is the general shape of the problem in any decentralised state. But Indonesia's scale makes it visible. The relevant question for adaptation is not whether a national plan exists but whether the permit office three levels down has the incentive, the data, and the technical capacity to refuse.

::: {.box title="Box 3.2 — How the subsidence numbers are made" kind="note"}
The Jakarta figures come from repeat GPS campaign measurements at monuments across the city, compared across survey epochs from 1997 to 2010 (@abidin2011).

Three limits matter when using them. First, GPS measures at points; interpolation between monuments can hide local variation, particularly where pumping is concentrated. Second, the rates are averages over the interval, not a current rate — extraction policy has changed since 2010. Third, "up to 28 cm/yr" describes specific locations, not the city; quoting it as a citywide rate overstates the average.

Satellite radar interferometry (InSAR) now provides far denser coverage, and independent InSAR studies broadly confirm the pattern, though with different local maxima.
:::

## The archipelago as a warning about "exposure"

Most exposure metrics assume a stable geography. A flood map, an asset register, a census tract — each
assumes that the land is where it was when the map was drawn.

Indonesia is one of the places where that assumption fails fastest. A deltaic plain that subsides 10
centimetres a year changes its exposure without any construction, any population movement, or any
policy decision. The asset that was above the design flood level in 2000 is below it in 2020, and no
register records the change.

This produces a specific kind of invisibility. A house that floods is counted as a flood loss. A house
that has sunk below the tide line and floods twice a year is counted as a flood loss too — but the
cause is not the flood. It is the ground, and the ground is not in the model.

The practical remedy is mundane and rarely implemented: a monitoring network that measures land level,
not just water level, and a planning system that updates its exposure maps on a schedule rather than
after a disaster.

## The eruption that was not a climate hazard and still tested the system

Not every Indonesian disaster in this book is climatic, and one is worth including because it tests the
framework's boundary.

The 2010 eruption of Merapi, in Central Java, displaced hundreds of thousands of people and killed more
than 350. The hazard was volcanic; the exposure was the densely populated slopes; the vulnerability was
the settlement pattern on fertile volcanic soil, which is fertile precisely because it is volcanic.

The response is instructive. The Indonesian system had a hazard map, an evacuation plan, and a network of
village volunteers. The evacuation worked, in the sense that the death toll was far below the 1930
eruption, which killed more than a thousand. The failures were in the aftermath: compensation, housing,
and the return to the exclusion zone.

The case matters for this book because it shows that the framework's three terms apply to hazards that are
not climatic, and that the institutional capacity built for one hazard transfers to another. A country
that can evacuate for a volcano can evacuate for a tsunami; the reverse is also true.

## The smoke that crosses borders

The 2015 peat fires are worth a further paragraph because they show a hazard with no local solution.

The fires burned on drained peat in Sumatra and Kalimantan, but the smoke travelled. Schools closed in
Malaysia, Singapore, and parts of Thailand. Airlines cancelled flights. Hospitals in the affected
regions recorded rises in asthma and acute respiratory illness, and a widely cited estimate put the
regional premature mortality at around 100,000 [@worldbank2016].

The governance problem is structural. The districts where the fires burn have an incentive to permit
the land use that causes them — plantation revenue is local — while the harm is regional and
transboundary. No district bears the cost of the smoke it produces, and no regional body has the
authority to override the district's permits.

This is the same mismatch that runs through the whole book, in its purest form: the unit that makes
the decision is smaller than the unit that bears the consequence. The remedies that exist are
therefore indirect — a moratorium on new peatland conversion, restoration of degraded peat, enforcement
against burning, and a certification system for palm oil that consumers can use, however imperfectly,
to shift the incentive.

None of these is a solution on the scale of the problem. All of them are attempts to make the decision
unit match the harm unit, which is the definition of environmental governance.

## What a reference work should carry

Writing about one's own country has an obvious hazard, and it is worth stating what this chapter tries
to avoid.

The first is **exceptionalism**: the claim that Indonesia is so distinctive that general lessons do not
apply. The chapter takes the opposite position. Indonesia is useful precisely because it isolates
variables — subsidence, decentralisation, peat — that exist elsewhere in weaker form.

The second is **representativeness**: the claim that Indonesia stands for the Global South. It does
not. It is a middle-income archipelago with a functioning state, a large civil society, and a
sophisticated meteorological service. Its problems are not the problems of a small island state or a
landlocked Sahelian country.

The third is **tourism**: the use of a country as scenery for an argument developed elsewhere. The
remedy is to let the Indonesian cases do analytic work — to show, as the subsidence case does, that a
hazard can be manufactured — rather than to illustrate a point already made.

## The city that is also a river

A final Indonesian case, because it is the one that best illustrates the interaction of the three terms
without any single dominant cause: Banjarmasin, in South Kalimantan.

The city is built on a delta, and its older neighbourhoods are on stilts above the water. The houses are
connected by wooden walkways, the river is the road, and the tide determines the day. This is not a
degraded version of a modern city; it is a sophisticated adaptation to a water environment, developed over
generations.

Three pressures are eroding it.

**Subsidence and sea-level rise**, which raise the water against the stilts and the walkways.

**Sedimentation and upstream land use**, which change the river's flow and its flood behaviour.

**Modern construction**, which replaces stilt houses with concrete buildings on filled land, which is
precisely the construction most vulnerable to subsidence and flooding.

The case matters because it shows that adaptation is not always an addition. Sometimes it is the
maintenance of an existing adaptation, and the failure mode is the replacement of a working vernacular
system with a modern one that performs worse in the local conditions.

This is the pattern that Chapter 4 described for heat, applied to water: the concrete building that requires
electricity is not an improvement on the building that did not.

## What Indonesia teaches the framework

Four lessons transfer beyond the archipelago.

**Hazard can be manufactured.** Subsidence and peat fire show that the physical hazard term is not always exogenous. Where a hazard has a policy cause, adaptation spending that ignores the cause is likely to be wasted.

**Relative rates are what matter.** Sea-level rise is a global number; flooding is a local one. A city sinking at 15 centimetres a year is experiencing a relative sea-level rise of roughly fifteen times the global average, and its defence budget should be sized accordingly.

**Infrastructure is not only concrete.** Reefs, mangroves, and peat are infrastructure with no capital budget line, which is precisely why they are undervalued.

**Governance capacity is a risk term.** Decentralisation is not a flaw to be fixed; it is the operating environment. Adaptation that assumes centralised implementation will not survive contact with 500 district offices.

## The island that is buying land

A case that shows adaptation can be a foreign policy: Kiribati's purchase of land in Fiji.

In 2014, the government of Kiribati bought about 20 square kilometres of land on Vanua Levu in Fiji, for
around US$8.8 million. The stated purpose was food security and, in the longer term, a place for the
population to go.

The case is instructive for three reasons.

**It is a hedge, not a solution.** The land is not a state, and the population has not moved. It is an
option purchased while the option was affordable.

**It changed the diplomatic conversation.** A country that has bought land elsewhere is negotiating from a
different position than one that has not.

**It is controversial at home.** A government that buys land abroad can be accused of abandoning the
country, and the accusation is politically serious.

The general point is that adaptation for a small island state is not only infrastructure. It is law,
diplomacy, and finance, and the instruments are unfamiliar to the adaptation literature.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that Indonesia's distinctive contribution is to show hazard, exposure, and vulnerability being produced together — and that the most productive interventions are often institutional rather than engineering.

The argument would fail if, once subsidence is controlled for, Jakarta's flood outcomes turned out to be explained almost entirely by rainfall and sea level, with governance variables adding nothing measurable. That would reduce the Indonesian case to a standard coastal-city story with a geological complication.

The evidence so far suggests the opposite: districts with comparable exposure and rainfall show different outcomes, and the differences track drainage maintenance, permit enforcement, and pumping control — all institutional variables.

## The Coral Triangle, stated with numbers

The reef case is often made rhetorically, so it is worth stating the scale.

The Coral Triangle — the marine area spanning Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Papua New Guinea,
the Solomon Islands, and Timor-Leste — contains roughly 76 per cent of the world's coral species and
about 37 per cent of the world's reef fish species, on about 1.6 per cent of the ocean's area. Indonesian
waters alone hold a substantial share of that diversity.

For coastal households, the reef is not an aesthetic asset. It is a fish nursery, a breakwater, and in
many places a source of construction material — which is the trap. A community that mines its reef for
lime and building blocks is consuming the structure that protects it, one house at a time, because the
reef has no price and the house does.

The same logic applies to mangroves. Indonesia has lost a large share of its mangrove area in the past
half-century, mostly to aquaculture and coastal development. The loss is usually recorded as an
economic gain — ponds produce revenue — and the protection that was lost never appears on the other
side of the ledger.

This is the accounting problem of Chapter 8 in a different form: the most valuable infrastructure is
often the least visible, and what is not on a balance sheet is what gets traded away.

## What a national adaptation plan can and cannot do

Indonesia has a national adaptation plan, and its limits illustrate a general problem.

**It can set a standard.** A national plan can define what every district must do — a hazard map, a
contingency plan, a minimum drainage standard — and it can make compliance a condition of transfers.

**It can provide data.** A national service can produce the hazard maps, the rainfall projections, and the
sea-level data that districts cannot produce themselves.

**It can finance.** A national plan can create a fund that districts can draw on, which converts an
unfunded mandate into a funded one.

**It cannot enforce.** A district that permits construction in a floodplain is making a local political
decision, and the national government has limited instruments to override it.

**It cannot substitute for local capacity.** A district with no engineer will not implement a plan, however
well written.

The practical conclusion is that national adaptation policy should focus on the things only a national
government can do — data, standards, finance — and should not attempt to specify local decisions. The
Indonesian case, with more than five hundred districts, makes this unavoidable.
