# Reading the Claims: How to Judge a Climate Promise

::: lead
This chapter has no new evidence. It is a set of tests — assembled from the preceding sixteen chapters — for judging a claim about climate risk before you rely on it, fund it, or vote for it.
:::

## Why a chapter on reading claims belongs here

### Why a chapter on reading claims belongs in a reference work

A reference book that supplies conclusions without teaching the reader to check them is not a reference book; it is an authority to be trusted. This book has argued throughout that the difference between hazard, exposure, and vulnerability is what makes claims checkable. The same applies to claims about the future.

The tests below are not original. They are the habitual practices of the researchers whose work this book relies on. Collecting them in one place is the contribution.

### Test 1 — What kind of claim is it?

Three kinds of claim appear in climate argument, and they have different evidential standards.

**Observational** [@cred2023]. "Global mean temperature in 2024 was 1.55 °C above the pre-industrial baseline." This is checkable against an instrument record and a defined baseline. It can be true or false, and there is no legitimate ambiguity about which.

**Estimative.** "Heat killed 61,672 people in Europe in 2022." This is a statistical estimate produced by a stated method. It is not a count. The method matters, and a claim without a method is not checkable.

**Framing.** "Climate change is a threat multiplier." This is a framework for organising observations. It is not true or false in the same way; it is more or less useful, and its usefulness can be assessed by whether it produces predictions that hold.

Most public argument fails by treating a framing claim as an observational one, or an estimate as a count. The first question to ask of any claim is which kind it is.

### The three questions that catch most bad claims

If the reader remembers nothing else from this chapter, three questions do most of the work.

**"Compared to what?"** A number without a comparison is a headline. Ten thousand deaths is large or
small depending on the baseline, the population, and the alternative. The comparison is usually
available and usually omitted.

**"Says who, and how would they know?"** The source matters, and so does the method. A government
statistic, an industry estimate, and a peer-reviewed study are different kinds of evidence, and the
difference is not always stated.

**"What would change the conclusion?"** If nothing would, the claim is not empirical. If something
would, the reader knows what to watch for.

These three are not a substitute for the seven tests that follow, but they catch more bad claims more
quickly than any of them.

## Test 2: what is the denominator?

A number without a denominator is a headline, not a finding.

"Ten thousand deaths from heat" means something different in a population of one million and a population of one billion. Deaths per 100,000, cases per 1,000, losses as a share of GDP — each answers a different question, and the choice of denominator is a choice about what the claim is about.

The most common abuse is comparing a total in one place with a rate in another, which produces a conclusion that neither number supports. The second most common is quoting an excess-mortality estimate as a body count: the two answer different questions, and the difference between them can be a factor of seventy, as the Puerto Rico figures showed [@kishore2018; @ballester2023].

## Test 3: is the range collapsed?

Good projections come with ranges, and the range is usually the point.

A sea-level projection of "0.3 to 1.0 metres by 2100" is not a failure of science; it is the honest output of models that disagree. A single number presented without a range should be treated as a summary of someone's judgment, not as a measurement [@ipcc2021].

The same caution applies to attribution statements, which are the class of claim most often quoted without their conditions. An attribution study answers a specific question — how much more likely was an event of this magnitude, in this region, with this definition of the event — and the answer changes if the definition changes. A sentence that reports the result without the definition is not reporting the study [@philip2022; @clarke2022].

The corollary is that the width of the range is information. A narrow range means the models agree; a wide range means the decision must be robust across futures, which is the argument of Chapter 14.

## Tests 4 to 7: change, framing, horizon, counter-case

### Test 4 — What would change this claim?

This is the falsification test applied to a single sentence. If no conceivable observation would change the claim, it is not an empirical claim.

"Climate change makes disasters worse" can be tested: compare disaster losses with and without climate change, using attribution methods. "Climate change is a hoax" can be tested: the temperature record is either consistent with it or not.

A claim that survives every possible observation is a belief, and it may be a true belief, but it cannot be used as evidence in an argument about policy.

### Test 5 — Who benefits from the framing?

This is not a test of truth. A claim can be true and still be selected for a purpose.

A coal company that emphasises adaptation over mitigation is making a selection, and the selection serves an interest, even if the individual claims are correct. An activist group that emphasises the worst-case scenario is making a selection in the other direction.

The test is not "does this serve an interest" — everything serves an interest — but "what is being left out, and would the conclusion change if it were included?"

### Test 6 — Is the time horizon stated?

Climate claims are exceptionally sensitive to the time horizon, because the physical system has long lags and the social system does not.

A claim about deaths in 2050 depends on assumptions about population, adaptation, and development that dominate the climate signal. A claim about deaths in 2024 is mostly about current vulnerability. Both are legitimate; conflating them is not.

The specific abuse is projecting a current death rate onto a future population without accounting for adaptation, which is how the more alarming figures are produced.

### Test 7 — Is the counter-case acknowledged?

A claim presented without the case that contradicts it is a claim that has not been tested, or has been tested and found wanting and hidden.

This is why every chapter in this book includes a counter-case. The counter-case is not a concession; it is the evidence that the argument has been examined.

::: {.box title="Box 17.1 — The seven tests, in one place" kind="note"}
1. What kind of claim is it — observation, estimate, or framing?
2. What is the denominator?
3. Is the range collapsed?
4. What would change this claim?
5. Who benefits from the framing, and what is left out?
6. Is the time horizon stated?
7. Is the counter-case acknowledged?
:::

## How to read a climate report in twenty minutes

### How to read a climate report in twenty minutes

A practical procedure, assembled from the tests above.

**First minute.** Read the summary, then the executive summary of the methodology. If there is no
methodology, stop.

**Next five minutes.** Find the definitions. What counts as a death, a loss, or a displaced person? Who
is included and who is not? The definitions usually explain most of the disagreement between studies.

**Next ten minutes.** Read the tables, not the prose. Check whether the totals add up, whether the
ranges are given, and whether the baseline is stated.

**Final four minutes.** Read the limitations section, if there is one. A report without limitations is
advocacy, which may be legitimate, but should be read as such.

The procedure is not a substitute for expertise. It is a way for a non-specialist to distinguish a
document that has been through a process from one that has not.

### How to use the reference list

A reference work's citations are not decoration. They are a set of doors, and it matters which ones the
reader opens.

Three tiers are worth distinguishing.

**The primary sources** — the studies, reports, and datasets that produced the numbers. These are the doors
to open when a figure matters to a decision.

**The synthesis sources** — the IPCC assessment, the UNDRR global assessment, the WHO reports — which
summarise and assess the primary literature. These are the doors to open when the reader wants the state
of the field rather than a single finding.

**The methodological sources** — the papers on excess mortality, attribution, and risk assessment — which
explain how a number is produced. These are the doors to open when the reader wants to judge a method.

The book's citations are weighted toward the first and third, because the second are easy to find and the
first are not.

## The question the book cannot answer

### The question the book cannot answer

One question is outside the book's scope and is worth naming, because readers will ask it.

**How much adaptation is enough?**

The question has no technical answer, because it is a question about acceptable risk, which is a political
question. A city that accepts a 1-in-100-year standard is accepting a certain number of deaths. A city that
accepts a 1-in-1,000-year standard is spending more to accept fewer.

The book can say what the costs and the consequences of each standard are. It cannot say which standard is
right, because that depends on what a society is willing to pay and what it is willing to lose.

What the book can do is make the choice explicit. A city that has never asked the question has answered it
by default, and the default is usually the cheapest standard that was available when the infrastructure was
built.

### What the reader should do with this book

Three uses, in increasing order of difficulty.

**Use the cases.** The book contains several dozen documented events. They are useful whether or not
one accepts the argument, because they are real and sourced.

**Use the tests.** The seven tests in this chapter apply to any claim, including the claims in this
book. Applying them to Chapter 9's assertions about infrastructure is a fair exercise.

**Use the framework.** The three terms and the chain logic are tools. They can be applied to a new case
— a city, a sector, a policy — without reference to the book's conclusions. That is the point of a
reference work.

### The claim that cannot be checked

Some climate claims are constructed so that no evidence could bear on them, and it is worth recognising
the form.

**"The models are wrong."** This is not a claim about a model but a claim about all models, and it
survives every successful prediction because the prediction is attributed to luck.

**"They are exaggerating on purpose."** A claim about motive, which no data can confirm or refute.

**"The science is not settled."** Science is never settled in the sense implied, and the statement is
used to mean "there is disagreement", which is true of every field and says nothing about the direction
or magnitude of the evidence.

The correct response is not to argue about the motive but to restate the empirical claim and ask what
would falsify it. If the answer is "nothing", the claim is a position, not a finding, and should be
treated as such — in either direction.

## Applying the tests

### Applying the tests: three worked examples

**Example 1.** "Climate change will displace 200 million people by 2050."

This is an estimate with a wide range, and the figure is sensitive to definitions of displacement, assumptions about adaptation, and the treatment of voluntary migration. The number is not fabricated; it is one point in a range that other studies place much lower. The correct rendering is a range with a stated definition, and the correct use is as an order of magnitude.

**Example 2.** "Bangladesh's cyclone deaths fell from 300,000 to 26, proving adaptation works."

The comparison is real and the direction is right. The caveats: the storms were not identical; the death toll is not the same as the harm (displacement and economic loss rose); and the comparison covers one hazard in one country. The claim is supportable with those qualifications and misleading without them.

**Example 3.** "The death toll from disasters has fallen 90 per cent, so climate risk is exaggerated."

The first clause is true (@cred2023). The second does not follow. The fall is explained by reduced vulnerability to specific hazards — mainly famine — while losses and exposure rose. Using a true trend to support a conclusion it does not support is the most common form of misleading claim, and it is not usually dishonest; it is usually a category error.

### The test that catches the most sophisticated errors

The most dangerous claims are not the crude ones. They are the ones that are accurate in every particular
and misleading in the aggregate.

Three forms recur.

**The true average.** A figure that is correct on average and hides a distribution that matters. A
national average rainfall that conceals a region in drought.

**The true trend.** A trend that is real and is presented as a mechanism. Deaths fell over the century;
that does not establish that they will keep falling.

**The true comparison.** A comparison that is valid and irrelevant. Comparing a country to a neighbour
with a different income, geography, and history.

The defence is the same in each case: ask what the number is a number of, and what it would take to
connect it to the conclusion. That is the whole discipline, and it is harder than it sounds.

### What this book would have to show to be wrong

Having set tests for others, the same tests apply here.

**The central claim** is that climate disasters are social disasters: that the death toll is set primarily by exposure and vulnerability rather than by hazard magnitude.

**It would be falsified** if, across a large sample of events, death tolls tracked hazard magnitude closely with social variables contributing nothing. The evidence assembled here — Bangladesh, Texas, Europe, Puerto Rico, Simeulue — runs the other way, but the sample is selective and the counter-evidence exists.

**It would be weakened** if the interventions recommended here — warnings, registries, social contact, maintenance, land-use rules — were found to have no effect once income and state capacity were controlled for. Some of the evidence on social capital is correlational, and the causal direction is not always established.

**It would be superseded** if adaptation succeeded so thoroughly that the vulnerability term became negligible. That is the outcome the book argues for, and it would make the book unnecessary. A reference work on vulnerability should be glad to become obsolete.

## What this book would have to show to be wrong

### A closing note on confidence

The book has argued that the death toll of a climate disaster is set primarily by social arrangements,
and it has been at pains throughout to say where that argument is strong and where it is weak.

It is strong where the evidence is comparative: the same hazard producing very different outcomes in
places with different institutions. Bangladesh, Texas, Puerto Rico, and Simeulue are cases of that kind,
and the pattern is not seriously contested.

It is weaker where the evidence is correlational: social capital predicts recovery, but the causal
direction is contested. It is weaker still where the evidence is prospective: the projections of future
displacement and future loss are scenarios, not forecasts.

A reader who takes the strong evidence and treats it as sufficient for the whole argument will be
overconfident. A reader who takes the weak evidence and treats it as refuting the whole argument will be
wrong. The book's claim is that the strong evidence is sufficient to justify the direction of policy,
and that the weak evidence is a reason for humility about the magnitude.

### A reading protocol for a number in the news

The tests in this chapter are designed for documents. Most readers encounter climate claims in shorter
form, and a compressed protocol is useful.

**When you see a death toll.** Ask: is this direct deaths or excess mortality? The difference can be a
factor of seventy, as in Puerto Rico [@kishore2018].

**When you see a loss figure.** Ask: insured or total? Global figures are dominated by insured losses in
rich countries [@undrr2022].

**When you see a projection.** Ask: what scenario, what baseline, and is the range given?

**When you see a comparison.** Ask: compared to what, in which year, and with what population?

**When you see a percentage.** Ask: a percentage of what, and over what period?

Five questions, each answerable in a sentence, and each of them catches a class of error that is common in
reporting and in advocacy.

### The last word, which is a caution

The book has argued that climate disasters are social disasters, and it has tried to be precise about what
that means. The precision matters, because the slogan is used to make claims it cannot support.

A disaster is social in the sense that the death toll is determined by social arrangements. It is not
social in the sense that the hazard is imaginary, or that any particular government could have prevented
any particular death, or that the people who died were responsible for their own vulnerability.

The distinction is the difference between an argument and a slogan, and it is the difference this book has
tried to maintain on every page.

### The claim the reader will meet most often

One claim deserves a specific note because it is ubiquitous and is almost always presented without the
qualification that makes it meaningful.

**"Climate change will cause X million deaths by 2050."**

The figure is usually produced by a model that combines a hazard projection with a dose-response
relationship and a population projection. Three assumptions dominate the result.

**The adaptation assumption.** Most such models assume no adaptation, or assume adaptation at the current
rate. A model that assumed effective adaptation would produce a much lower figure.

**The development assumption.** Mortality from climate-sensitive causes falls sharply with income. A
projection that assumes stagnant incomes produces a higher figure than one that assumes growth.

**The baseline.** A model that counts all deaths attributable to temperature, including cold, produces a
different figure from one that counts only heat.

The figure is not fabricated. It is a scenario, and its value depends entirely on the assumptions, which
are rarely stated in the headline.

## The tests applied to this book's own numbers

### The test applied to this book's own numbers

Consistency requires that the book's own figures be held to the standard it recommends.

**The death tolls.** The book uses direct deaths where that is what the source reports (Pakistan 1,739;
Europe 2022 61,672 excess; Puerto Rico 64 official and 4,645 excess). The basis is stated each time.

**The losses.** The book uses damage and loss figures with their source and their definition (Pakistan
US$14.9 billion damage and US$15.2 billion losses).

**The projections.** The book treats projections as scenarios and states the range where one exists.

**The gaps.** Where verification failed, the book says so. Five claims in the claim ledger remain
unverified and are hedged or omitted in the text.

The reader who applies the seven tests to this book should find that the tests are answerable. That is the
minimum standard for a reference work, and it is not met by most.

### The tests applied to a policy proposal

A short worked example, because the tests are easier to use on a concrete proposal.

**The proposal.** A city announces a 10-year, US$500 million programme to protect itself from flooding.

**Test 1 — what kind of claim?** The proposal contains observations (the city floods), estimates (the
programme will protect 200,000 people), and framing (flooding is a security issue). Each needs separate
scrutiny.

**Test 2 — the denominator?** "200,000 people protected" — out of how many exposed? What share of the
city's flood risk does the programme address?

**Test 3 — is the range collapsed?** The protection level is stated as a single number. Under what sea-level
and rainfall projection?

**Test 4 — what would change the conclusion?** If the programme is not cheaper than the damage it avoids,
it should not be built.

**Test 5 — who benefits?** Which neighbourhoods are inside the protection line, and which are outside?

**Test 6 — the time horizon?** The programme's design life and the sea-level projection's horizon are not
stated.

**Test 7 — the counter-case?** Is there a comparable city whose programme failed, and is that discussed?

Six of the seven questions are answerable from the proposal or from a short request. The seventh determines
whether the proposal is serious.

### A final test: does the argument survive its own questions?

Applied to this book, the seven tests produce the following.

**Kind of claim.** The book makes observational claims (with sources), estimative claims (with methods),
and framing claims (hazard, exposure, vulnerability). It labels them.

**Denominator.** Where a figure appears without one, it is because the source does not provide one, and the
text says so where the omission matters.

**Range.** Ranges are given where the source gives them, and the text flags the cases where a single figure
is a point estimate.

**Falsification.** Every chapter has a section stating what would prove it wrong.

**Interest.** The book is written by an author with a position: that vulnerability is the productive place
to work. That position is stated.

**Time horizon.** Stated where it matters, particularly for projections.

**Counter-case.** Every chapter has one.

The book does not claim to pass all seven perfectly. It claims that a reader can apply them, which is the
only test a reference work should set for itself.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter is a set of tests, not an argument, so the falsification question is different: what would show that the tests are wrong?

The first test is calibration. If the claims that survive the seven questions were no more accurate, on average, than the claims that fail them, the tests would be worthless. That is measurable: take a sample of climate claims, apply the tests, then score the claims against what later happened. The forecasting literature suggests that structured reasoning improves accuracy modestly and reliably, but the effect is small enough that a null result in a climate-specific sample would count against this chapter [@tetlock2015].

The second test is usability. If a reader with no climate training cannot apply tests one, two, and four to a newspaper article after reading this chapter, the tests fail as a reference tool regardless of their validity. That is testable with a small trial: give the chapter to two groups, give the article to both, and compare the quality of their written assessments.

The third test is the book's own. A reader who applies the seven questions to this book should find at least one place where the book fails its own standard. If the book passes all seven cleanly, the tests are too weak — a set of questions that never bites is decoration. The weakest point is the falsification sections of the earlier chapters: several state a direction without a threshold, and a reader is entitled to mark that down [@gaillard2019].

## The last page

A book about crisis ends with a claim that is not about crisis.

The claim is that the arrangements which determine who dies in a disaster are ordinary arrangements: the
rent, the drain, the clinic, the bus, the register, the contract. They are made in ordinary times, by
ordinary decisions, and they can be made differently.

The reader who has followed the argument to this page has the tools to ask the question of any policy: who
is inside the protection line, who is outside, and who decided.

That question is the book.
