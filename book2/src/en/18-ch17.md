# Reading the Claims: How to Judge a Climate Promise

::: lead
This chapter has no new evidence. It is a set of tests — assembled from the preceding sixteen chapters — for judging a claim about climate risk before you rely on it, fund it, or vote for it.
:::

## Why a chapter on reading claims belongs in a reference work

A reference book that supplies conclusions without teaching the reader to check them is not a reference book; it is an authority to be trusted. This book has argued throughout that the difference between hazard, exposure, and vulnerability is what makes claims checkable. The same applies to claims about the future.

The tests below are not original. They are the habitual practices of the researchers whose work this book relies on. Collecting them in one place is the contribution.

## Test 1 — What kind of claim is it?

Three kinds of claim appear in climate argument, and they have different evidential standards.

**Observational.** "Global mean temperature in 2024 was 1.55 °C above the pre-industrial baseline." This is checkable against an instrument record and a defined baseline. It can be true or false, and there is no legitimate ambiguity about which.

**Estimative.** "Heat killed 61,672 people in Europe in 2022." This is a statistical estimate produced by a stated method. It is not a count. The method matters, and a claim without a method is not checkable.

**Framing.** "Climate change is a threat multiplier." This is a framework for organising observations. It is not true or false in the same way; it is more or less useful, and its usefulness can be assessed by whether it produces predictions that hold.

Most public argument fails by treating a framing claim as an observational one, or an estimate as a count. The first question to ask of any claim is which kind it is.

## Test 2 — What is the denominator?

A number without a denominator is a headline, not a finding.

"Ten thousand deaths from heat" means something different in a population of one million and a population of one billion. Deaths per 100,000, cases per 1,000, losses as a share of GDP — each answers a different question, and the choice of denominator is a choice about what the claim is about.

The most common abuse is comparing a total in one place with a rate in another, which produces a conclusion that neither number supports.

## Test 3 — Is the range collapsed?

Good projections come with ranges, and the range is usually the point.

A sea-level projection of "0.3 to 1.0 metres by 2100" is not a failure of science; it is the honest output of models that disagree. A single number presented without a range should be treated as a summary of someone's judgment, not as a measurement.

The corollary is that the width of the range is information. A narrow range means the models agree; a wide range means the decision must be robust across futures, which is the argument of Chapter 14.

## Test 4 — What would change this claim?

This is the falsification test applied to a single sentence. If no conceivable observation would change the claim, it is not an empirical claim.

"Climate change makes disasters worse" can be tested: compare disaster losses with and without climate change, using attribution methods. "Climate change is a hoax" can be tested: the temperature record is either consistent with it or not.

A claim that survives every possible observation is a belief, and it may be a true belief, but it cannot be used as evidence in an argument about policy.

## Test 5 — Who benefits from the framing?

This is not a test of truth. A claim can be true and still be selected for a purpose.

A coal company that emphasises adaptation over mitigation is making a selection, and the selection serves an interest, even if the individual claims are correct. An activist group that emphasises the worst-case scenario is making a selection in the other direction.

The test is not "does this serve an interest" — everything serves an interest — but "what is being left out, and would the conclusion change if it were included?"

## Test 6 — Is the time horizon stated?

Climate claims are exceptionally sensitive to the time horizon, because the physical system has long lags and the social system does not.

A claim about deaths in 2050 depends on assumptions about population, adaptation, and development that dominate the climate signal. A claim about deaths in 2024 is mostly about current vulnerability. Both are legitimate; conflating them is not.

The specific abuse is projecting a current death rate onto a future population without accounting for adaptation, which is how the more alarming figures are produced.

## Test 7 — Is the counter-case acknowledged?

A claim presented without the case that contradicts it is a claim that has not been tested, or has been tested and found wanting and hidden.

This is why every chapter in this book includes a counter-case. The counter-case is not a concession; it is the evidence that the argument has been examined.

## Applying the tests: three worked examples

**Example 1.** "Climate change will displace 200 million people by 2050."

This is an estimate with a wide range, and the figure is sensitive to definitions of displacement, assumptions about adaptation, and the treatment of voluntary migration. The number is not fabricated; it is one point in a range that other studies place much lower. The correct rendering is a range with a stated definition, and the correct use is as an order of magnitude.

**Example 2.** "Bangladesh's cyclone deaths fell from 300,000 to 26, proving adaptation works."

The comparison is real and the direction is right. The caveats: the storms were not identical; the death toll is not the same as the harm (displacement and economic loss rose); and the comparison covers one hazard in one country. The claim is supportable with those qualifications and misleading without them.

**Example 3.** "The death toll from disasters has fallen 90 per cent, so climate risk is exaggerated."

The first clause is true (@cred2023). The second does not follow. The fall is explained by reduced vulnerability to specific hazards — mainly famine — while losses and exposure rose. Using a true trend to support a conclusion it does not support is the most common form of misleading claim, and it is not usually dishonest; it is usually a category error.

## What this book would have to show to be wrong

Having set tests for others, the same tests apply here.

**The central claim** is that climate disasters are social disasters: that the death toll is set primarily by exposure and vulnerability rather than by hazard magnitude.

**It would be falsified** if, across a large sample of events, death tolls tracked hazard magnitude closely with social variables contributing nothing. The evidence assembled here — Bangladesh, Texas, Europe, Puerto Rico, Simeulue — runs the other way, but the sample is selective and the counter-evidence exists.

**It would be weakened** if the interventions recommended here — warnings, registries, social contact, maintenance, land-use rules — were found to have no effect once income and state capacity were controlled for. Some of the evidence on social capital is correlational, and the causal direction is not always established.

**It would be superseded** if adaptation succeeded so thoroughly that the vulnerability term became negligible. That is the outcome the book argues for, and it would make the book unnecessary. A reference work on vulnerability should be glad to become obsolete.
