# Money: Insurance, Aid, and Who Counts as Worth Saving

::: lead
Two curves have been diverging for thirty years. The number of people killed by disasters has fallen. The cost of disasters has risen. The gap between them is where this chapter lives, because it is the gap between being protected and being paid for.
:::

::: {.box title="Box 8.1 — How insurance penetration is measured" kind="note"}
Insurance penetration is usually reported as premiums as a share of GDP, or as the share of catastrophe losses that are insured.

Both understate the gap. Premiums as a share of GDP are low in poor countries partly because incomes are low, not only because cover is absent. The share of losses insured is closer to the relevant question, and the figures are stark: in high-income countries the majority of catastrophe losses are insured; in low- and middle-income countries the majority are not (@undrr2022).

A third measure is more useful for policy: the share of households with any cover against the specific peril they face. It is rarely collected.
:::

## The divergence

Global mortality from weather-related disasters has declined by more than 90 per cent since the 1920s, while population roughly quadrupled (@cred2023). Global reported disaster losses, in contrast, have risen steadily, reaching several hundred billion dollars a year in the last decade (@undrr2022).

Both trends are real, and together they describe something important: the world has become much better at keeping people alive and much worse at keeping them whole.

The improvement in survival is attributable to early warning, shelters, response capacity, and the disappearance of famine as a mass killer in most regions. The rise in losses is attributable to exposure — more assets, more valuable, in more dangerous places — and to the increasing frequency of extreme events (@undrr2022).

A society that measures only deaths will conclude it is winning. A society that measures only losses will conclude it is losing. Neither measure alone describes the situation.

## What the divergence actually contains

The two curves — falling deaths, rising losses — are usually presented as a paradox. Decomposed, they
are three separate trends, and each has a different cause.

**Deaths per event have fallen**, because early warning, shelters, and response capacity improved. This
is the good news, and it is real.

**Losses per event have risen**, because there are more assets in the path of the hazard, and because
the assets are more valuable. A house built in 1970 and a house built in 2020 in the same place have
very different replacement costs, and the second is what the loss statistics record.

**The number of events has risen**, for weather-related hazards, in most regions, over the period
[@undrr2022]. This is the part that is attributable to climate change, and it is the part that is not
in dispute in the scientific literature, though it is disputed in public argument.

The three trends combine in a way that makes any single number misleading. A country can report
falling deaths, rising losses, and more events simultaneously, and all three statements can be true
without contradiction. A reader who takes any one as evidence about the others will be wrong.

## The humanitarian financing gap, in numbers

The architecture of international disaster finance has a structural feature that determines most of
its failures: it is discretionary.

An appeal is launched, pledges are made, and money arrives. The gap between what is requested and what
is received is the funding gap, and it is persistent. For major appeals it has averaged roughly half to
two-thirds of the request over the past decade, with large variation by crisis and by the attention the
crisis receives.

The consequence is triage. Agencies allocate scarce funds across crises, and the allocation follows
attention as much as need. A sudden-onset disaster with television coverage is funded; a slow-onset
crisis with none is not.

This is the aid version of the attention problem in Chapter 2, and it has the same remedy: a
pre-agreed, rules-based allocation that is not contingent on coverage.

## Insurance is a signal, not a solution

Insurance prices risk. When an insurer withdraws from a region or raises premiums sharply, it is transmitting information that the public system has not processed: the risk has become too high to carry at a price anyone will pay.

In the last decade, insurers have withdrawn from parts of California, Florida, and Australia, and reinsurance costs have risen globally. This is not a failure of the insurance industry. It is the industry doing what it is designed to do: declining to underwrite risk it cannot price.

The consequence is a transfer of risk from the private to the public sector, and from the public sector to households. A household that cannot insure its home is self-insured, which in practice means it bears the loss alone.

This matters for equity because insurance penetration is already unequal. In high-income countries, most catastrophe losses are insured. In low- and middle-income countries, the vast majority are not — often less than 10 per cent (@undrr2022). The global loss figures, which are dominated by insured losses, therefore describe the experience of the rich world and systematically understate the burden in the poor world.

## Aid: the timing problem

Post-disaster aid is the main instrument of international solidarity, and it is structurally late.

The sequence is familiar: an event occurs, an assessment is conducted, an appeal is launched, pledges are made, and funds arrive. Each step takes weeks to months. By the time the money reaches a household, the household has already made the decisions that determine its recovery: which assets to sell, whether to withdraw children from school, whether to borrow at informal rates.

This is why the same dollar is worth more before the event than after it. A cash transfer delivered before a flood allows a household to move livestock and store grain; the same transfer after the flood pays for what has already been lost.

Anticipatory action, discussed in Chapter 6, is the response to this timing problem. Its obstacle is political rather than technical: a government that releases funds on a forecast risks being seen to have wasted money if the forecast fails, and receives no credit for the disaster that did not happen (@ifrc2022).

## How a sovereign pays for a disaster

A government facing a large disaster has four sources of money, and the order in which it uses them
determines the economic damage.

**Reserves.** Money already set aside. Cheap, fast, and rarely sufficient — few finance ministries hold
disaster reserves at the scale of a major event.

**Reallocation.** Money moved from other budget lines. Fast, and it transfers the cost to whatever was
defunded — often maintenance, which raises future risk.

**Borrowing.** Domestic or international. Slow to arrange, and it converts a one-off loss into a
recurring debt service burden. The debt is repaid over decades, which is to say by a generation that
did not experience the disaster.

**Insurance and risk transfer.** Pre-paid, fast if parametric, and the premium is a recurring cost that
must be justified every year against a hazard that has not happened.

The order matters because the last resort is the most expensive. A country that borrows after every
event accumulates debt that crowds out adaptation spending, which raises the next event's cost, which
requires more borrowing. The loop is real and it is visible in the fiscal histories of small island
states.

The instrument that breaks it is pre-arranged contingent finance: a credit line, a parametric policy, or
a regional risk pool, agreed before the event and disbursed on a trigger. The cost is a premium, and the
benefit is that the government does not have to choose between rebuilding and borrowing at a distressed
rate.

## Why catastrophe bonds are both clever and limited

A catastrophe bond pays investors a coupon, and if a defined event occurs the principal is used to pay
the issuer. The trigger is parametric — a magnitude, a wind speed, a rainfall total — and the payment
is triggered by the measurement rather than by an assessment of loss.

The design solves the timing problem elegantly. Payment can be made within days, because there is
nothing to assess. For a government that needs cash immediately, that is worth a great deal.

The limitation is basis risk: the trigger may fire for an event that did not cause the issuer's loss, or
fail to fire for one that did. A parametric policy triggered by wind speed at a weather station does not
know whether the storm destroyed the port or missed it.

The practical resolution is a layered structure: parametric instruments for the fast, large, first-response
layer, and indemnity instruments for the slower, assessed layer. Most mature sovereign risk programmes
use both.

## Loss and damage: the accounting of the uninsured

At COP27 in 2022, parties agreed to establish a fund for loss and damage — the costs that cannot be adapted to or insured against (@unfccc2022). The agreement was a genuine breakthrough and an incomplete one: the fund's capitalisation, governance, and disbursement rules have been slow to settle.

The category is analytically useful regardless of the politics, because it names three kinds of loss that existing instruments miss.

**Economic loss** — damaged assets that are not insured and cannot be rebuilt from savings. This is the most straightforward.

**Non-economic loss** — loss of culture, territory, ecosystem, and identity. A community that loses a burial ground has lost something no insurance policy covers, and no reconstruction budget line records.

**Slow-onset loss** — sea-level rise, salinisation, desertification. These are not events, so they trigger no emergency appeal, and by the time they are acknowledged the loss is irreversible.

The justice argument is that the countries bearing these losses contributed least to the emissions causing them. That argument is developed in Chapter 13. The accounting argument is narrower and sufficient for this chapter: existing financial instruments are structurally unable to cover a large share of climate harm, and that gap does not close by itself.

## Who counts as worth saving

Insurance and aid both answer a prior question: whose loss is legible?

A formal house with a title deed and a mortgage is legible to an insurer. A house built on customary land without title, in a settlement that appears on no map, is not. The household may be equally exposed and less able to recover, but the instruments that transfer risk cannot see it.

The same bias operates in government compensation. Post-disaster payments usually require proof of ownership, a bank account, and an address. Households without those documents — often the poorest, and often renters — are excluded from the compensation for a loss they fully experienced.

This is not corruption or malice. It is an administrative design that assumes a particular kind of household. The fix is specific: presumptive registration before the event, community-based verification, and payment mechanisms that do not require formal title.

## The counter-case: when insurance worked

It would be wrong to present insurance as useless. In several settings, well-designed schemes have transferred risk effectively.

**Mexico's catastrophe bonds** pay out automatically when a parametric trigger is met — an earthquake above a defined magnitude, a hurricane above a defined wind speed — without an assessment process. The money arrives within days [@ccrif2023].

**The Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility** operates on the same principle across member states, and has paid out after hurricanes and earthquakes with a speed that conventional aid cannot match (@ccrif2023).

**Index-based crop insurance** in India and Kenya pays when rainfall falls below a threshold, avoiding the need to verify individual losses. The design has problems — basis risk, when the index does not match a farmer's actual loss — but it reaches smallholders who would otherwise have no cover.

The common feature is parametric design: pay on a measured trigger rather than an assessed loss. The trade-off is accuracy for speed, and in disaster finance speed is often worth more.

::: {.box title="Box 8.2 — Why the loss figures are not comparable across countries" kind="note"}
A US$1 billion loss in the Netherlands and a US$1 billion loss in Bangladesh are not the same event.

Three adjustments are needed before any comparison.

**Insured share.** Global loss figures are dominated by insured losses, which are concentrated in rich countries. The uninsured share in low-income countries can exceed 90 per cent.

**Asset value.** A country with more valuable assets records larger losses for the same physical event. Normalising by GDP or by exposed asset value changes the ranking.

**Lives and livelihoods.** A loss figure counts what has a price. It does not count the school year lost, the land sold, or the migration that follows.

The UNDRR global figures are useful for trend analysis. They are not a measure of suffering.
:::

## What a finance ministry should do

Four measures, in order of return.

**Buy contingent finance before the event.** A pre-arranged credit line or parametric policy is cheaper
than post-event borrowing, because the premium is paid in normal times and the interest rate is not.

**Protect maintenance in the budget.** The line item most often cut in a fiscal squeeze is the one whose
absence produces the next disaster. Ring-fencing it is unglamorous and effective.

**Make compensation conditional on risk reduction.** Payments after an event should support moving or
rebuilding to a standard, not rebuilding in place. The condition is the policy.

**Report uninsured losses honestly.** A government that reports only insured losses is telling its
citizens that the losses of the poor did not happen. The reporting standard is a political choice.

## Türkiye and Syria, 2023: when the finance arrives after the search ends

The February 2023 earthquakes in southern Türkiye and northern Syria killed more than 50,000 people and
destroyed or damaged hundreds of thousands of buildings. The financial response illustrates the timing
problem in its starkest form.

Within days, international pledges were announced. Within weeks, assessment teams were deployed. Within
months, the reconstruction financing was being negotiated. The households in the affected area,
meanwhile, had already made the decisions that determined their recovery: which relatives to move in
with, whether to sell livestock, whether to send children to another city for school.

The delay is not a failure of goodwill. It is a structural feature of a system in which money is
committed after an assessment, and assessment requires access, which in a disaster zone is itself
disrupted.

Three design changes would shorten the delay.

**Pre-positioned finance.** Funds released on a trigger — the earthquake magnitude, the number of
buildings collapsed, the declaration of a state of emergency — without an assessment round.

**Pre-registered households.** A register of residents, with bank details, held by the municipality and
usable after the event.

**Pre-agreed procurement.** Contracts for shelter, water, and medical supplies negotiated before the
event, so that the response is a delivery rather than a tender.

None of these is technically difficult. All of them require a government to spend money in advance of a
disaster it cannot know will happen.

## The debt swap that worked

An instrument that deserves a paragraph because it is one of the few that addresses the fiscal trap
described in this chapter: the debt-for-nature or debt-for-climate swap.

A creditor forgives a portion of a country's debt in exchange for the country committing to invest in
conservation or adaptation. The country gains fiscal space, the creditor gains a claim to have supported
the environment, and the environment gains a funding stream.

The record is mixed and worth stating honestly.

**The gains are real** where the swap is large relative to the debt and the commitment is credible.

**The transaction costs are high**, and the negotiation can take years.

**The additionality is contested.** A country may have been going to invest anyway, in which case the swap
has financed a commitment that existed.

**The conditionality can be intrusive**, and it substitutes a creditor's priorities for a country's.

The instrument is not a solution. It is a mechanism that converts an unpayable debt into a funded
commitment, and it is worth using where the alternative is default.

## The disaster that becomes a debt crisis

For a small island state, a single major event can be a macroeconomic event, and the mechanism is worth
following because it explains why loss and damage is a fiscal argument and not only a moral one.

**The event destroys capital.** A cyclone removes housing, roads, and the tourism infrastructure that
provides foreign exchange.

**The government borrows to rebuild.** It has no reserve at the scale required, and insurance covers a
fraction of the loss.

**The debt service crowds out everything else.** The budget that would have funded schools, health, and
adaptation is committed to interest payments.

**The next event arrives before the debt is repaid.** The country borrows again, at a worse rate, because
its creditworthiness has fallen.

This is the sequence that has produced a debt crisis in several Caribbean states after successive
hurricanes, and it is the reason that the loss and damage debate is about grants rather than loans. A loan
that must be repaid after a disaster transfers the cost to a generation that experienced nothing.

The instruments that break the sequence are concessional finance, debt pauses triggered by a disaster, and
grants — each of which is politically difficult for the lender and transformative for the borrower.

## The insurance retreat, in one country

Australia is the clearest case of an insurance market adjusting to climate risk in public, and it is
worth following because the sequence is likely to be repeated elsewhere.

The pattern since the early 2020s has been a gradual increase in premiums in exposed areas, and a
withdrawal from the most exposed. Insurers have publicly stated that the pricing reflects updated
catastrophe models, and that some properties are becoming uninsurable at any commercially viable
premium.

Three consequences follow.

**House prices adjust, slowly.** A house that cannot be insured is a house that is difficult to sell to
a mortgage holder, because lenders require insurance. The market price of the risk eventually appears,
and it appears abruptly when it appears.

**The government becomes the insurer of last resort.** A publicly funded scheme may be created to cover
what the market will not. The scheme is necessary and it holds the exposure in place.

**The distributional effect is regressive.** Wealthier households can self-insure; poorer households
cannot, and are the ones most likely to live in the exposed areas.

The sequence is not unique to Australia. It is the mechanism by which climate risk becomes a household
financial problem rather than a future projection.

## The ten largest losses, and what they have in common

The list of the most costly insured disasters is dominated by a small number of events in a small number of
countries, and the pattern is instructive.

Three features recur.

**Concentration in rich countries.** The events are in the United States, Japan, and Europe, because that is
where the insured assets are. A cyclone in Bangladesh that kills a thousand people can cause less insured
loss than a hailstorm in Texas that kills nobody.

**The dominance of secondary perils.** Floods, hail, and wildfire increasingly dominate the loss tables,
and these are the perils that are hardest to model and least well covered.

**The growth of the tail.** The largest events are becoming larger, which is consistent with both
increasing exposure and intensifying extremes, and the two are difficult to separate.

The implication for this chapter is that the global loss statistics are a record of what happened to
insured assets, not a record of what happened to people. A reader who uses them as a measure of disaster
severity will systematically miss the places where the deaths are.

## The fund that was promised

A note on the loss and damage fund, because it is the most concrete test of the international system and its
progress is checkable.

The fund was agreed at COP27 in 2022 [@unfccc2022]. Its operationalisation required decisions on
governance, on eligibility, on the sources of finance, and on the share that would be grants rather than
loans. Each of those decisions was contested, and the contest consumed the years that followed.

The pattern is familiar from other funds. The Adaptation Fund took years to capitalise. The Green Climate
Fund took years to disburse. The gap between the agreement and the money is measured in years, and the
people waiting are measured in lives.

The lesson for a reader is not cynicism. It is that the establishment of a fund is the beginning of a
process, not the end, and that the process is where the substance is decided.

## The insurance that reaches the poorest

A design that is being tested in several countries and deserves attention: the micro-insurance or social
protection linkage.

A social protection programme — a cash transfer, a public works scheme — already holds a register of poor
households, a payment mechanism, and a delivery system. Adding a climate trigger to it converts a social
programme into an anticipatory disaster response.

The advantage is that the architecture exists. The disadvantage is that the register may be incomplete, the
payment mechanism may be slow, and the trigger may not match the hazard the household faces.

The evidence from pilot programmes is encouraging and limited: the transfers arrive faster than through a
humanitarian appeal, and the coverage is broader than an insurance scheme that requires a premium. The
limitation is that the trigger design determines whether the transfer arrives before the coping strategies
are exhausted.

## The reserve that is not in the budget

A fiscal observation that matters for disaster response and is rarely discussed: most governments do not
hold a disaster reserve at the scale of a major event.

The reason is opportunity cost. A reserve that is not spent is a reserve that could have funded a school.
Finance ministries, under pressure, prefer to borrow after the event, which is more expensive and slower.

Three mechanisms have been used to solve the problem.

**A contingency line** in the budget, ring-fenced and audited, which can be released without a new
appropriation.

**A contingent credit line** from a development bank, which is drawn only if the event occurs and carries a
commitment fee.

**A sovereign insurance policy**, which pays out on a trigger and costs a premium.

Each has a cost and each is cheaper than borrowing after a disaster. The choice among them is a technical
question that most governments have not examined.

## What the loss and damage debate is really about

A framing, because the debate is often described as a dispute about money and is more accurately a
dispute about obligation.

If climate harm is a liability, then the payments are compensation, they are owed, and the amount is
determined by the damage. If it is solidarity, then the payments are assistance, they are voluntary, and the
amount is determined by the political process.

The fund agreed at COP27 was structured in the second register and argued in the first [@unfccc2022], which
is why it exists and is undercapitalised.

The distinction matters for the reader because it predicts the fund's behaviour. A solidarity fund disburses
when the donors are willing; a liability fund disburses when the damage is established. The current
arrangement behaves like the first and is judged against the second.

## The figure that is a floor

A note on every loss figure in this book.

Every damage figure is a floor, for the reasons Chapter 2 set out: it counts assets that have a price, it
excludes the uninsured, and it excludes the losses that are not transactions.

The practical consequence is that a statement like "the flood caused US$30 billion in damage" should be
read as "the flood caused at least US$30 billion in damage, measured in the ways we measure".

A reader who treats the figure as the total is systematically underestimating the harm, and the
underestimation falls on the people whose losses are not counted.

## What would prove this chapter wrong


This chapter argues that the financial architecture of disaster response favours the legible and the insured, arrives after the decisions that determine recovery, and leaves a large category of loss uncovered.

The falsifier is a distributional test with a benchmark. Take post-disaster transfers by household and compare them with losses by household. If transfers were proportional to loss — regardless of title, income, documentation, or insurance — the chapter is wrong. The measurable version: regress transfer receipts on assessed loss and on tenure status. The chapter predicts a positive coefficient on tenure that survives controls for loss, and a negative coefficient on informality.

The finding is consistent across countries and event types, which is what makes the claim strong and also what would make a clean counter-example decisive [@undrr2022]. A country with a functioning presumptive-registration system that paid informal dwellers in proportion to loss would be that counter-example, and the chapter should name it if one exists.

The timing claim is falsified by dates: if the median household transfer arrived before the median household recovery decision — the decision to rebuild, to move, or to borrow — the claim fails. That is measurable from payment records and survey dates, and it is rarely measured, which is itself part of the argument.
