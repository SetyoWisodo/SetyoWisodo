# Introduction: The Sentence Under Test

In the years after a disaster, a sentence tends to be repeated: *this was not a natural disaster, it was a social one.* It is said about hurricanes, heatwaves, floods, and fires. It is said by researchers, by aid workers, and increasingly by the people who survived.

The sentence is a correction, and it is a good one. A storm is natural. The number of people who die in it is not. The difference between the wind speed and the death toll is made of decisions: where the houses were built, who was warned, who could leave, who was counted, and who was forgotten.

But the correction is also imprecise, and imprecision is expensive. If a disaster is social, then presumably every death is a social failure, and presumably every social failure has a policy that could have prevented it. Neither is quite true. Some hazards are so extreme that no arrangement of social decisions would have saved the people in their path. Some social failures are so deeply embedded — in land tenure, in labour markets, in the structure of the state — that no government in the relevant window could have undone them.

A book that claims everything is social is as unusable as a book that claims nothing is. The useful project is narrower and harder: to say, case by case, **which part** of a death toll is made by society and which part is made by physics, and to be honest about the boundary.

That is what this book attempts.

## What the book does

The structure is deliberately simple, and it follows from a single distinction.

Chapter 1 separates three things that are usually merged: **hazard** (the physical event), **exposure** (who and what is in its path), and **vulnerability** (how easily those things break). The relationship between them is a frame, not an equation, and the frame does most of the work.

Part I establishes the framework and the failure mode that makes it necessary. Part II examines who dies and why the distribution is not random. Part III examines what makes a place able to absorb a shock. Part IV addresses the decisions that cannot be avoided. Part V turns to action — for households, for cities, and for the reader who has to judge a claim.

Each chapter follows the same architecture, which is unusual for a trade book and standard for a reference work:

- an opening case, sourced and specific;
- the mechanism that explains why the case generalises;
- the evidence, including where it disagrees;
- a box on **how the numbers are made**, because the method is part of the claim;
- a counter-case, because an argument that has not survived a contrary example has not been tested;
- a section on **what would prove the chapter wrong**;
- annotated further reading.

## What the book does not do

It does not argue that climate change is not happening. The physical record is not in dispute here, and where the book uses physical figures it names a source.

It does not argue that individual behaviour is the solution. Household action matters, and Chapter 15 is specific about what works, but the largest determinants of risk are public decisions.

It does not propose a policy programme. It describes what the evidence supports and where the evidence is thin, and it leaves the political conclusions to the reader, who is better placed to make them.

It does not pretend to completeness. The literature on each of these topics is enormous, and the book cites what it uses rather than surveying the field.

## How the evidence was handled

Three practices are worth stating at the outset, because they shape what follows.

**Every figure is registered.** The book's figures were recorded in a claim ledger with a source and a confidence status before they were written into the text. Where verification changed a number, the number in the text is the verified one. Where verification failed, the claim is either hedged explicitly in the text or omitted. Two figures were corrected during the writing of this edition: the length of Indonesia's coastline, and the size of the rice price increase after India's 2023 export restriction.

**Ranges are not collapsed.** Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated rather than averaged. A reader who needs a single number for a decision should be able to see the range it came from.

**Attribution is named.** "The study found" rather than "scientists say". Where a figure comes from a government, it is attributed to the government. Where it comes from an advocacy organisation, that is stated, even when the method is sound.

## Who the book is for

It is written for three kinds of reader, and the structure is designed to let each use it differently.

**The researcher or student** who needs a defensible account of the social dimensions of climate risk, with citations that can be followed. The chapters can be read independently, and the reference list gives the primary sources.

**The practitioner** — in a city government, an NGO, a health service, or a utility — who needs to know what the evidence says about a specific intervention. The chapter architecture and the boxes are designed for this: the method box tells you what the number means, and the counter-case tells you where the intervention has failed.

**The general reader** who wants to understand why the same storm kills different numbers of people in different places. The chapters are written to be read in order, and no prior knowledge is assumed beyond what a newspaper reader would have.

## A note on place

The book is written by an Indonesian author, and it uses Indonesia as a case throughout — but as a reader of the framework rather than as an illustration of it. Chapter 3 explains why: the archipelago stresses the framework at exactly the points where it is weakest, particularly where the hazard itself is partly manufactured by land-use and water policy.

The risk of writing from one place is parochialism. The book tries to avoid that by testing each claim against cases from other regions, and by naming the places where the pattern does not hold. The reader should judge whether it succeeds.

## What the reader should expect to be convinced by

Not the book's authority. The book's arguments are checkable, and the final chapter is a set of tests for checking them. A reader who finishes this book should be able to take a climate claim — from a government, an activist, a company, or this book — and ask the questions that determine whether it is worth acting on.

That is the standard the book sets for itself, and it is the standard it asks the reader to apply.
