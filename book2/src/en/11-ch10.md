# Social Capital: The Thing That Cannot Be Bought

::: lead
Puerto Rico, September 2017. The official death toll was 64. A study published eleven months later estimated 4,645. The difference between those two numbers is not arithmetic. It is a measurement of what happens when the systems that count people stop working, and neighbours take over.
:::

## The two numbers

Hurricane María crossed Puerto Rico on 20 September 2017 as a Category 4 storm. It destroyed the electricity grid, knocked out communications, and cut road access to much of the interior.

The official government count, revised over months, settled at 64 deaths (@puertorico2018). A study by researchers at Harvard and Penn State, using excess mortality from birth and death records, estimated 4,645 (@kishore2018). Later revisions of the official figure moved toward the higher number.

The gap is not a dispute about hurricane severity. It is a dispute about what counts as a death caused by a disaster. The official count recorded direct deaths: people killed by wind, water, or debris. The excess mortality estimate captured everyone who died because the health system stopped functioning: dialysis patients whose clinics had no power, people who could not reach a hospital, elderly people in homes without water for weeks.

Both numbers are defensible as answers to different questions. The official figure answers: how many people did the storm kill directly? The excess figure answers: how many more people died than would have died otherwise?

The second question is the one that matters for policy, and it is the one that cannot be answered without knowing how a community organises itself when the state is absent.

## What social capital actually is

The term is used loosely, so it is worth being precise. Social capital refers to the resources available to a person through their relationships: the neighbour who has a generator, the cousin who can lend a car, the church group that knows who is missing, the network that transmits information faster than official channels (@putnam1993).

Three kinds are distinguished in the literature, and they do different work in a disaster (@aldrich2012).

**Bonding capital** [@putnam1993] — ties within a homogeneous group: family, close friends, neighbours of the same background. It is the fastest to mobilise and the most reliable in the first hours.

**Bridging capital** — ties across groups: the association that includes people from different neighbourhoods, the sports club, the mosque or church with a mixed congregation. It is what allows resources to move from where they are to where they are needed.

**Linking capital** — ties to institutions and people with power: the local official who answers the phone, the NGO with a warehouse. It determines whether a community's needs reach the decision-making table.

The empirical finding that matters most is Aldrich's: in a study of disasters across several countries, social capital predicted recovery outcomes better than income, damage severity, or government aid (@aldrich2012). Communities with denser networks recovered faster even when they had less money.

## Smong: the knowledge that was stored in a story

Simeulue, an island off the coast of Aceh, offers the most striking case in the literature.

On 26 December 2004, the Indian Ocean tsunami killed approximately 300,000 people in Aceh. On Simeulue, 7 people died out of a population of 78,128 (@mcadoo2006).

The difference was not distance from the epicentre — Simeulue was close. It was not infrastructure or warning technology. It was a story.

The island's oral tradition included the *smong*: a remembered account of the 1907 tsunami, transmitted through lullabies, poems, and stories, which encoded the signs — the sea withdrawing, the ground shaking — and the response: run to high ground immediately. When the 2004 earthquake struck, people recognised the signs and ran. The evacuation was not organised by any agency. It was transmitted through families (@mcadoo2006).

This is social capital in its purest form: a resource held collectively, transmitted without institutions, and activated without a chain of command. It is also fragile, because it depends on transmission, and transmission can be interrupted by migration, schooling, or the disruption of the community that holds it.

## Japan, 2011: the neighbourhood that organised itself

The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami provide the clearest large-scale evidence of social capital operating before any agency arrived [@aldrich2012].

The tsunami killed more than 15,000 people and destroyed entire towns. The survival patterns were not
random. In communities with strong neighbourhood associations (*chōnaikai*), evacuation was faster and
more complete. In communities where the association had weakened — because of ageing, because of
housing redevelopment, because the young had moved away — the evacuation was slower.

The mechanism was specific and observable. The *chōnaikai* had a roster of who lived where, including
older residents living alone. It had a routine of drills. It had people with cars who knew who needed
a lift. When the earthquake struck, these structures activated within minutes, before any official
instruction.

Two details are worth recording.

**The drills mattered more than the warnings.** The meteorological agency issued a tsunami warning
within minutes, but the warning reached people who were already moving, because the drills had
established what to do. The warning confirmed the plan; it did not create it.

**The associations had been declining.** Japan has been losing *chōnaikai* membership for decades, as
urbanisation and ageing reduce participation. The towns that suffered most in 2011 were, in several
cases, the ones where the association had already thinned.

This is the chapter's core argument in a single case: a resource that is invisible in normal times
determined survival in the worst hour, and the resource was declining because of social changes that
no one connected to disaster risk.

## The dark side of bonding capital

It would be dishonest to present social capital as unambiguously good. The same dense networks that
save lives can exclude, punish, and entrench inequality.

**Exclusion.** A community with strong internal ties has a boundary. Newcomers, migrants, renters, and
minorities may be outside it, and are therefore least likely to be rescued and most likely to be blamed.

**Enforcement of norms.** Dense networks enforce conformity. In disasters, this can mean that people
who do not evacuate in the expected way are stigmatised, or that women who use shelters are judged.

**Capture.** A community organisation with strong ties can be captured by a local elite, and the
resources it controls can be distributed along existing hierarchies. The disaster literature is full of
cases in which aid was captured by whoever controlled the local committee.

**The cost is borne by women.** The labour of maintaining social capital — care, feeding, organising —
falls disproportionately on women, and it is unpaid. A policy that relies on community self-help is
therefore a policy that extracts unpaid labour from the people with the least power.

The correct conclusion is not to distrust social capital, but to ask who is inside the network and who
is paying for it.

## Gotong royong and its limits

In Indonesia, the tradition of *gotong royong* — mutual assistance, literally "carrying together" — is often cited as a form of social capital that reduces disaster impact.

The evidence is mixed and worth stating carefully. Community self-help does mobilise quickly after events, particularly in rural areas, and it can substitute for absent state capacity in the first days. But it also has limits that are rarely acknowledged.

**It is not a substitute for public services.** A community can clear debris. It cannot rebuild a hospital, treat contaminated water, or replace a road.

**It can be exclusionary.** Mutual assistance operates within a group, and the group has boundaries. Newcomers, migrants, renters, and minorities may be outside the network, which means the people most in need of help may be the least connected to it.

**It can be extracted.** When governments rely on community self-help to fill gaps left by budget cuts, the cost is transferred to the people with the least time and the fewest resources — usually women, who carry most of the care work.

The correct conclusion is not that social capital is unimportant. It is that social capital is a complement to public capacity, not a replacement for it, and that treating it as a substitute is a way of making inequality invisible.

## What destroys social capital

Social capital is easier to destroy than to build, and the destruction is often a side effect of well-intentioned policy.

**Displacement** breaks networks. A community relocated as a unit preserves its ties; a community dispersed into scattered housing does not. This is one of the strongest arguments for in-place recovery where it is safe, and for keeping relocated communities together where it is not.

**Housing policy** can break networks. A redevelopment that moves residents out for two years and returns them to different units has destroyed the neighbour relations that made the place function.

**Disaster response** can break networks. A response that treats affected people as passive recipients, and that ignores existing community organisations, bypasses the very structures that are doing the work.

**Migration** breaks networks by design. The people who leave are often the most capable, which is the mechanism behind "brain drain" and also behind the weakening of the community that remains.

## The 2018 Sulawesi earthquake: when the network was the response

The earthquake and tsunami that struck Palu, Central Sulawesi, in September 2018 killed more than 2,000
people and destroyed entire neighbourhoods. The official response was slow to reach many areas, and for
the first days the response was largely self-organised.

Three features of that self-organisation are documented in the post-event literature.

**Religious networks mobilised first.** Mosques and churches became distribution points, information
hubs, and shelters, because they had buildings, trusted leaders, and members who knew who was missing.

**Radio amateur networks carried information.** With communications down, amateur radio operators
provided the only link between some districts and the outside.

**The diaspora mobilised through social media.** Relatives abroad were often the first to know which
neighbourhoods had been destroyed, because they were in contact with people on the ground.

The pattern is consistent with the chapter's argument, and it adds a dimension: in a large, sudden
event, the response is a network before it is an organisation, and the quality of the network determines the first 72 hours [@aldrich2012].

The policy implication is that preparedness should identify and support the nodes that will activate —
the mosques, the churches, the radio clubs, the neighbourhood associations — rather than assuming that
the response will be delivered by agencies.

## The association that outlives the disaster

One finding from the recovery literature is worth carrying into policy: the organisations that matter in
recovery are usually the ones that existed before the event.

A sports club, a choir, a credit union, a religious congregation — these have the properties that a
disaster response needs: a membership list, a reason to contact each other, a small treasury, and a
trusted leader. They can organise a distribution, check on members, and negotiate with an agency.

The implication for preparedness is that the cheapest investment is often not a new organisation but the
maintenance of existing ones. A grant to a sports club is not a disaster programme; it is the substrate
on which a disaster response can be built.

The corollary is that the decline of associational life — which is documented in many rich countries over
several decades — is a slow reduction in adaptive capacity that no disaster budget records.

## Measuring what resists measurement

Social capital is hard to measure, which is one reason it is underweighted in policy. The available instruments are imperfect but usable.

Survey-based measures of trust and association membership; counts of community organisations per capita; network analysis of who calls whom after an event; and the density of local media, which is a proxy for shared information.

None of these is as clean as a damage estimate. All of them predict recovery better than damage estimates do (@aldrich2012).

The policy implication is that investment in the social fabric — community centres, local organisations, festivals, sports clubs, and the mundane infrastructure of association — is adaptation, even though it appears in no adaptation budget.

## The informal network that the state did not see

One of the recurring findings in disaster research is that the organisations that matter most in the
first hours are often invisible to the agencies that plan the response.

In many cities, the effective first responders are not the fire service but the mosque committee, the
market association, the taxi drivers' union, and the neighbourhood watch. They have members, vehicles,
buildings, and knowledge of who lives where. They are not in the emergency plan.

The consequence is that the response runs on two parallel systems: the formal one, which is coordinated
and slow, and the informal one, which is uncoordinated and fast. The informal system does most of the
early work and receives none of the support.

The policy implication is not to formalise the informal networks — that would destroy what makes them
fast — but to include them in planning, give them information, and provide the resources they lack.
A mosque committee with a list of vulnerable residents and a stock of water is a better first responder
than an agency that arrives on day three.

## The counter-case: when social capital was not enough

It would be dishonest to end this chapter without the limit case.

Social capital did not save the Lower Ninth Ward in New Orleans in 2005. The neighbourhood had dense networks and strong churches, and it flooded anyway, because the levee failed. After the flood, the networks that remained were dispersed across the country, and the neighbourhood's recovery was slow and partial.

Social capital operates within the space that physical and institutional protection leaves. It cannot compensate for a failed levee, a nonexistent hospital, or a government that does not return.

The correct formulation is: social capital is the most important resource in the first 72 hours and the least sufficient one after that.

## What a city can do about it

Social capital is not a programme, but there are policies that strengthen or weaken it, and they are
usually made outside the disaster office.

**Keep public space.** A square, a market, a library, a park: the physical places where people
encounter each other. Their loss is a loss of the infrastructure of acquaintance.

**Fund local organisations.** Small grants to neighbourhood associations, sports clubs, and religious
bodies are cheap and are the most direct investment in bonding capital.

**Resist dispersed relocation.** When relocation is necessary, move communities together and keep the
institutions — the school, the mosque, the shop — that hold them.

**Support local media.** A local newspaper or radio station is a shared information system, and its
decline reduces the capacity of a community to know itself.

**Count the unpaid labour.** Any policy that relies on community self-help should account for who
provides it, and should not treat it as free.

The general principle is that social capital is produced by ordinary institutions, and destroyed by
ordinary decisions. The disaster office cannot create it, but the city can stop destroying it.

## The network that was formalised and stopped working

A cautionary case, because the chapter's enthusiasm for informal networks needs a counterweight.

In several disasters, the informal networks that performed well immediately were later formalised by the
response system — invited into coordination meetings, given reporting requirements, and asked to work
within the cluster system. The formalisation improved coordination on paper and degraded the networks'
speed and flexibility.

The mechanism is familiar from organisational theory. A network that operates on trust and reciprocity
loses its advantage when it becomes an implementing partner subject to reporting, procurement, and
accountability requirements.

The design implication is not to exclude informal networks but to support them at arm's length: fund them,
share information, and do not absorb them.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that social capital predicts disaster recovery better than material resources, and that policy which destroys networks destroys resilience.

It would be wrong if recovery outcomes tracked aid volumes and damage levels closely, with network variables adding nothing once those are controlled for. Aldrich's cross-national work found the opposite (@aldrich2012).

## What the evidence does not establish

The literature on social capital is strong on correlation and weaker on causation, and the chapter should
say so.

The core finding — that communities with denser networks recover faster — is robust across cases. What is
less established is the direction of causation. Dense networks may produce recovery, or wealthier and
more stable communities may have both denser networks and faster recovery, with the network as a
symptom rather than a cause.

The studies that address this use instrumental variables or natural experiments, and they generally find
a causal effect, but the estimates vary widely and the identification is contestable.

The practical consequence is not that the finding should be discarded. It is that a policy justified by
"social capital predicts recovery" should be modest about what it can claim, and should measure the
network rather than assume that a community centre creates one.

## The ten years after

Social capital is usually measured at the moment of the event. The evidence on what happens over the
following decade is more interesting.

Three trajectories recur.

**Reinforced.** The event produces new organisations, new trust, and new routines. The community is better
prepared for the next event than it was for the last. This is the case in communities that stayed together
and rebuilt in place.

**Eroded.** The event disperses the community, the organisations lose members, and the trust declines. This
is the case in communities that were relocated or that never returned.

**Replaced.** The community is repopulated by newcomers who have no memory of the event and no network.
The physical reconstruction is complete and the social reconstruction is absent.

The determinant is almost always whether the community stayed together. That is why the relocation
literature in Chapter 12 keeps returning to the same recommendation: move communities as units.

## The institution that is not a government

One more category of social capital is worth naming because it is large and almost never counted: the
religious institution.

In most of the world, the building that is open every day, has a trusted leader, a membership list, a
kitchen, and a source of discretionary funds is a place of worship. In a disaster it becomes a shelter, a
distribution point, a morgue, and a counselling service.

The evidence from the cases in this chapter is consistent: in Palu, in Tōhoku, in the Bangladesh cyclones,
and in Puerto Rico, the first organised response was often religious.

The policy question is how to work with an institution that is not a government and does not want to be
one. Three approaches have worked.

**Include it in planning.** Invite the leadership to the table, share the hazard maps, and agree the role
in advance.

**Fund the role.** A small grant for water, fuel, and supplies converts goodwill into capacity.

**Do not formalise it.** An institution that becomes an implementing partner loses the informality that
makes it trusted.

## What a volunteer programme costs

A concrete comparison, because the argument that social capital is cheap needs numbers.

Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme operates with about 76,000 volunteers [@wmo2022]. The volunteers
receive training, a small allowance, and equipment — megaphones, flags, first aid kits. The programme's
annual cost is a small fraction of the value of the assets it protects in a single cyclone.

A comparable investment in engineering — a sea wall, a shelter network — costs orders of magnitude more per
life saved in many settings.

The comparison is not exact, because the programme depends on the volunteers' unpaid time, which is a
transfer from the volunteers to the state. That transfer is real and should be acknowledged, and it does not
change the conclusion that the programme is extraordinarily cost-effective.

## The festival that is a preparedness measure

One of the more surprising findings in the social capital literature: cultural events that bring a community
together have a measurable effect on disaster response.

The mechanism is not mystical. A festival produces a membership list, a group of people who have worked
together, a communication network, and a set of norms about mutual obligation. These are the properties a
response needs.

The implication for policy is that funding cultural and sporting events is not a distraction from
preparedness. It is, indirectly, an investment in the network that will be activated when the event comes.

The implication for a community is that the organisation that runs the festival is a disaster asset, and
should be treated as one.
