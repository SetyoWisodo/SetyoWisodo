# A Note on Sources, Figures, and the Limits of Knowledge {.unnumbered .unlisted}

This book uses numbered citations. The small number after a claim points to an entry in the References; the numbers follow the order of first appearance, not the alphabet. The system was chosen for two reasons: the reader can check a claim without leaving the page, and every entry in the References is actually used — there are no dangling entries included to suggest breadth.

## Three grades of claim

Not every claim in this book has the same force, and the reader is entitled to know the difference.

**Observational claims** — figures that are measured, recorded, and published by an institution. Examples: the global mean temperature in 2024 of 1.55 °C (WMO), the atmospheric CO₂ concentration of 422.8 ppm (NOAA). Claims of this kind can be rechecked in minutes, and they will change when new data are published.

**Estimative claims** — figures produced by a model or a statistical procedure, not by direct counting. Example: the 61,672 heat-related deaths in Europe in 2022 are excess deaths calculated from seasonal patterns, not a count of bodies recorded. Claims of this kind are true as estimates, and false if quoted as exact counts.

**Framing claims** — statements about relationships among variables, not about magnitudes. Example: "vulnerability determines how far a society falls within the bounds of the hazard." Claims of this kind cannot be proved or refuted by a single number; they are tested by their ability to explain patterns across cases.

Every chapter closes with a section naming what would refute its framing claim. That is not rhetoric; it is how a claim that cannot be tested with a single number remains checkable.

## Figures deliberately left un-averaged

Several figures in this book are still moving, and the differences are stated rather than averaged away.

**Heat deaths in Europe.** The figure of roughly 70,000 for 2003 comes from Robine et al. (2008). The figure of 61,672 for 2022 comes from Ballester et al. (2023). Both are excess-mortality estimates, and the methods are not identical. Chapters 4 and 13 compare them with that caveat.

**Deaths from Hurricane Maria.** The initial official figure of 64 deaths is compared with an excess-mortality estimate of 4,645 (range 793–8,498) from Kishore et al. (2018). The difference is not a counting error but a definitional one: direct deaths during the storm versus excess deaths over the months that followed.

**Deaths from Cyclone Bhola.** Published estimates range from 300,000 to 500,000. This book uses 300,000 and states the range whenever the figure is used for comparison.

**Sea-level rise.** The figure of 4.75 mm per year is the global average from satellite altimetry. In some locations local subsidence is far larger than this — and it is subsidence, not the global figure, that determines tidal flooding.

## What is not in this book

**No single projection for 2100.** The range across scenarios is too wide to be useful without explanation. Whenever a chapter needs a future figure, it is given as a range with its conditions attached.

**No single attribution without an attribution study.** If an event is described as a consequence of climate change, the basis of the claim is an attribution study, not a correlation with the temperature trend.

**No reconstructed expert quotations.** All direct quotations come from documents that can be checked. If an expert statement cannot be traced to a primary source, it is not used — however convincing it sounds.

## Two limits that should be stated

**First limit: uncertainty that cannot be removed.** Some figures in this book will change after publication. Not because they are wrong, but because the data are updated. A reader who finds a different figure in a newer report should treat it as an update rather than a contradiction — unless the difference changes the direction of the conclusion, which is rare.

**Second limit: uneven geographical coverage.** The available literature is densest for Europe and North America, because that is where most studies have been done and most data have been published. Chapter 3 and several other sections try to correct this imbalance with Indonesian and South Asian cases, but the imbalance does not disappear. The book should be read with the awareness that "the available evidence" is not the same as "the world".

## How to check this book

For any numbered claim, the reader can take three steps:

1. Find the number in the References, then open the source.
2. Check whether the source actually states the figure claimed — not a similar figure.
3. Check the publication date. For climate data, a gap of two years can mean a meaningful difference.

If any of the three steps fails, the claim is wrong, and the correction will be accepted.
