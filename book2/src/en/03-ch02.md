# Crisis Does Not Arrive as a Single Blow

::: lead
Every disaster report has a moment: the wall of water, the gust, the fire. Almost no disaster is one moment. It is a sequence in which the first failure removes the protection that would have caught the second.
:::


## The question the single-cause story cannot answer

When a flood kills people, the natural question is how high the water rose. The more useful question is what would have stopped the people from being in it.

In Pakistan in 2022, the Indus rose for eight weeks. Watching the satellite record alone, the disaster looks hydrological. But the toll was shaped by decisions taken before the flood: whether drainage had been maintained, whether the provincial coordination channel worked, whether the early warning reached a village with one radio. The flood was the trigger. The chain was the cause (@pakistan2022).

Framing disaster as a chain rather than an event is not rhetorical. It changes what you count as evidence, what you count as prevention, and where you look for failure. A single-cause frame produces single-cause solutions — higher embankments, larger pumps — and these frequently transfer the risk to someone else.

## The four kinds of link

The chain has links of four kinds. Each fails in a different way and each needs a different repair.

**Physical links** [@undrr2022]. A hillside stripped of trees sheds water instead of absorbing it. A floodplain built over loses the capacity to store a flood. A wetland drained for agriculture no longer buffers a storm surge; the peat underneath then oxidises and subsides, and the land ends up below the river it used to hold.

**Infrastructure links.** Systems where one component feeds everything downstream. A grid that depends on one fuel chain. A water supply that depends on one pump. A hospital that depends on one generator. In Texas in February 2021, the failure of gas generation propagated into the water supply, because water pumps need electricity. Households that got their power back still had no water (@ferc2021).

**Information links.** A warning that is accurate but does not arrive, or arrives but is not understood, or is understood but not believed, or is believed but cannot be acted on. In the Ahr valley in Germany in July 2021, the forecast was correct. The message arrived as weather information rather than as an instruction to evacuate, and the valley had minutes (@kreienkamp2021).

**Institutional links.** Aid that arrives after the critical window. Coordination broken by an administrative boundary. Emergency funds that need a signature from someone not present. This is the least discussed link and the most frequent source of losses that were avoidable (@undrr2022).

::: {.box title="Box 2.1 — The Texas chain, link by link" kind="note"}
In February 2021, a polar vortex brought temperatures below −10 °C to Texas for several days.

**Link 1 — generation.** Gas wells and supply lines were not winterised. Water mixed with gas froze and blocked flow. Gas, coal, and one nuclear unit went offline. The wind turbines that were blamed mostly performed as expected (@ferc2021).

**Link 2 — the grid.** ERCOT, the state's grid operator, is largely isolated from the national grids to avoid federal regulation. Isolation removed the ability to import power from neighbours during the emergency. A design choice made for regulatory reasons became a reliability failure.

**Link 3 — water.** Pumps need power. Losing power forced boil-water notices across 1,100 systems in 190 counties. More than 14 million people were affected (@ferc2021).

**Link 4 — housing.** Poorly insulated homes, a housing stock built for heat, and a population instructed to shelter in place. The people who died were mostly not outside. They died indoors, of cold, in one of the wealthiest countries on earth (@cbs2021).

No single link explains the death toll. Each made the next one worse.
:::

::: {.box title="Box 2.2 — How disaster loss is counted" kind="note"}
Loss figures are assembled from damage assessments, insurance claims, and government reports, and the three are not comparable.

**Direct damage** is the replacement value of destroyed assets. **Losses** are the flow of income and production forgone. The Pakistan 2022 assessment separated them: US$14.9 billion in damage and US$15.2 billion in losses (@pakistan2022).

**Insured vs total.** Global loss databases are dominated by insured losses, which are concentrated in high-income countries. Uninsured losses are estimated separately, often by extrapolation, and the extrapolation is where most of the uncertainty lies.

**What is excluded.** Mortality is counted separately, and non-market losses — ecosystems, heritage, health, schooling — are usually excluded entirely. A loss figure is therefore a floor, and it is a floor built on assets that had a price.
:::
## The chain has a history

Treating disaster as a chain is not a conceptual innovation of the last twenty years; it is a
rediscovery. The American engineer and administrator who ran the response to the 1972 Buffalo Creek
flood, and later the sociologists who studied it, described the event as a sequence in which a coal
company's slurry dam failed into a valley whose emergency services had been organised for a different
kind of flood, in a county where the roads ran along the only escape route [@erikson1976].

What changed since then is the language, not the phenomenon. The modern vocabulary — cascading
failure, dependency chain, systemic risk — comes from engineering and network science, and it has the
advantage of being quantitative. Interdependent networks fail in ways that can be modelled, and the
models show something counter-intuitive: a system of two networks, each robust in isolation, can be
extremely fragile when coupled [@buldyrev2010].

That result matters for policy because it says that assessing the resilience of each sector separately
tells you almost nothing about the resilience of the system. A hospital that passes its own
inspection, a water utility that passes its own, and a grid that passes its own can produce a city
that fails.

## Puerto Rico, 2017: a chain measured in months

The clearest case of a chain whose links failed in sequence, rather than simultaneously, is Puerto
Rico after Hurricane María in September 2017.

**Link 1 — the grid.** The storm destroyed the transmission system. Puerto Rico's grid had been
deferred-maintenance for years, and the island's utility was insolvent before the storm arrived. Power
was not restored to all customers for eleven months.

**Link 2 — communications.** Cellular towers lost power and backup fuel. Without communications, the
distribution of generators, fuel, and water could not be coordinated, and households could not find
out where to go.

**Link 3 — water.** Pumping stations need electricity. Boil-water notices covered most of the island
for weeks, and in some municipalities for months.

**Link 4 — health.** Hospitals ran on generators with limited fuel. Dialysis patients could not be
treated. Insulin and oxygen supplies ran down. The official death count was 64; the excess mortality
estimate was 4,645 [@kishore2018].

**Link 5 — the records.** Death certificates were issued by a system that had itself been disrupted,
which is one reason the official count was so far below the excess estimate. The failure of the
administrative system concealed the failure of the health system.

The sequence is the point. Any single link could have been cut: hardened transmission, resilient
communications, pre-positioned fuel, a registry of electricity-dependent patients. Each intervention
is cheaper than the next link's failure, and none was funded because each belonged to a different
sector's budget.

## Why chains are more dangerous than single failures

Two properties of chains make them worse than the sum of their links.

The first is **dependency**: the failure of one link removes the protection another link would have provided. In Texas, the grid failure removed the water supply's ability to function, and the water failure removed the fire service's ability to fight fires. A system with more links is not necessarily more resilient; it can be more fragile because it has more ways to transmit failure.

The second is **timing**: links fail at different speeds. Physical links may take years to degrade — a drained wetland, a subsiding delta. Information links fail in minutes. Institutional links fail over hours and days, during the response. This mismatch is why disasters look like sudden events when they are actually slow failures that arrive together (@undrr2022).

## Non-linearity and thresholds

Chains do not fail proportionally. This is the least intuitive part and the most important for policy.

A levee does not lose 10 per cent of its protection when the water rises 10 per cent. Overtopping is a threshold: below it, little happens; above it, the failure is total. The same is true for a drainage system, a power transformer's thermal limit, a crop's tolerance for salt, and a human body's ability to shed heat.

Thresholds explain why the same hazard produces wildly different outcomes on different days. In Europe, a 2 °C difference in a heatwave can separate a normal summer from a mass-casualty event, because the population's physiological margin is small at the top of the distribution. In the 2003 heatwave, the excess deaths were concentrated among people whose bodies could no longer compensate (@robine2008).

Thresholds also explain why averages mislead. A mean temperature rise of 2 °C does not mean every day is 2 °C warmer. It means the tail of the distribution moves further, and the tail is where the damage is.

## Slow-onset disasters and the attention problem

Some disasters do not have a moment at all. Drought, salinisation, sea-level rise, and desertification unfold over years, and their human cost accumulates in a way no camera records.

The clearest case is Somalia. The drought of 2010–2012 killed an estimated 258,000 people, more than half of them children under five (@checchi2013). There was no single event to point to. No building collapsed on camera. The disaster was a slow failure of food access, in a country where a militant group restricted humanitarian access, and where the international system was slow to declare famine because the evidence thresholds were met late (@checchi2013).

The comparison with a sudden disaster is instructive. A cyclone that kills 300 people in one night gets global coverage within hours. A drought that kills 258,000 over two years gets a report, and the report is published after the deaths. Attention is a function of suddenness, not of severity — which means the disasters that kill the most are systematically the least visible.

## The losses that never enter the statistics

Post-disaster accounting counts what can be valued: buildings, infrastructure, crops, livestock. In the Pakistan 2022 assessment, direct damage was estimated at US$14.9 billion and losses at US$15.2 billion (@pakistan2022). These are large numbers and still lower bounds.

What is missing is everything without a market price. A child who leaves school for a year to work in a rebuilt household. A family that sells its land to repay a loan taken for reconstruction. A woman who does not return to the labour market because the family's care burden increased. These losses are rarely counted, and they are the mechanism that turns a temporary shock into a permanent decline in welfare (@carleton2016).

The evidence on this is now substantial: a household can lose a fifth of its assets in a flood and never recover, not because of the flood, but because it loses its capacity to generate income. Recovery is not the restoration of the pre-disaster state; it is a new state, usually worse.

Every loss figure in this book should therefore be read as a floor. The US$30.1 billion for Pakistan is the value of assets that could be valued. The value of what could not be valued is larger and is not inside it.

## How to cut a chain: the economics of the cheapest link

If a disaster is a sequence, then an intervention at any link shortens it. The question is which link
to cut, and the answer is almost never the one that gets funded.

Three principles recur in the cases.

**Intervene upstream of the amplification.** A dollar spent keeping water out of a substation is worth
more than a dollar spent restoring power after the substation floods, because the first prevents the
second. The general rule is that the cheapest link is the one whose failure would be most expensive —
which requires knowing the dependency chain before the event.

**Prefer interventions that work under multiple failures.** A hospital with its own water, power, and
communications survives more chains than a hospital with a perfect generator and no water. Redundancy
across sectors beats redundancy within one.

**Price the invisible.** Winterising gas infrastructure in Texas costs a small fraction of the losses
from a single freeze, but the benefit appears as an absence of an event, which no one can photograph.
The asymmetry is structural, and it is why the same recommendations are issued after every freeze and
implemented after none [@ferc2021].

The economics is not subtle. What is difficult is the accounting: the cost sits in one budget, the
benefit in another, and the event that justifies the spending has not happened yet.

## Thresholds, in places you would not expect

Chapter 2 has already argued that chains fail non-linearly. Three examples show how far the threshold
logic extends.

**Currency reserves.** A country with reserves sufficient to cover three months of imports can defend
its exchange rate after a disaster. A country with two months of cover cannot, and the currency falls,
which raises the price of imported food, which produces a second crisis. The threshold is not physical
but financial, and it is crossed abruptly.

**Cold-chain capacity.** A vaccine that must be kept at 2–8 °C survives a power cut of a few hours and
is destroyed by one of a few days. The damage is not proportional to the duration; it is a step.

**Institutional trust.** A population that has seen three warnings that came to nothing will not
evacuate on the fourth. The erosion is cumulative and the effect is a cliff: once trust crosses a
level, the warning system is functionally absent even though every component works.

In each case, the relevant question is not the average condition but the distance to the threshold.
That is a different kind of measurement, and it is rarely reported.

## The case that defined the field: Aberfan

On 21 October 1966, a coal waste tip above the village of Aberfan in Wales collapsed after days of heavy
rain and buried a primary school. One hundred and forty-four people died, 116 of them children.

The disaster is in every textbook for a reason: the chain was documented in full, and it had been
documented before the event.

**The physical link.** The tip had been built on a spring, over sandstone, in a valley. The spoil was
saturated and unstable; the spring fed it from below.

**The institutional link.** The National Coal Board had received warnings. A tribunal after the disaster
found that the tip had been known to be unsafe, that complaints had been made, and that no one with the
authority to act had acted.

**The information link.** The tip had moved before. Local residents had reported it. The reports did not
produce a decision.

**The exposure link.** The school was at the bottom of the valley, directly below the tip, because the
village had grown around the colliery in the only flat land available.

The tribunal's finding is the sentence that the field still quotes: the disaster was not an act of God, and
it could have been prevented by ordinary care.

Aberfan is the case that established, in British public administration, the principle that a known hazard
with a known owner requires action, and that the absence of action is a decision. It is also the case that
shows why the principle is hard to enforce: the warnings existed, the knowledge existed, and the
institutional structure had no mechanism to convert them into a stop order.

## The chain is also a chain of information

One link deserves separate treatment because it is the one that determines whether the others are
diagnosed in time.

Information about a disaster travels through a chain of its own: sensor, agency, ministry, media, public.
Each step has a latency, and the sum of the latencies determines when the response begins.

In the days after a large event, the binding constraint is often not money or supplies but knowledge:
how many people are affected, where they are, and what they need. Assessments take days, and in the
interval the response is guided by guesswork.

Three practices shorten the information chain.

**Pre-agreed data sharing.** Agencies that have agreed in advance to share feeds in an emergency do not
spend the first day negotiating.

**Satellite and remote sensing, pre-arranged.** A charter that activates imagery within hours, with
analysts ready to interpret it.

**Community reporting.** A network of local contacts who can report by phone, which is faster than a
formal assessment and less reliable, and both properties have to be managed.

The pattern is that the information chain is a dependency like any other, and it fails in the same way.

## The counter-case: when the chain held

Not every chain fails. The interesting cases are the ones where an identical hazard produced a different ending — because they show which links are load-bearing.

In Bangladesh, the chain from cyclone to death toll has been progressively rebuilt since 1970: shelters, volunteers, warnings, drills, and — critically — a decision to include women in the volunteer network after evidence showed female deaths outnumbered male deaths by 14 to 1 in Bhola (@sammonds2021). The physical hazard did not decline. The chain was shortened at several points simultaneously.

The lesson is not that Bangladesh solved the problem. Economic losses there rise every year, and displacement continues. The lesson is narrower and more useful: **chains can be cut at more than one point, and the cheapest cut is rarely the one that gets funded.** Strengthening warning systems is cheaper than building embankments (@wmo2022). Calling vulnerable residents is cheaper than building cooling centres. Keeping a list of patients who depend on electricity is cheaper than preparing an emergency hospital.

## Why this matters more as the climate changes

The chain argument is not only a description of past disasters. It has a specific implication for the
next few decades that is easy to miss.

As hazards intensify, the failure that matters is increasingly the one that occurs **outside the design
envelope** of a system. A drainage network designed for the rainfall of 1980 is not merely less
protective in 2040; it fails in a different mode, at a threshold, when the storm exceeds its capacity
rather than degrading gracefully.

Chains therefore become more relevant, not less. The components that fail first are the ones whose
design assumed a stationary climate, and those components are embedded in dependencies that were
themselves designed under the same assumption. A substation built for a 50-year flood plain, feeding a
pump designed for a 30-year drought, serving a hospital that assumed both — the whole assembly has a
single shared assumption, and it is wrong together.

The adaptation implication is that upgrading components one at a time will not be enough. The
dependencies have to be re-examined as a system, which is organisationally difficult because budgets,
departments, and professional standards are organised by component.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that disasters are chains, that the links fail at different speeds, and that interventions at the information and institutional links are usually cheaper than interventions at the physical link.

It would be wrong if, across a large sample of events, the death toll were predicted almost entirely by the magnitude of the physical hazard, with the state of information and institutions contributing nothing once hazard is controlled for. In that world, the rational policy would be to build bigger barriers and stop funding drills.

The evidence points the other way: comparable storms produce death tolls that differ by orders of magnitude, and the differences track the state of the chain rather than the strength of the wind.

## A note on attribution in chained events

One consequence of the chain view deserves its own paragraph, because it explains a persistent
argument in the literature.

If a disaster is a chain, then the question "what caused it?" has no single answer, and the answer
depends on where the analyst starts. Start at the rainfall and climate change caused it. Start at the
drainage and the municipality caused it. Start at the land market and the colonial land registry
caused it. Each is defensible, and each supports a different policy.

Attribution studies handle this by asking a narrow question: how much more likely was the physical
hazard because of climate change? That is answerable, and it is what the Pakistan 2022 analysis did
[@otto2022]. It does not answer the broader question of why the hazard became a catastrophe, and the
studies are usually careful to say so.

The habit worth forming as a reader is to notice which link a claim is about, and to refuse the slide
from "climate change made the rain heavier" to "climate change caused the deaths". The first is a
physical attribution; the second is a chain argument that requires evidence about drainage, tenure,
warning, and response.

## What the chain looks like in a slow event

The chapter has used storms and floods as its cases, and the chain logic applies to slow events with one
difference: the links fail in sequence over years, and no single moment triggers the response.

A drought becomes a famine through a chain: rainfall deficit, soil moisture deficit, harvest failure, price
rise, asset sales, nutritional deficit, mortality. Each link is separated from the next by months, and each
is an opportunity to intervene.

The reason the chain is not cut is that the links that matter fall in different institutional jurisdictions.
Meteorological services monitor rainfall; agricultural ministries monitor harvests; market regulators
monitor prices; social protection agencies monitor nutrition. No one monitors the chain.

A drought early warning system that integrates the links exists in several countries and works. It reports
a single index, computed from rainfall, vegetation, prices, and nutrition, and it triggers a pre-agreed
response. The index is not a prediction; it is a measurement of where the chain has reached.

The general principle is that a chain is cut most cheaply at the link that is furthest upstream and
cheapest to observe. For drought, that is often the price, not the rainfall.
