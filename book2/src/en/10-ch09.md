# Infrastructure as a Chain of Dependencies

::: lead
A power grid is not a machine. It is an agreement that everyone will keep doing their part at the same time. When the agreement fails in one place, it fails everywhere downstream — and downstream is where the hospital is.
:::

## The sectoral mistake


Infrastructure is planned sector by sector: energy, water, transport, health, communications. Each has its own ministry, its own budget, its own standards, and its own definition of resilience.

A disaster does not respect the boundaries. It moves through the dependencies between sectors, which are invisible in the plans.

The Texas 2021 failure is the clearest recent case. Gas wellheads and supply lines were not winterised, because the gas sector's standards were written for a climate that had not yet produced a sustained freeze. When they froze, generation fell. When generation fell, pumps stopped. When pumps stopped, water systems lost pressure. When pressure fell, boil-water notices spread across 190 counties, affecting roughly 14.4 million people (@ferc2021).

Every sector complied with its own code. The system failed because no one was responsible for the dependencies.

### Four properties that determine whether a system holds

Not all infrastructure fails in the same way. Four properties predict behaviour in a shock.

**Redundancy.** Can the function be performed by more than one component? A hospital with one generator has no redundancy; the generator is a single point of failure. A city with one water treatment plant has no redundancy; a flood that reaches the plant removes the city's water.

**Isolation.** Can a failure be contained? ERCOT's grid is largely isolated from the national grids, which protects it from regulatory oversight and also from help. Isolation can be resilience or fragility depending on which side of the boundary the failure occurs (@ferc2021).

**Recovery time.** How long does it take to restore the function? A transformer that must be manufactured overseas has a recovery time of months. The physical damage may be trivial; the recovery time is not.

**Substitutability.** If the function fails, is there another way to meet the need? A household with an electric stove and a gas hob can still cook when the power fails. A household with only electric cooking cannot eat hot food.

These four properties are more useful than any single resilience index, because they can be assessed for a specific system by people who work in it.

## The grid, and the hospital that stayed open

### The grid, in detail, because everything depends on it

Of all infrastructure systems, electricity deserves separate treatment, because almost every other
critical function depends on it: water pumping, hospitals, communications, fuel distribution, and
refrigeration.

Three features make a grid fragile in ways that are not obvious.

**Frequency is a shared variable.** Every generator on a synchronous grid must be at the same frequency,
and the frequency is a physical consequence of the balance between generation and demand. If generation
falls below demand, frequency drops, and generators protect themselves by disconnecting, which reduces
generation further. A large disturbance can propagate across a continent in seconds.

**Fuel supply is a dependency, not a given.** A gas plant is only as reliable as its pipeline. In Texas
in 2021, the gas system failed first, and the electricity failure followed [@ferc2021]. The two systems
were planned separately and failed together.

**Transmission is the bottleneck.** A grid with abundant generation in one region and demand in another
needs transmission capacity between them. Building it is slow, expensive, and locally unpopular, which
is why interconnection is chronically undersized.

The implication for disaster risk is that a grid is a national system with local failure modes, and that
protecting it requires investment in the parts that no one sees: fuel supply resilience, transmission
capacity, and the frequency-response services that keep the system stable.

### The hospital that stayed open

The clearest positive case in the infrastructure literature is the hospital that continued to function
through an event, and the features that made it possible are consistent.

The hospital of the future, as several ministries of health now design it, has four redundancies.

**Dual power feed**, plus on-site generation, plus fuel for at least 72 hours, plus a plan for
refuelling that does not depend on the same road that floods.

**On-site water storage**, sufficient for the same period, plus a plan for non-potable use and manual
sanitation.

**Communications redundancy**: satellite, radio, and cellular, with staff trained to switch.

**Staff resilience**: a plan for staff who cannot reach the hospital, including accommodation on site,
and a plan for staff who cannot leave.

The cost of these measures is small relative to the cost of a hospital evacuation, and the design
standard is well established. The gap is in enforcement and in retrofitting older buildings, which is
where most of the risk sits.

### Cascading failure is normal, not exceptional

The engineering literature treats cascading failure as an expected property of interconnected systems, not an anomaly (@buldyrev2010). Interconnection is what makes a system efficient; it is also what makes failure transmissible.

The practical implication is that resilience cannot be added at the end of a design process. It has to be designed in, which means asking, at the design stage, what happens downstream when this component fails.

That question is rarely asked, because the budget that pays for a component belongs to the sector that owns it, and the consequences belong to everyone else.

## Building codes, and the community-scale alternative

### Building codes: the cheapest adaptation

If one intervention is reliably cost-effective across disasters, it is the enforcement of building codes.

The evidence from earthquakes is well established: the difference in mortality between two earthquakes of similar magnitude is largely explained by the quality of construction and the enforcement of codes. The 2010 Haiti earthquake killed more than 100,000 people; the 2010 Chile earthquake, 500 times stronger in energy release, killed fewer than 600. The difference was not the hazard. It was the buildings.

The same principle applies to wind and flood. A house built to a cyclone standard survives wind that destroys a house built without one. The cost premium for code-compliant construction is usually a few per cent of the build cost; the loss avoided is the entire structure.

The obstacle is enforcement, not knowledge. Codes exist in most countries. Their application is uneven, and it is weakest precisely where construction is cheapest and least formal — which is where the most vulnerable people live.

### The community-scale alternative

Large centralised infrastructure is efficient and fragile. Small distributed infrastructure is less efficient and more resilient. The interesting design space is in between.

**Decentralised water.** A community well with a hand pump works when the grid fails. It is a worse water source than a treated piped supply in normal times, and a better one in an emergency.

**Distributed generation.** A hospital with solar and battery backup can maintain critical loads when the grid fails. The same investment in a central plant does not protect the hospital.

**Local storage.** A community cold room powered by a standalone system preserves food and medicine during an outage. The value is concentrated in exactly the days when the central system is down.

The pattern is that resilience is bought by accepting some inefficiency in normal times in exchange for function in abnormal ones. Systems optimised for normal times are, almost by definition, fragile.

## The cost of redundancy, and what a regulator can require

### The cost of redundancy and who pays

Redundancy costs money that shows no return until the day it is needed. This creates a predictable political failure: the budget for backup capacity is the first to be cut in a fiscal squeeze, because its absence is invisible.

The asymmetry is structural. A utility that invests in winterisation pays a visible cost and receives no visible benefit until the freeze. A utility that does not invest pays nothing and is judged on the price of electricity. The incentive is to underinvest until after a failure, then overinvest briefly while attention lasts.

Texas 2021 is again instructive. The state had experienced freezes before, in 1989 and 2011, and had received recommendations after each. The recommendations were not implemented, because the cost was certain and the benefit was contingent (@ferc2021).

This is the general problem of anticipatory spending, and it is the same problem discussed in Chapters 6 and 8. Institutions are rewarded for visible response and not for invisible prevention.

::: {.box title="Box 9.1 — Four questions for any critical system" kind="note"}
Ask these of a hospital, a water utility, a district government, or a household.

1. **What is the single point of failure?** The component whose loss removes the function entirely. Name it.
2. **What is the recovery time?** Hours, days, or months? If the answer requires a part from another continent, it is months.
3. **What fails next?** The dependency chain. A hospital's generator is useless if the fuel supply depends on the same road that floods.
4. **Who is responsible for the dependency?** Usually no one. That is the finding.
:::

### What a regulator can require

If the analysis is right that dependencies, not components, are the problem, then the regulator's job
is different from what most regulators do.

**Require dependency mapping.** Every critical operator should hold a documented map of what it depends
on and what depends on it, updated annually. The map is not the intervention; it is the precondition
for one.

**Set performance standards, not design standards.** A hospital should be required to function for 72
hours without external services, not to install a specific generator. The outcome is what matters, and
it leaves room for innovation.

**Test, publicly.** A resilience plan that has never been tested is a document. A drill that closes a
water plant for a day and records what happens is evidence.

**Fund the unglamorous layer.** Maintenance, spares, training, and fuel contracts are where resilience
actually lives, and they are the first items cut.

The reason this is hard is that none of these produces a visible asset. A regulator who requires
dependency mapping produces a stack of documents; a regulator who approves a new pumping station
produces a photograph.

## Roads, and systems that held

### Roads, and why they matter more than they seem

Transport is the infrastructure least discussed in climate adaptation and among the most consequential
in a response.

Three functions matter in an emergency.

**Access.** A road that is cut isolates a population from food, water, and medical care. In mountainous
and deltaic regions, a single road is often the only route, and a landslide or a flood removes it.

**Evacuation.** The capacity of the road network determines how many people can leave and how quickly.
A city with two exit routes and a population of a million has an evacuation problem that no warning
system can solve.

**Supply.** Fuel, food, and medical supplies move by road in most countries. A cut route raises prices
within days and produces shortages within weeks.

The design principles follow: redundancy (more than one route), elevation (routes above the design
flood), and maintenance (a road that is not maintained fails in the first storm, not the worst one) [@ipcc2022a].

The maintenance point deserves emphasis. In most countries, the road budget is dominated by new
construction and the maintenance backlog grows. A network that is extended faster than it is maintained
becomes, over time, a network that fails in ordinary weather.

### The counter-case: systems that held

Not every system fails. Some have been deliberately designed for the abnormal case, and their design choices are transferable.

**Japan's earthquake early warning** stops trains and shuts down industrial processes before the shaking arrives, using the seconds between the detection of the primary wave and the arrival of the destructive wave. The system works because it is integrated into the operations of the infrastructure it protects, not run as a separate public information service.

**The Netherlands' Delta Works** were designed with a probability standard — a defined return period for the design flood — and the standard is reviewed and revised by law. The institutional design, not the concrete, is the achievement.

**Rwanda's community health worker system** provides a distributed first-response layer that functions when the formal health system is overwhelmed. It is infrastructure in the social sense, and it is cheap.

The common feature is that the design starts from the failure case rather than the normal case [@ferc2021; @flyvbjerg2014].

## How infrastructure fails in a heatwave

### How infrastructure fails in a heat wave, which is different

The chapter has focused on storms and floods, but the fastest-growing failure mode in many countries is
heat, and it fails infrastructure in ways that are not intuitive.

**Roads buckle** [@ipcc2022a]. Asphalt softens above a threshold, and a heavy vehicle can deform a road surface
permanently. The failure is not dramatic, and it is expensive.

**Rails expand.** Rail lines have a design temperature, and above it they buckle. Railways in temperate
countries have imposed speed restrictions and cancelled services in recent European summers for this
reason.

**Transmission lines sag.** A power line carries less current at higher ambient temperature, because the
conductor's capacity to shed heat falls. A grid designed for a 35 °C day loses capacity precisely when
demand for cooling peaks.

**Transformers overheat.** A transformer's insulation degrades faster at high temperature, and the
failure is slow and cumulative.

**Water pipes burst.** Thermal expansion and soil movement break pipes, which is one reason water losses
rise during heat events.

The common feature is that each component has a design temperature, and the design temperature was set
by a climate that is changing. The failures are not spectacular, and they accumulate.

### Why redundancy is the first thing cut, and what to do about it

The political economy of redundancy deserves a paragraph, because it explains a persistent pattern across
countries and sectors.

Redundancy is invisible in normal operation [@undrr2022]. A second substation, a spare pump, a backup route, a reserve
of fuel — none of these produces any output. They produce the absence of a failure, which is not
observable and cannot be claimed.

A budget process that allocates resources to visible outputs will therefore underprovide redundancy, and
the underprovision is rational for each decision-maker. The utility that spends on a spare transformer
cannot show a return; the utility that spends on a new connection can.

Three mechanisms have changed the incentive in places that manage it well.

**Regulatory standards.** A requirement to maintain a defined level of redundancy, audited, converts an
invisible good into a compliance obligation.

**Public reporting.** Publishing the results of drills and the state of critical spares creates a
reputational cost for underprovision.

**Dedicated funding streams.** A maintenance and resilience fund that cannot be reallocated to new
construction.

Each mechanism does the same thing: it gives the invisible good a visible owner.

## Standards for the buildings we already have

### The bridge that was designed for a flood that came

A short case, because the infrastructure literature is dominated by failures and one success is instructive.

The Eastern Scheldt storm surge barrier in the Netherlands was designed in the 1970s, built over two
decades, and has been operated since 1986. It closes when the surge exceeds a threshold, and the threshold
is defined by a legal standard.

What makes it a case worth citing is the design process. The barrier was designed with the closure frequency
as an explicit parameter, with a maintenance regime, and with a review cycle. The standard has been revised
upward since, and the barrier has been adapted as part of a programme rather than replaced.

The general point is that the design of the institution — the standard, the review, the funding — is as
important as the design of the structure, which is the chapter's argument applied to a case that worked.

### The most underrated intervention: standards for existing buildings

Building codes govern new construction. Most of the buildings that will be standing in 2050 already exist,
and they were built to standards that no longer reflect the hazard.

The retrofit problem is therefore larger than the new-build problem, and it is systematically neglected
because retrofitting produces no visible new asset.

Three approaches are in use.

**Mandatory disclosure.** A building's hazard performance is disclosed at sale or rent, which allows the
market to price it. Slow, and it works where the market is informed.

**Conditional occupancy.** A building must meet a standard to be let, with a phased timetable. Effective,
and politically difficult because it imposes costs on owners who are often poor.

**Subsidised retrofit.** Public money covers part of the cost, usually targeted at low-income owners.
The most expensive per building and the most equitable.

The choice between them is a political choice about who bears the cost of a risk that was created by
standards set decades ago.

### The building that fails at the joints

A building is not a single structure but an assembly of connections: the roof to the wall, the wall to the
foundation, the foundation to the ground.

Most wind damage occurs at the roof-to-wall connection. The roof is lifted by pressure differences and
separates from the wall, and the building is then open. The failure is not a failure of materials but of
fasteners and of the continuity of the load path.

Most flood damage occurs at the openings: doors, windows, and the seals around them. Water enters, the
interior is destroyed, and the structure may be fine.

Most earthquake damage occurs at the connections between structural elements, where the forces
concentrate.

The lesson is that resilience is a property of joints, and joints are cheap to design and are the first
thing value-engineered out of a project. A hurricane strap costs a small fraction of a roof and is the
difference between a house that loses its roof and one that does not.

## A worked example: the water system


To make the dependency argument concrete, take a city water system, because it is the one every reader
depends on.

**Source.** A river intake, a reservoir, or an aquifer. Each has a hazard: a flood can damage an intake,
a drought can empty a reservoir, an aquifer can be depleted.

**Treatment.** A plant that requires power, chemicals, and staff. The plant is the single point of
failure in most systems, and it is usually located near the source, which is usually near the water,
which is usually where the flood is.

**Distribution.** Pumps that require power, pipes that require pressure, and a network that leaks.

**Disposal.** Sewers that require gravity or pumps, and a treatment works that requires power.

Four dependencies stand out. Power is needed at three stages. Chemicals are needed at one and are
imported. Staff are needed at all stages and may not be able to reach the plant. And the whole system
depends on a control room that is itself a single point of failure.

The resilience measures follow directly: on-site power and fuel, chemical stock for 30 days, a plan for
staff to stay on site, a backup control room, and — the cheapest — a way to bypass the plant for
non-potable uses so that the system does not fail completely.

None of this is exotic. All of it is routinely omitted.

### The dependency that is not in the plan

One dependency escapes most critical-infrastructure assessments because it is not a sector: money.

A response requires cash — for fuel, for contractors, for supplies, for wages. A government that cannot pay
in the first week cannot respond, regardless of its plans. The constraint is fiscal, and it is rarely
modelled.

Three features of the fiscal dependency matter.

**Payment systems fail with the power.** A cash-based economy survives a grid failure; a card-based one
does not, unless there is a mechanism for offline transactions.

**Contractors need to be paid.** A pre-agreed contract with a construction company is worthless if the
payment authority has been disrupted.

**Local government is often the payer of last resort** and the level of government with the least fiscal
capacity.

The practical remedy is pre-arranged emergency procurement and a cash reserve held at the level of
government that will actually spend it.

### The single point of failure in a national system

Zooming out from the city, a national system has its own single points of failure, and they are rarely the
ones that get attention.

**The capital's infrastructure.** A country whose ministries, banks, and broadcasters are in one city has a
concentration risk that no sectoral plan addresses.

**The undersea cable.** Most countries' international connectivity depends on a small number of cables that
come ashore at a small number of points. A cut, by an anchor or an event, isolates the country.

**The fuel terminal.** A country with one import terminal has a single point of failure for transport,
agriculture, and emergency response.

**The payment system.** A centralised payment system that fails stops the economy, including the response.

Each is a dependency that is invisible in normal operation, and each can be addressed with redundancy at a
cost that is small relative to the consequence.

## The hospital that moved, and the vulnerability that is a location

### The hospital that moved

An adaptation that is rarely considered and is increasingly necessary: the relocation of a critical facility.

A hospital built on a site that will flood, a water plant on a riverbank, a substation on a floodplain — the
option of moving the facility is expensive, disruptive, and sometimes cheaper than protecting it in place.

The decision has three features.

**It is irreversible**, which is why it is delayed.

**It requires a replacement site**, which in a dense city does not exist.

**It requires a sequence**, because the service cannot be interrupted during the move.

The practical approach is a staged replacement: build the new facility, commission it, then retire the old
one. The cost is a period of double operation, and the alternative is a facility that fails in the event it
was built for.

### The infrastructure that is a schedule

A reframing that helps with policy: much of what we call infrastructure is a schedule.

A bus service is a schedule. A water supply is a pumping regime. A drainage system is a maintenance
calendar. A hospital is a rota.

In a disaster, the schedule is the thing that fails. The pumps stop because the power is out; the rota
collapses because the staff cannot reach the hospital; the maintenance calendar was already behind.

The implication for resilience is that the schedule must have a fallback: a manual pump, a rota with a
redundant shift, a maintenance backlog that is cleared before the season. These are unglamorous and they are
the difference between a system that functions and one that does not.

### The vulnerability that is a location

A connection back to Chapter 1.

Infrastructure vulnerability is often described as a property of the asset — its age, its design, its
condition. It is more accurately a property of its location.

A substation is vulnerable because it is on a floodplain. A water intake is vulnerable because it is on a
riverbank. A hospital is vulnerable because it is on the coast. The asset may be well built and well
maintained, and its location determines its fate.

The implication is that the siting decision is the most consequential one, and it is made early, cheaply,
and often without any hazard analysis.

The remedy is procedural: a hazard screening for any new critical facility, and a register of existing
facilities in hazardous locations, ranked by consequence.

### The question to ask about any critical system

A single question, which the chapter has been building toward.

**What happens downstream when this component fails?**

Ask it of a substation, a pump, a bridge, a hospital generator, a payment system. The answer is the
dependency map, and the dependency map is the resilience plan.

A city that has answered the question for its twenty most critical systems has done more than a city with a
hundred-page strategy.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that infrastructure failure is primarily a property of dependencies rather than of individual components, and that redundancy and code enforcement are systematically underfunded because their benefits are invisible.

The falsifier is a comparison of failure modes. Take a sample of infrastructure failures in extreme events and classify each by proximate cause: component failure under load, or failure propagated through a dependency. The chapter is wrong if component failures predominate — say, more than two-thirds of outages and service failures — and dependency chains explain little.

The Texas 2021 case is the chapter's clearest evidence and also its limit: the grid failed because gas supply, water, and power were mutually dependent, and the sequence is documented [@ferc2021]. A system that failed in a heat wave with no dependency chain — a single transformer, a single pump — would not refute the claim, but a population of such failures would.

The funding claim is falsified by budget data: if utilities that spent more on redundancy had the same outage duration as those that did not, controlling for event severity, the claim fails. That comparison is available from utility reliability filings, and it is the kind of test this book should invite rather than avoid [@flyvbjerg2014].
