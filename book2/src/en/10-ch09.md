# Infrastructure as a Chain of Dependencies

::: lead
A power grid is not a machine. It is an agreement that everyone will keep doing their part at the same time. When the agreement fails in one place, it fails everywhere downstream — and downstream is where the hospital is.
:::

## The sectoral mistake

Infrastructure is planned sector by sector: energy, water, transport, health, communications. Each has its own ministry, its own budget, its own standards, and its own definition of resilience.

A disaster does not respect the boundaries. It moves through the dependencies between sectors, which are invisible in the plans.

The Texas 2021 failure is the clearest recent case. Gas wellheads and supply lines were not winterised, because the gas sector's standards were written for a climate that had not yet produced a sustained freeze. When they froze, generation fell. When generation fell, pumps stopped. When pumps stopped, water systems lost pressure. When pressure fell, boil-water notices spread across 190 counties, affecting roughly 14.4 million people (@ferc2021).

Every sector complied with its own code. The system failed because no one was responsible for the dependencies.

## Four properties that determine whether a system holds

Not all infrastructure fails in the same way. Four properties predict behaviour in a shock.

**Redundancy.** Can the function be performed by more than one component? A hospital with one generator has no redundancy; the generator is a single point of failure. A city with one water treatment plant has no redundancy; a flood that reaches the plant removes the city's water.

**Isolation.** Can a failure be contained? ERCOT's grid is largely isolated from the national grids, which protects it from regulatory oversight and also from help. Isolation can be resilience or fragility depending on which side of the boundary the failure occurs (@ferc2021).

**Recovery time.** How long does it take to restore the function? A transformer that must be manufactured overseas has a recovery time of months. The physical damage may be trivial; the recovery time is not.

**Substitutability.** If the function fails, is there another way to meet the need? A household with an electric stove and a gas hob can still cook when the power fails. A household with only electric cooking cannot eat hot food.

These four properties are more useful than any single resilience index, because they can be assessed for a specific system by people who work in it.

## The grid, in detail, because everything depends on it

Of all infrastructure systems, electricity deserves separate treatment, because almost every other
critical function depends on it: water pumping, hospitals, communications, fuel distribution, and
refrigeration.

Three features make a grid fragile in ways that are not obvious.

**Frequency is a shared variable.** Every generator on a synchronous grid must be at the same frequency,
and the frequency is a physical consequence of the balance between generation and demand. If generation
falls below demand, frequency drops, and generators protect themselves by disconnecting, which reduces
generation further. A large disturbance can propagate across a continent in seconds.

**Fuel supply is a dependency, not a given.** A gas plant is only as reliable as its pipeline. In Texas
in 2021, the gas system failed first, and the electricity failure followed [@ferc2021]. The two systems
were planned separately and failed together.

**Transmission is the bottleneck.** A grid with abundant generation in one region and demand in another
needs transmission capacity between them. Building it is slow, expensive, and locally unpopular, which
is why interconnection is chronically undersized.

The implication for disaster risk is that a grid is a national system with local failure modes, and that
protecting it requires investment in the parts that no one sees: fuel supply resilience, transmission
capacity, and the frequency-response services that keep the system stable.

## The hospital that stayed open

The clearest positive case in the infrastructure literature is the hospital that continued to function
through an event, and the features that made it possible are consistent.

The hospital of the future, as several ministries of health now design it, has four redundancies.

**Dual power feed**, plus on-site generation, plus fuel for at least 72 hours, plus a plan for
refuelling that does not depend on the same road that floods.

**On-site water storage**, sufficient for the same period, plus a plan for non-potable use and manual
sanitation.

**Communications redundancy**: satellite, radio, and cellular, with staff trained to switch.

**Staff resilience**: a plan for staff who cannot reach the hospital, including accommodation on site,
and a plan for staff who cannot leave.

The cost of these measures is small relative to the cost of a hospital evacuation, and the design
standard is well established. The gap is in enforcement and in retrofitting older buildings, which is
where most of the risk sits.

## Cascading failure is normal, not exceptional

The engineering literature treats cascading failure as an expected property of interconnected systems, not an anomaly (@buldyrev2010). Interconnection is what makes a system efficient; it is also what makes failure transmissible.

The practical implication is that resilience cannot be added at the end of a design process. It has to be designed in, which means asking, at the design stage, what happens downstream when this component fails.

That question is rarely asked, because the budget that pays for a component belongs to the sector that owns it, and the consequences belong to everyone else.

## Building codes: the cheapest adaptation

If one intervention is reliably cost-effective across disasters, it is the enforcement of building codes.

The evidence from earthquakes is well established: the difference in mortality between two earthquakes of similar magnitude is largely explained by the quality of construction and the enforcement of codes. The 2010 Haiti earthquake killed more than 100,000 people; the 2010 Chile earthquake, 500 times stronger in energy release, killed fewer than 600. The difference was not the hazard. It was the buildings.

The same principle applies to wind and flood. A house built to a cyclone standard survives wind that destroys a house built without one. The cost premium for code-compliant construction is usually a few per cent of the build cost; the loss avoided is the entire structure.

The obstacle is enforcement, not knowledge. Codes exist in most countries. Their application is uneven, and it is weakest precisely where construction is cheapest and least formal — which is where the most vulnerable people live.

## The community-scale alternative

Large centralised infrastructure is efficient and fragile. Small distributed infrastructure is less efficient and more resilient. The interesting design space is in between.

**Decentralised water.** A community well with a hand pump works when the grid fails. It is a worse water source than a treated piped supply in normal times, and a better one in an emergency.

**Distributed generation.** A hospital with solar and battery backup can maintain critical loads when the grid fails. The same investment in a central plant does not protect the hospital.

**Local storage.** A community cold room powered by a standalone system preserves food and medicine during an outage. The value is concentrated in exactly the days when the central system is down.

The pattern is that resilience is bought by accepting some inefficiency in normal times in exchange for function in abnormal ones. Systems optimised for normal times are, almost by definition, fragile.

## The cost of redundancy and who pays

Redundancy costs money that shows no return until the day it is needed. This creates a predictable political failure: the budget for backup capacity is the first to be cut in a fiscal squeeze, because its absence is invisible.

The asymmetry is structural. A utility that invests in winterisation pays a visible cost and receives no visible benefit until the freeze. A utility that does not invest pays nothing and is judged on the price of electricity. The incentive is to underinvest until after a failure, then overinvest briefly while attention lasts.

Texas 2021 is again instructive. The state had experienced freezes before, in 1989 and 2011, and had received recommendations after each. The recommendations were not implemented, because the cost was certain and the benefit was contingent (@ferc2021).

This is the general problem of anticipatory spending, and it is the same problem discussed in Chapters 6 and 8. Institutions are rewarded for visible response and not for invisible prevention.

::: {.box title="Box 9.1 — Four questions for any critical system" kind="note"}
Ask these of a hospital, a water utility, a district government, or a household.

1. **What is the single point of failure?** The component whose loss removes the function entirely. Name it.
2. **What is the recovery time?** Hours, days, or months? If the answer requires a part from another continent, it is months.
3. **What fails next?** The dependency chain. A hospital's generator is useless if the fuel supply depends on the same road that floods.
4. **Who is responsible for the dependency?** Usually no one. That is the finding.
:::

## What a regulator can require

If the analysis is right that dependencies, not components, are the problem, then the regulator's job
is different from what most regulators do.

**Require dependency mapping.** Every critical operator should hold a documented map of what it depends
on and what depends on it, updated annually. The map is not the intervention; it is the precondition
for one.

**Set performance standards, not design standards.** A hospital should be required to function for 72
hours without external services, not to install a specific generator. The outcome is what matters, and
it leaves room for innovation.

**Test, publicly.** A resilience plan that has never been tested is a document. A drill that closes a
water plant for a day and records what happens is evidence.

**Fund the unglamorous layer.** Maintenance, spares, training, and fuel contracts are where resilience
actually lives, and they are the first items cut.

The reason this is hard is that none of these produces a visible asset. A regulator who requires
dependency mapping produces a stack of documents; a regulator who approves a new pumping station
produces a photograph.

## The counter-case: systems that held

Not every system fails. Some have been deliberately designed for the abnormal case, and their design choices are transferable.

**Japan's earthquake early warning** stops trains and shuts down industrial processes before the shaking arrives, using the seconds between the detection of the primary wave and the arrival of the destructive wave. The system works because it is integrated into the operations of the infrastructure it protects, not run as a separate public information service.

**The Netherlands' Delta Works** were designed with a probability standard — a defined return period for the design flood — and the standard is reviewed and revised by law. The institutional design, not the concrete, is the achievement.

**Rwanda's community health worker system** provides a distributed first-response layer that functions when the formal health system is overwhelmed. It is infrastructure in the social sense, and it is cheap.

The common feature is that the design starts from the failure case rather than the normal case.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that infrastructure failure is primarily a property of dependencies rather than of component strength, and that redundancy and code enforcement are systematically underfunded because their benefits are invisible.

It would be wrong if systems with strong individual components and no redundancy performed as well in shocks as redundant systems, controlling for hazard. The engineering evidence on cascading failure points the other way.
