# Disease That Moves Because the Climate Moves

::: lead
Mosquitoes do not carry passports. When the climate that suits them shifts, the diseases they carry shift with it, and they arrive in health systems that were built for a different map.
:::

## The mechanism is ecological, not meteorological

A warming climate changes disease geography through several distinct channels, and they are often conflated.

**Vector range shifts** [@mordecai2020]. The *Aedes aegypti* mosquito, which carries dengue, chikungunya, and Zika, is limited by temperature: too cold and the virus does not replicate fast enough inside the insect; too hot and the mosquito dies before it can transmit. Warming expands the range at the cold edge and, in some tropical regions, compresses it at the hot edge. The net direction is generally poleward and uphill.

**Season length.** A longer warm season means a longer transmission window. In highland areas of East Africa and Latin America, malaria has appeared at altitudes where it was previously absent (@mordecai2020).

**Extreme events.** Floods create breeding sites and displace people into temporary settlements with poor drainage. Droughts concentrate water sources and can increase vector density around them. Both mechanisms are non-linear and both are hard to model (@mordecai2020).

**Water and sanitation.** The larger driver of climate-sensitive disease is not the mosquito but the failure of water and sanitation systems in extreme events. Cholera and other diarrhoeal diseases follow flooding and displacement far more reliably than they follow temperature.

## Dengue: a 30-fold rise, and what it means

Dengue is the clearest case, and the numbers are large.

The WHO recorded about 0.5 million cases in 2000 and about 4.2 million in 2022 (@who2023dengue). The incidence of dengue has increased roughly 30-fold over fifty years. Approximately 129 countries are now endemic, and an estimated 100–400 million infections occur each year (@who2023dengue).

Three cautions are necessary before those numbers are used.

First, a large part of the increase is surveillance. Countries that did not report dengue in 1970 now have reporting systems, and a disease that is tested for is a disease that is counted. Second, urbanisation is a major driver independent of climate: *Aedes aegypti* breeds in containers and thrives in dense cities with unreliable water supply, because households store water in exactly the containers the mosquito prefers. Third, the 30-fold figure is a WHO summary, and the underlying calculation is not published in a form that can be reproduced.

None of this means climate is irrelevant. It means the correct statement is that climate change is one driver among several, operating on a disease whose expansion was already underway. That is a less dramatic claim and a more defensible one.

The 2023–2024 surge in the Americas, with record cases in Brazil and Argentina, is more clearly climate-linked: high temperatures and El Niño-driven rainfall coincided with the expansion of the mosquito's range into temperate zones.

## Cholera in Yemen: the infrastructure case in its purest form

The Yemen cholera epidemic of 2016–2019 is the clearest example in the recent literature of a disease
whose transmission was determined almost entirely by infrastructure, in a setting where the climate
contribution was real but secondary.

The country was in the third year of a war that had destroyed or degraded water and sanitation systems,
displaced millions of people, and left the health system dependent on a fraction of its former staff
and supplies. Cholera is a waterborne disease whose transmission depends on contaminated water and
inadequate sanitation; in a functioning system it is treatable and preventable.

The epidemic produced more than a million suspected cases — the largest ever recorded in a single
country in a single outbreak [@who2017yemen]. The case fatality rate was kept low by rapid
rehydration, which is a cheap intervention, but the number of cases was determined by the state of the
plumbing.

The rainfall contribution was not zero. Seasonal rains and flooding created conditions for
contamination, and the pattern of cases followed the rainy season in some governorates. But the
difference between a rainy season that produces a few hundred cases and one that produces hundreds of
thousands is the integrity of the water system.

The lesson generalises across the chapter. When a climate-sensitive disease appears, the first question
should not be "what is the climate doing" but "what has failed in the water, sanitation, and health
system". The second question is usually the one with an actionable answer.

## Malaria and the limits of the climate signal

Malaria is the disease most often cited in climate-health arguments, and the evidence on its climate
sensitivity is more complicated than the headlines suggest.

The mosquito that transmits malaria is sensitive to temperature, and warming does expand the altitudinal
and latitudinal range in some regions. Highland malaria in East Africa has appeared at altitudes where
it was previously absent, and the mechanism — a longer transmission season at altitude — is well
established [@mordecai2020].

But the dominant driver of malaria incidence over the last two decades has not been temperature. It has
been control: insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, artemisinin-based treatment, and the
funding that pays for them. Global malaria incidence fell substantially between 2000 and 2015, and the
stalls and reversals since then track funding and insecticide resistance more closely than they track
temperature.

This matters for policy because the two explanations point to different interventions. A climate-first
account points to surveillance and range prediction. A control-first account points to nets, spraying,
and treatment, which are known to work and are underfunded.

Both are true. The mistake is to let the climate signal displace the control agenda, which is a
temptation because the climate story is more novel and easier to fund.

## Why "climate disease" is usually an infrastructure disease

The most useful analytical move in this chapter is to separate the climate signal from the infrastructure signal.

Cholera in Yemen, dengue in Dhaka, and leptospirosis after flooding all share a pattern: the climate event triggers a disease that spreads through a system that was already broken. The pathogen is not new; the exposure route is.

This has a direct policy consequence. Vaccines and vector control are disease-specific and expensive. Water supply, drainage, and sewage are generic and protect against a whole family of diseases at once. A health system that treats the climate signal as a reason to stockpile vaccines will miss the cheaper intervention — which is usually plumbing.

## Health systems as the weakest link

A disease that arrives in a functioning health system is a manageable event. The same disease in a system with no surge capacity is a disaster.

The distinguishing variables are not only medical. They are the number of community health workers, the stock of oral rehydration salts, the presence of a laboratory that can confirm a diagnosis within a day, and the ability to move a patient from a rural clinic to a district hospital.

During the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, the virus killed more people through the collapse of routine health services than through direct infection: measles vaccination dropped, malaria went untreated, and maternal deaths rose (@who2016). The same pattern appears in every major epidemic, and it is the reason climate-sensitive disease outbreaks have effects far beyond the disease.

## The 2024 dengue surge: when the signal is unambiguous

The dengue season of 2023–2024 in the Americas is worth examining because it is the case where the
climate signal is least contestable.

Brazil recorded more than six million probable cases in 2024, the highest in its history, and several
states declared emergencies. Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay reported cases in areas where transmission
had not previously been established. The pattern was associated with high temperatures and El Niño-driven
rainfall, and it was forecast by surveillance systems before it peaked.

Three features distinguish this event from the long-term 30-fold increase.

**The range expansion was into temperate zones.** Transmission in Argentina and Uruguay is not a
reporting artefact; it is the mosquito establishing in places where it was previously absent, which is
the climate signal made visible.

**The speed was fast.** The surge developed over weeks, not years, which is characteristic of a
climate-driven event rather than a development-driven trend.

**The response was uneven.** Cities with functioning *Aedes* surveillance and vector control contained
the surge better than cities without, which is the infrastructure argument of this chapter in
miniature.

The lesson is not that climate change caused the increase in dengue cases. It is that climate change
has made the baseline higher and the surges sharper, and that the difference in outcomes within a single
country is determined by the state of the public health system.

## The disease that did not arrive

A useful check on any claim that climate change is spreading disease is to look for the disease that
did not spread.

Malaria was eliminated from Europe and North America in the twentieth century, and the mosquitoes that
can transmit it are still present in many of those places. *Anopheles* species survive in southern
Europe; the parasite was eliminated by draining breeding sites, improving housing, and treating cases,
not by cooling.

The same is true of yellow fever in the Americas, of cholera in Europe, and of plague in most of the
world. These diseases were driven out by infrastructure and public health, in climates that remained
suitable for their transmission.

That history matters because it establishes the counterfactual. A warming climate moves the boundary of
suitability, but suitability is not transmission. Transmission requires a mosquito, a pathogen, a
susceptible person, and a system that fails to interrupt the chain. The first two are climate-sensitive;
the last two are policy.

## Mental health: the part that is counted last

No chapter on health and climate is complete without the part that does not appear in case counts.

The evidence on disaster-related mental health is now substantial: elevated rates of post-traumatic stress, depression, and anxiety in the years after events; a measurable rise in suicides among farmers following drought; and a pattern of "solastalgia" — distress caused by the loss of a familiar environment — that is not resolvable by reconstruction (@doherty2016).

The mechanisms are specific. Displacement breaks social networks. Loss of livelihood removes identity as well as income. Repeated exposure to a hazard that cannot be escaped produces chronic stress, which has physiological consequences.

Mental health is the clearest case of a harm that the standard disaster accounting misses entirely, and the clearest argument for treating recovery as a social process rather than a construction project.

::: {.box title="Box 7.1 — Reading a disease number" kind="note"}
Four questions to ask of any claim that a disease is "spreading because of climate change."

1. **Is the increase in cases or in reporting?** Surveillance expansion produces rising case counts with no change in incidence.
2. **What is the denominator?** Cases per 100,000 is a different claim from total cases in a growing population.
3. **What else changed?** Urbanisation, water supply, insecticide resistance, and vaccination coverage all move independently.
4. **Is the link mechanistic or correlational?** A mechanism — the virus replicating faster at higher temperature — is stronger evidence than a time-series correlation.

The WHO dengue figures pass questions one and two reasonably well and struggle with three. That does not make them wrong; it makes them a starting point.
:::

## Surveillance is the intervention

If the chapter has one recommendation, it is surveillance, and it is worth explaining why it is more
important than any single disease-specific measure.

A health system that detects an outbreak within days can contain it with cheap interventions —
rehydration, vector control, isolation, and communication. The same outbreak detected within weeks
requires a large emergency response, which is expensive and arrives after most of the harm.

The difference between days and weeks is not clinical skill. It is the presence of a laboratory that
can confirm a diagnosis, a reporting chain that functions, and a threshold at which someone is
authorised to act.

Three features of a functioning surveillance system recur in the successful cases.

**Syndromic surveillance.** Counting cases of fever and diarrhoea, not just confirmed diagnoses, catches
outbreaks before the lab confirms them.

**Community reporting.** Community health workers who can report by phone shorten the chain from
household to district.

**A pre-authorised response.** A system that must apply for permission to act will act late. The
authority to close a water source, distribute treatment, or spray should be delegated in advance.

The cost is small relative to an outbreak. The reason it is underprovided is the same reason that runs
through this book: the benefit is an outbreak that did not happen.

## The vaccine that exists and does not arrive

Dengue has a vaccine. It is not a perfect vaccine — it protects against some serotypes better than
others, and its use requires careful targeting because of the interaction with prior infection — but it
exists, and it has been licensed in several countries.

The gap between its existence and its effect illustrates the chapter's argument.

**Cost.** The vaccine is expensive relative to the budgets of the health systems in the places with the
highest burden.

**Delivery.** A vaccine that requires two or three doses over a year requires a functioning primary care
system and a population that returns. In places where the same system is struggling with the basics,
completion rates are low.

**Targeting.** The vaccine is most useful in populations with prior exposure, which requires
seroprevalence data that most countries do not have.

**The vector does not care.** Even a successful vaccination programme does not reduce the mosquito
population, so the transmission pressure continues and the programme must be maintained indefinitely.

The result is that vector control — the unglamorous work of removing standing water, larviciding, and
community clean-up — remains the mainstay, and it is chronically underfunded because it produces no
headline.

## The counter-case: places that eliminated the disease

Some places have driven climate-sensitive diseases down, and the methods matter.

Sri Lanka eliminated malaria in 2016 after decades of indoor residual spraying, insecticide-treated nets, and — critically — a surveillance system that could detect and respond to every case. The climate did not change. The system did.

Cuba reduced dengue transmission substantially through *Aedes* control programmes that combined community cleanup with larvicide, at a cost that a middle-income country could sustain.

The common feature is a functioning public health system with the authority to act locally. The countries that struggle are those where the health system is centralised, underfunded, and slow to reach the periphery.

## What a health ministry should actually budget for

The chapter's argument implies a shift in the health budget that is counter-intuitive, because it
spends money outside the health sector.

**Surveillance and laboratory capacity.** The single highest-return item. Cheap, and it shortens every
response.

**Community health workers.** A distributed workforce that can detect, report, and treat at the
periphery, where the outbreaks start.

**Water and sanitation.** Not a health budget item in most countries, and the largest determinant of
climate-sensitive disease burden. The case for health ministries to co-fund it is strong and rarely made.

**Vector control with a monitoring component.** Insecticide resistance is a moving target; a programme
without resistance monitoring becomes ineffective without anyone noticing.

**Mental health capacity.** The part that is always last and always underfunded, despite a consistent
evidence base on post-disaster need.

The pattern is that the items with the largest effect are the ones that are hardest to attribute to a
single disease, and therefore hardest to fund from a disease-specific budget line.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that climate-sensitive disease is primarily a function of health and water infrastructure, that climate change is one driver among several, and that generic infrastructure investment protects better than disease-specific campaigns.

It would be wrong if disease incidence tracked climate variables closely across countries with very different health systems, with infrastructure variables adding nothing once temperature and rainfall are controlled for.

The evidence suggests otherwise: neighbouring countries with similar climates have very different disease burdens, and the differences track surveillance capacity, water supply, and vector control.

## The outbreak that was contained

A counter-example is worth recording, because the chapter has described mostly failures.

In 2014, Nigeria reported its first Ebola case, imported by a traveller from Liberia. The country was
poorly prepared in infrastructure terms and had recently emerged from conflict in the north. It contained
the outbreak within three months.

The mechanism was a rapid, coordinated response built on a pre-existing infrastructure: a national
emergency operations centre established years earlier for polio eradication, a surveillance system with
staff in every state, and a laboratory network that could confirm cases quickly. The polio programme had
built the capacity, and it was repurposed.

The case establishes three points.

**Capacity is transferable.** The infrastructure built for one disease works for another, which argues for
generic surveillance capacity rather than disease-specific programmes.

**Speed is decisive.** The outbreak was contained because the first case was detected and isolated within
days, not weeks.

**The capacity existed before it was needed.** It was not created in response to the outbreak; it was
maintained for another purpose.

The lesson for climate-sensitive disease is direct: the systems that respond to a dengue surge are the same
systems that respond to cholera, and they are built in the quiet years.

## The disease surveillance dividend

One number is worth carrying out of this chapter, because it justifies the whole investment.

The cost of a basic surveillance system — a laboratory network, a reporting chain, and a handful of
trained epidemiologists — is small relative to the cost of a single outbreak response. The 2014 Ebola
outbreak in West Africa cost the international system several billion dollars in response and produced
an economic loss in the affected countries estimated at more than two billion. A surveillance system
that had detected the outbreak in its first weeks would have cost a fraction of that.

The same arithmetic applies to dengue, cholera, and every climate-sensitive disease. Detection is
cheap; response is expensive; the difference between them is measured in days.

The reason it is not funded is the same reason that runs through this book: the benefit is an outbreak
that did not happen, and no one holds a press conference for it.
