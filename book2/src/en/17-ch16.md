# Communities and Cities: Capacity Built Before the Rain

::: lead
The most useful thing a city can do about climate risk is rarely the thing that gets a ribbon cutting. It is the unglamorous, distributed work of making the ordinary systems — drainage, shade, transport, shelters, social services — work on the worst day of the decade.
:::

## What adaptive capacity actually is

### What adaptive capacity actually is

Adaptive capacity is the ability of a system to adjust to a shock without losing its essential functions. It is not the same as resilience, which is the ability to absorb a shock and return to the previous state. Adaptive capacity includes the ability to change the state.

For a city, the components are concrete.

**Fiscal capacity.** Can the city pay for maintenance, not just construction? A drainage system that is not maintained is a drainage system that will fail, and maintenance budgets are the first to be cut.

**Technical capacity.** Does the city have engineers, planners, and public health staff who understand the risks? This is a hiring and retention problem, and it is severe in smaller cities.

**Institutional capacity.** Can departments coordinate? Most climate risks cross departmental boundaries, and most city governments are organised along those boundaries.

**Social capacity.** Do residents trust the government, and do they have organisations that can act? This is the social capital of Chapter 10, at city scale.

The four are not interchangeable. A city with money and no technical staff will build the wrong thing. A city with good staff and no money will plan well and implement nothing.

### The city that moved early: an example worth copying

Among the cities that have done the unglamorous work, one case is worth setting out because it is
replicable rather than heroic.

**Ahmedabad** adopted a heat action plan in 2013, three years after a heat wave killed more than a
thousand people. The plan combined a forecast-triggered alert, a register of vulnerable residents,
changes to outdoor working hours, and free drinking water at public places. An evaluation found lower
mortality in a subsequent heat wave than the temperature alone would have predicted [@hess2018].

The replicable features are unremarkable.

**A lead agency.** The health department, not the environment department, because heat is a health
outcome.

**A partner with technical capacity.** An NGO provided the epidemiological analysis that the municipality
could not.

**A trigger tied to a forecast.** The plan activates on a temperature threshold, not on a political
decision.

**A register.** Names and addresses of the people most at risk, held by the municipality and the health
service.

None of these requires a large budget. All of them require a decision, which is why they are not
universal.

## Shelters that get used

A shelter that nobody uses is not a shelter. The difference between a shelter that is used and one that is not is rarely the building.

**Distance.** People will not travel far in an emergency, especially with children or with limited mobility. Shelters need to be within walking distance of the population they serve.

**Trust.** A school or a mosque that people already know is more likely to be used than a purpose-built facility they have never entered.

**Acceptance of pets and livestock.** In many communities, people will not evacuate without animals, and a shelter that excludes them will be empty. This is not sentimentality; livestock is a household's savings.

**Dignity and privacy.** Shelters that cannot provide basic privacy for women and girls are shelters that women will not use. This is one of the most consistent findings in the evacuation literature, and one of the most commonly ignored.

**A known destination.** People evacuate to places they know. A shelter that has been used for a community event is a known destination.

## Land use is the real climate decision

### Land use is the real climate decision

Most cities have a climate adaptation plan and a land-use plan, and the land-use plan determines the outcome.

Where new development goes determines exposure for the next century. Whether a floodplain is zoned for storage or for housing is a decision that no drainage investment can reverse. Whether the city extends into a wildfire interface is a decision made in a planning committee, not in a climate office.

The practical problem is that land-use decisions are made incrementally, parcel by parcel, under pressure from developers and landowners, and the cumulative effect is rarely assessed. A city can approve every individual development on its merits and end up with a pattern that is catastrophic in aggregate.

The intervention that works is not a better plan document. It is a decision rule: a line beyond which development is not permitted, a density requirement in safe areas, and a consistent refusal that developers can rely on.

### The school as infrastructure

A school is the most multipurpose building a city owns, and it is systematically underused in disaster
planning.

It has: a large roof, a kitchen, toilets, water storage, a hall, a known location, and a population that
already knows how to find it. In a heat wave it is a cooling centre. In a flood it is a shelter. In an
emergency it is a distribution point. In a drought it is a water collection surface.

The reasons it is not used are administrative. The education department controls it, the disaster agency
needs it, and no one has authority to open it. The building is empty and locked while people are
sheltering under a bridge.

The fix is a protocol, agreed in advance: who opens the school, who staffs it, who pays for the
electricity and the cleaning, and who is liable. These are boring questions, and they are the reason the
building stays locked.

### Open space as infrastructure

The cheapest flood defence in many cities is the space that is already open.

A park that floods is a park that is doing its job. A schoolyard that becomes a basin is a schoolyard that protects the neighbourhood. A wetland that is preserved is a wetland that stores water and reduces the peak downstream.

The design principle is **multifunctionality**: land that serves a normal function most of the year and a hazard function in the event. This is cheaper than single-purpose infrastructure and it is more politically achievable, because it produces a visible amenity.

The barrier is institutional. A park is managed by the parks department, a drainage basin by the water utility, and a schoolyard by the education department. Multifunctional land requires someone to be responsible for the function that crosses departmental lines.

### Heat and the shape of the city

For heat, the city-scale interventions are well established and cheap relative to engineering.

**Tree cover** [@ipcc2022a]. Shade reduces surface temperature and pedestrian heat exposure. The evidence on the magnitude varies by species, location, and irrigation, but the direction is consistent: more canopy, less heat (@ipcc2022a).

**Cool surfaces.** Light-coloured roofs and pavements reflect more solar radiation. The effect is real and smaller than tree cover.

**Ventilation corridors.** Street orientation and building height determine whether air moves through the city. Cities that have built dense, low-permeability blocks trap heat.

**Cooling access.** Public buildings that are open and free during heat events. The barrier is not the building; it is the decision to keep it open, staffed, and welcoming.

## Where to allow building

### The one decision that matters most: where to allow building

If a city could make only one decision about climate risk, it should be this one.

Where new construction is permitted determines exposure for the life of the buildings, which is fifty to
a hundred years. No drainage scheme, warning system, or emergency plan can compensate for a city that
has built in the wrong place.

The decision is made through zoning, permits, and infrastructure extension, and it is usually made
incrementally, parcel by parcel, by officials who are not thinking about climate.

Three practical mechanisms have worked.

**A hazard line with legal force.** Not a map in a plan, but a rule that prohibits construction beyond a
line, with a defined review process to move the line as the evidence changes.

**Conditional permits.** Construction permitted only if the developer demonstrates that the building will
function at the design hazard, which transfers the cost to the party creating the exposure.

**Infrastructure sequencing.** Not extending water, drainage, and roads into areas that should not be
built, which is the quietest and most effective form of land-use control.

The third is the most powerful and the least discussed. A city that does not run pipes to a floodplain
does not need to prohibit building there.

### The city that is a hundred villages

Most rapidly growing cities are not single systems. They are conurbations of former villages, each with
its own road, its own drainage, its own water supply, and its own informal leadership, absorbed by
expansion.

This structure is invisible in the city plan and decisive in a disaster. A village that was built on high
ground and has never flooded keeps its own drainage and its own social network; a village that was built
on low ground has neither. The city's average performance conceals a variance that determines who dies.

Three implications follow.

**Map the sub-systems.** The unit of adaptation is the former village, not the administrative district.

**Use the existing leadership.** A village head who is still recognised by residents is a more effective
convenor than a municipal appointee.

**Do not standardise prematurely.** A uniform drainage standard applied to a hundred settlements with
different topography produces a hundred failures of different kinds.

The practical recommendation is that a city should know its own seams, and should plan at the scale of the
seam rather than the scale of the map.

## The budget test

### The budget test

A simple test distinguishes cities that are adapting from cities that are writing plans.

**Look at the maintenance budget.** Is it funded at the level required to keep drainage, water, and road systems functioning? A city that underfunds maintenance is not adapting, regardless of its plan.

**Look at the capital budget.** What share goes to distributed, multifunctional, low-regret measures rather than to a single large structure?

**Look at the land-use decisions.** What has been refused? A city that has never refused a development on hazard grounds has not integrated risk into planning.

**Look at who is at the table.** Are public health, social services, and community organisations involved, or only engineering and planning?

::: {.box title="Box 16.1 — Five questions for a city climate plan" kind="note"}
1. **What is the specific hazard, and where?** A plan that lists all hazards for the whole city has not made a decision.
2. **What is funded?** Distinguish the measures with a budget line from the aspirations.
3. **What is refused?** The land-use rules are the plan.
4. **Who maintains it?** A measure without a maintenance budget is a measure with an expiry date.
5. **Who is not protected?** The distribution of protection is the political content of the plan.
:::

### The neighbourhood scale, which is where most adaptation happens

The chapter has moved between the city and the household, and the scale in between is where most of the
work is done.

A neighbourhood has properties that neither the city nor the household has: a shared drainage system, a
shared street, a shared school, and a set of people who know each other.

Four interventions operate at this scale and are cheap.

**The street drain.** A shared responsibility that no one owns. A neighbourhood agreement to clear it
before the rainy season is worth more than a municipal programme that arrives after the flood.

**The shaded route.** A walking route to the school, the clinic, and the market that has shade and water.
Mapping it is a weekend project and it changes behaviour.

**The meeting point.** A raised building, a mosque, a school, identified in advance as the place to go.

**The list.** Names and numbers of the people who will need help, held by someone on the street.

The reason these work is that they are small enough to be done without a budget and specific enough to be
tested. The reason they are not universal is that they require a convenor, and convenors are not funded.

### What a city cannot do alone

The chapter has described what a city can do, and it is important to state the limits, because the
rhetoric of "city leadership" often obscures them.

**A city cannot change the national building code.** It can enforce a stricter local standard, and it can
be overruled.

**A city cannot finance a major defence alone.** A seawall is a national project, and a city that tries to
build one usually discovers the fiscal constraint.

**A city cannot stop migration.** A receiving city can plan for it, and it cannot prevent it.

**A city cannot control the emissions that set the hazard.** It can reduce its own, which is a small
fraction of the total.

The result is that the most effective city action is often advocacy for national policy, and the most
effective national policy is often the one a city has already demonstrated.

## Cities that moved fast

### The counter-case: cities that moved fast

Some cities have made substantial progress, and the conditions are instructive.

**Ahmedabad** adopted a heat action plan within three years of a mass-casualty heatwave, and the plan was evaluated and found effective (@hess2018). The conditions: a mayor who prioritised it, a health department with data, and an NGO partner with technical capacity.

**Medellín** built green corridors along roads and waterways, reducing surface temperatures in the intervention areas. The project was cheap relative to infrastructure, visible to residents, and politically popular.

**Rotterdam** built water plazas and shifted from defending against water to accommodating it, with a communication strategy that made the change legible to residents.

The common conditions are political leadership, a technical partner, and a measure that produces a visible benefit in normal times [@hess2018; @bubeck2017].

### The maintenance budget, in one number

A city's commitment to adaptation can be read from a single ratio: maintenance spending as a share of
the asset value it maintains.

The ratio is not usually published, and it can be estimated from budget documents. A city that spends
less than roughly one per cent of asset value a year on maintenance is, over a few decades, consuming its infrastructure [@undrr2022].

The climate significance is that the consumption is not visible until a storm. A drainage system that has
been under-maintained for twenty years looks adequate on a map and fails in the first intense rainfall.

The metric is crude, and it is more informative than most climate plans.

## The city that adapts by accident

### The city that adapts by accident

A qualification: some cities adapt effectively without a climate plan, and the reason is worth understanding.

A city that maintains its drainage because it floods every year, that enforces its building code because
the last earthquake was remembered, and that keeps its public spaces because they are used — is adapting,
without a strategy document.

The adaptation is embedded in routines and in institutional memory, and it is fragile in a specific way: it
depends on the memory surviving. A generation that did not experience the flood may not maintain the drain.

The implication for policy is that codifying the routine — the maintenance schedule, the building standard,
the drill — is a way of transmitting the memory to a generation that does not have it. A plan is a memory
aid, and its value is in the institutionalisation, not the document.

### Measuring a city's capacity in a week

A practical diagnostic, for a reader who wants to assess a city rather than read a plan.

**Day 1.** Ask for the drainage maintenance budget for the last five years. If it is not available, that
is the answer.

**Day 2.** Ask which development applications were refused on hazard grounds in the last three years. A
city that has refused none has not integrated risk.

**Day 3.** Visit a shelter. Ask who opens it, who staffs it, and whether it accepts animals.

**Day 4.** Ask for the list of vulnerable residents held by the health service. If there is no list, the
heat plan does not exist.

**Day 5.** Ask a neighbourhood organisation what it would do in the first six hours. The answer is the
city's real capacity.

The diagnostic does not require expertise and it produces a better picture than any planning document.

### The city's own footprint

A city is not only a place exposed to climate; it is a cause of exposure downstream.

Three mechanisms.

**Impervious surface.** Paving a catchment increases runoff, which raises the flood peak downstream. A city
that floods its downstream neighbour is doing so partly because of its own construction.

**Groundwater extraction.** A city that pumps its aquifer lowers the water table across a wider area and
contributes to subsidence beyond its boundary.

**Waste and drainage.** A city that discharges untreated water into a river transfers a health risk
downstream.

The governance implication is that a city's adaptation plan should account for its downstream effects,
which requires a catchment authority with jurisdiction beyond the city boundary. Few cities have one, and
the consequence is that upstream decisions are made without regard to downstream costs.

## The city as a collection of routines

### The measure a city can take in a year

A realistic programme for a city that wants to begin, in four steps and twelve months.

**Quarter 1 — count.** Establish a mortality baseline for heat and flood, and a register of vulnerable
residents. This requires data sharing between the health service and the municipality, which is the hardest
part.

**Quarter 2 — open.** Identify buildings that can be opened in a heat event, agree the protocol for opening
them, and test one.

**Quarter 3 — clear.** Survey the drainage system and clear the blockages. Publish what was found.

**Quarter 4 — drill.** Run a tabletop exercise for a flood and a heat event, with the agencies and the
community organisations, and publish the lessons.

None of these requires a capital budget. All of them require a decision and a person responsible.

### The city as a collection of routines

An observation on what a city is, for the purposes of adaptation.

A city is not a plan, a budget, or a set of assets. It is a collection of routines: the maintenance schedule,
the permit review, the school calendar, the emergency drill, the budget cycle.

Adaptation succeeds when it changes the routines, and fails when it produces a document that sits beside
them. A climate plan that does not alter the maintenance schedule, the permit review, or the budget cycle
has not changed the city.

This is why the most effective interventions in this chapter are procedural: a register, a trigger, an
annual drill, a line in the budget. They are small, they are boring, and they persist after the political
attention has moved on.

### The measure that a city can take without money

A list for a city with no capital budget.

**Publish the hazard.** A map on a website, produced from existing data, changes what people do.

**Open the buildings.** A decision to open schools and libraries in a heat event costs staff time and
little else.

**Share the register.** An agreement between the health service and the municipality costs a memorandum.

**Clear the drains.** A labour-intensive programme that uses existing staff.

**Drill.** A tabletop exercise costs a meeting room and a morning.

None of these requires capital, and each of them changes the outcome of the next event. The reason they are
not done is not money; it is that no one is responsible, which is the argument of the whole book.

## The city and the basin

### The city and the basin

A scale observation.

A city sits inside a basin, and its water decisions have effects beyond its boundary in both directions.
Water that the city does not store floods downstream; water that the city abstracts reduces the flow
downstream; water that the city discharges contaminates it.

The governance implication is that a city's adaptation plan should be a basin plan, negotiated with the
authorities upstream and downstream. Few cities do this, and the ones that do — in the Netherlands, in the
Rhine basin — have institutions that cross the boundary.

The lesson is that the unit of water adaptation is the catchment, and the unit of political decision is the
municipality. The mismatch is the problem, and the solution is an institution at the scale of the catchment.

### The city that is a set of contracts

An observation about how cities actually change.

A city does not adapt through a plan. It adapts through contracts: a maintenance contract, a service
agreement, a concession, a lease, a procurement. Each contract specifies what someone is obliged to do, and
the obligations are what the city actually is.

A resilience strategy that is not reflected in a contract is a document. A clause in a concession that
requires the operator to restore service within a stated time is an adaptation, even if no strategy
mentions it.

The practical implication is that the place to look for a city's real adaptation is not its strategy
document but its contracts, and the place to change it is the next procurement.

**What would prove this wrong.** A city whose strategy document changed behaviour without any change in
contracts or budgets: a mayor who read it and acted, an agency that reorganised because of it. Such cases
exist and they are the exception that shows how much usually depends on the contract.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter is a set of tests, not an argument, so the falsification question is different: what would show that the tests are wrong?

The first test is calibration. If the claims that survive the seven questions were no more accurate, on average, than the claims that fail them, the tests would be worthless. That is measurable: take a sample of climate claims, apply the tests, then score the claims against what later happened. The forecasting literature suggests that structured reasoning improves accuracy modestly and reliably, but the effect is small enough that a null result in a climate-specific sample would count against this chapter [@tetlock2015].

The second test is usability. If a reader with no climate training cannot apply tests one, two, and four to a newspaper article after reading this chapter, the tests fail as a reference tool regardless of their validity. That is testable with a small trial: give the chapter to two groups, give the article to both, and compare the quality of their written assessments.

The third test is the book's own. A reader who applies the seven questions to this book should find at least one place where the book fails its own standard. If the book passes all seven cleanly, the tests are too weak — a set of questions that never bites is decoration. The weakest point is the falsification sections of the earlier chapters: several of them state a direction without a threshold, and a reader is entitled to mark that down [@gaillard2019].
