# Communities and Cities: Capacity Built Before the Rain

::: lead
The most useful thing a city can do about climate risk is rarely the thing that gets a ribbon cutting. It is the unglamorous, distributed work of making the ordinary systems — drainage, shade, transport, shelters, social services — work on the worst day of the decade.
:::

## What adaptive capacity actually is

Adaptive capacity is the ability of a system to adjust to a shock without losing its essential functions. It is not the same as resilience, which is the ability to absorb a shock and return to the previous state. Adaptive capacity includes the ability to change the state.

For a city, the components are concrete.

**Fiscal capacity.** Can the city pay for maintenance, not just construction? A drainage system that is not maintained is a drainage system that will fail, and maintenance budgets are the first to be cut.

**Technical capacity.** Does the city have engineers, planners, and public health staff who understand the risks? This is a hiring and retention problem, and it is severe in smaller cities.

**Institutional capacity.** Can departments coordinate? Most climate risks cross departmental boundaries, and most city governments are organised along those boundaries.

**Social capacity.** Do residents trust the government, and do they have organisations that can act? This is the social capital of Chapter 10, at city scale.

The four are not interchangeable. A city with money and no technical staff will build the wrong thing. A city with good staff and no money will plan well and implement nothing.

## The city that moved early: an example worth copying

Among the cities that have done the unglamorous work, one case is worth setting out because it is
replicable rather than heroic.

**Ahmedabad** adopted a heat action plan in 2013, three years after a heat wave killed more than a
thousand people. The plan combined a forecast-triggered alert, a register of vulnerable residents,
changes to outdoor working hours, and free drinking water at public places. An evaluation found lower
mortality in a subsequent heat wave than the temperature alone would have predicted [@hess2018].

The replicable features are unremarkable.

**A lead agency.** The health department, not the environment department, because heat is a health
outcome.

**A partner with technical capacity.** An NGO provided the epidemiological analysis that the municipality
could not.

**A trigger tied to a forecast.** The plan activates on a temperature threshold, not on a political
decision.

**A register.** Names and addresses of the people most at risk, held by the municipality and the health
service.

None of these requires a large budget. All of them require a decision, which is why they are not
universal.

## Shelters that get used

A shelter that nobody uses is not a shelter. The difference between a shelter that is used and one that is not is rarely the building.

**Distance.** People will not travel far in an emergency, especially with children or with limited mobility. Shelters need to be within walking distance of the population they serve.

**Trust.** A school or a mosque that people already know is more likely to be used than a purpose-built facility they have never entered.

**Acceptance of pets and livestock.** In many communities, people will not evacuate without animals, and a shelter that excludes them will be empty. This is not sentimentality; livestock is a household's savings.

**Dignity and privacy.** Shelters that cannot provide basic privacy for women and girls are shelters that women will not use. This is one of the most consistent findings in the evacuation literature, and one of the most commonly ignored.

**A known destination.** People evacuate to places they know. A shelter that has been used for a community event is a known destination.

## Land use is the real climate decision

Most cities have a climate adaptation plan and a land-use plan, and the land-use plan determines the outcome.

Where new development goes determines exposure for the next century. Whether a floodplain is zoned for storage or for housing is a decision that no drainage investment can reverse. Whether the city extends into a wildfire interface is a decision made in a planning committee, not in a climate office.

The practical problem is that land-use decisions are made incrementally, parcel by parcel, under pressure from developers and landowners, and the cumulative effect is rarely assessed. A city can approve every individual development on its merits and end up with a pattern that is catastrophic in aggregate.

The intervention that works is not a better plan document. It is a decision rule: a line beyond which development is not permitted, a density requirement in safe areas, and a consistent refusal that developers can rely on.

## The school as infrastructure

A school is the most multipurpose building a city owns, and it is systematically underused in disaster
planning.

It has: a large roof, a kitchen, toilets, water storage, a hall, a known location, and a population that
already knows how to find it. In a heat wave it is a cooling centre. In a flood it is a shelter. In an
emergency it is a distribution point. In a drought it is a water collection surface.

The reasons it is not used are administrative. The education department controls it, the disaster agency
needs it, and no one has authority to open it. The building is empty and locked while people are
sheltering under a bridge.

The fix is a protocol, agreed in advance: who opens the school, who staffs it, who pays for the
electricity and the cleaning, and who is liable. These are boring questions, and they are the reason the
building stays locked.

## Open space as infrastructure

The cheapest flood defence in many cities is the space that is already open.

A park that floods is a park that is doing its job. A schoolyard that becomes a basin is a schoolyard that protects the neighbourhood. A wetland that is preserved is a wetland that stores water and reduces the peak downstream.

The design principle is **multifunctionality**: land that serves a normal function most of the year and a hazard function in the event. This is cheaper than single-purpose infrastructure and it is more politically achievable, because it produces a visible amenity.

The barrier is institutional. A park is managed by the parks department, a drainage basin by the water utility, and a schoolyard by the education department. Multifunctional land requires someone to be responsible for the function that crosses departmental lines.

## Heat and the shape of the city

For heat, the city-scale interventions are well established and cheap relative to engineering.

**Tree cover.** Shade reduces surface temperature and pedestrian heat exposure. The evidence on the magnitude varies by species, location, and irrigation, but the direction is consistent: more canopy, less heat (@ipcc2022a).

**Cool surfaces.** Light-coloured roofs and pavements reflect more solar radiation. The effect is real and smaller than tree cover.

**Ventilation corridors.** Street orientation and building height determine whether air moves through the city. Cities that have built dense, low-permeability blocks trap heat.

**Cooling access.** Public buildings that are open and free during heat events. The barrier is not the building; it is the decision to keep it open, staffed, and welcoming.

## The one decision that matters most: where to allow building

If a city could make only one decision about climate risk, it should be this one.

Where new construction is permitted determines exposure for the life of the buildings, which is fifty to
a hundred years. No drainage scheme, warning system, or emergency plan can compensate for a city that
has built in the wrong place.

The decision is made through zoning, permits, and infrastructure extension, and it is usually made
incrementally, parcel by parcel, by officials who are not thinking about climate.

Three practical mechanisms have worked.

**A hazard line with legal force.** Not a map in a plan, but a rule that prohibits construction beyond a
line, with a defined review process to move the line as the evidence changes.

**Conditional permits.** Construction permitted only if the developer demonstrates that the building will
function at the design hazard, which transfers the cost to the party creating the exposure.

**Infrastructure sequencing.** Not extending water, drainage, and roads into areas that should not be
built, which is the quietest and most effective form of land-use control.

The third is the most powerful and the least discussed. A city that does not run pipes to a floodplain
does not need to prohibit building there.

## The budget test

A simple test distinguishes cities that are adapting from cities that are writing plans.

**Look at the maintenance budget.** Is it funded at the level required to keep drainage, water, and road systems functioning? A city that underfunds maintenance is not adapting, regardless of its plan.

**Look at the capital budget.** What share goes to distributed, multifunctional, low-regret measures rather than to a single large structure?

**Look at the land-use decisions.** What has been refused? A city that has never refused a development on hazard grounds has not integrated risk into planning.

**Look at who is at the table.** Are public health, social services, and community organisations involved, or only engineering and planning?

::: {.box title="Box 16.1 — Five questions for a city climate plan" kind="note"}
1. **What is the specific hazard, and where?** A plan that lists all hazards for the whole city has not made a decision.
2. **What is funded?** Distinguish the measures with a budget line from the aspirations.
3. **What is refused?** The land-use rules are the plan.
4. **Who maintains it?** A measure without a maintenance budget is a measure with an expiry date.
5. **Who is not protected?** The distribution of protection is the political content of the plan.
:::

## The counter-case: cities that moved fast

Some cities have made substantial progress, and the conditions are instructive.

**Ahmedabad** adopted a heat action plan within three years of a mass-casualty heatwave, and the plan was evaluated and found effective (@hess2018). The conditions: a mayor who prioritised it, a health department with data, and an NGO partner with technical capacity.

**Medellín** built green corridors along roads and waterways, reducing surface temperatures in the intervention areas. The project was cheap relative to infrastructure, visible to residents, and politically popular.

**Rotterdam** built water plazas and shifted from defending against water to accommodating it, with a communication strategy that made the change legible to residents.

The common conditions are political leadership, a technical partner, and a measure that produces a visible benefit in normal times.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that city adaptive capacity is determined by maintenance funding, land-use rules, and coordination rather than by the size of a climate plan or a single infrastructure project.

It would be wrong if cities with large climate plans and weak maintenance consistently outperformed cities with modest plans and strong maintenance in actual events. The evidence on drainage maintenance and land-use enforcement points the other way.
