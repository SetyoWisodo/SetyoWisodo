# Justice: Who Emits, Who Absorbs

::: lead
There are two inequalities in the climate crisis and they are usually conflated. One is about who caused it. The other is about who pays for it. A policy can be just on the first and unjust on the second, and most are.
:::

## Two distributions, not one

### Two distributions, not one

The first distribution is historical responsibility. Cumulative emissions since the industrial revolution are concentrated in a small number of countries: the United States and the European Union together account for the largest share of the total since 1850, while the countries most exposed to climate harm — small island states, Sahelian countries, parts of South Asia — account for a fraction of it [@matthews2015; @ipcc2021].

The accounting is sensitive to where it starts. Matthews' calculation of national carbon debt depends on the start date chosen and on whether emissions are counted where fuel is burned or where it is extracted, and the choice moves a country's position by decades [@matthews2015]. The distribution is therefore a family of distributions, not one fact — which is the point of the next section.

The second distribution is current harm. The people dying in heatwaves, losing harvests to drought, and abandoning coastlines are disproportionately in countries with low emissions and low capacity to adapt.

These two distributions are not the same, and the difference matters for policy. A country can be a large emitter and highly vulnerable — India is both, and so is Indonesia in some respects. A country can be a small emitter and moderately resilient — Singapore. The categories overlap without coinciding.

Most of the political argument about climate justice conflates the two, which is why it produces more heat than light.

### Responsibility: the accounting problem

Assigning responsibility for emissions requires a decision about the accounting basis, and each basis produces a different answer.

**Territorial emissions** [@matthews2015] count what is burned within a country's borders. This is the basis used in the UN Framework Convention and in most national inventories. It assigns responsibility to the country where the factory is.

**Consumption-based emissions** count what is consumed within a country, wherever it was produced. This shifts responsibility for manufacturing to the importing country, which changes the picture substantially: the EU's consumption-based emissions are higher than its territorial ones, and China's are lower.

**Cumulative emissions** count the historical stock, which is what determines temperature. This is the basis most favourable to the argument that rich countries owe a debt, because they emitted first and most.

**Per capita emissions** divide by population, which changes the ranking again: the United States remains high, but India falls to near the bottom.

There is no neutral choice among these. Each basis embeds a moral claim about what counts. The honest position is to state the basis, which most political argument does not do (@matthews2015).

## The Carbon Majors accounting

The most concrete attempt to assign responsibility to firms rather than states is the Carbon Majors
research, which traces cumulative emissions to the entities — investor-owned companies, state-owned
companies, and states — that extracted the fossil carbon [@heede2014].

The headline result is that a small number of entities account for a large share of cumulative
industrial emissions since the mid-nineteenth century, and that a large majority of those entities are
still operating [@heede2014]. Heede's trace begins in 1854 and ends in 2010, which matters for how the
result should be read: it is a historical accounting of extraction, not a current emissions inventory.

The accounting is contested, and the contest is instructive.

**Attribution to extraction versus combustion.** An oil company extracts the fuel; the buyer burns it.
Assigning the emissions to the extractor is a moral claim about who caused the demand, not a physical
fact.

**The period covered.** Emissions before a certain date are excluded in some analyses on the grounds
that the science was not known. The choice of date changes the shares substantially.

**State-owned versus investor-owned.** A state company's emissions are also the state's emissions, which
double-counts if the state is also counted in the national inventory.

None of this makes the accounting invalid. It makes it an argument with a defensible basis, which should
be stated — the same rule that applies to the state-level accounting above.

## Adaptation finance, and the gap that defines it

### Adaptation finance, and the gap that defines it

The commitment by developed countries to mobilise US$100 billion a year in climate finance was met late,
and the definition of what counts is elastic.

Three problems recur.

**Loans count as finance.** A large share of what is reported as climate finance is concessional or
non-concessional lending, which adds to the recipient's debt. A grant and a loan are not the same
instrument, and reporting them together overstates the transfer.

**Mitigation dominates.** The balance between mitigation and adaptation finance has consistently
favoured mitigation, despite adaptation being the priority for the most vulnerable. Adaptation finance
is harder to measure and easier to double-count.

**The need estimate is larger than the commitment.** The UNEP Adaptation Gap Report has consistently
found that the finance flowing is five to ten times below the estimated need, and the gap is growing
as the hazard intensifies.

The consequence for this chapter's argument is that the justice claim has been accepted in principle and
not in practice. That is a familiar pattern in international environmental politics, and it is the
reason the loss and damage fund's operational rules matter more than its founding declaration.

### Loss and damage: the test case

The loss and damage fund agreed at COP27 is the clearest test of whether the justice argument produces action.

The agreement established a fund. It did not establish who pays, how much, or who is eligible. Those questions were deferred, and the deferral has been long (@unfccc2022).

The underlying disagreement is about the nature of the obligation. If climate harm is a liability, then the obligation is legal and the amounts should be calculated from damage. If it is a form of solidarity, the obligation is voluntary and the amounts are whatever is politically possible. The fund was designed in the second register while being argued in the first.

The result is a mechanism that exists, is undercapitalised, and has been slow to disburse. For a small island state facing a seawall it cannot afford, the distinction between liability and solidarity is not academic.

## Justice inside countries

### Justice inside countries

The international argument receives most of the attention, and the domestic argument matters more for most people.

Within every country, exposure and vulnerability are distributed unequally. The people who die in heatwaves are poor and old. The people who lose their homes to floods live on the cheaper land. The people who cannot evacuate do not own cars.

This is not only an outcome of the market. It is produced by policy: where infrastructure is built, which neighbourhoods are protected, who receives compensation, and which claims are believed.

The pattern in post-disaster compensation is consistent across countries: payments flow to those who can prove ownership, which means those with formal title, which means those who are already better off. Renters, informal dwellers, and people without documentation — who are often the majority of the affected — receive less or nothing (@undrr2022).

A climate policy that is just internationally and unjust domestically will fail, for two reasons. It will not reduce vulnerability where vulnerability is concentrated, and it will lose political support from the people it excludes.

### The domestic version: who is compensated

The international argument is about states; the domestic argument is about households, and it is where
most people experience climate injustice.

Three mechanisms distribute harm and compensation within a country.

**Land and housing.** Exposure follows the price gradient: the cheapest land is the most dangerous.
A household on a floodplain is usually there because the alternative was unaffordable, not because it
preferred the view.

**Compensation rules.** Post-disaster payments typically require title, a bank account, and an address.
Renters, informal dwellers, and people without documentation are excluded from compensation for a loss
they fully experienced.

**Recovery speed.** Wealthier households have savings, insurance, and political access. They rebuild
faster, and the gap in recovery is often larger than the gap in damage.

The consequence is that a disaster produces a redistribution from poor to rich, not through any
deliberate policy but through the ordinary operation of markets and administration. The redistribution
is usually invisible in the aggregate figures, which is why it persists.

The remedy is administrative and specific: presumptive registration before the event, community-based
verification, and payment mechanisms that do not require formal title.

### What justice is not

The word is used loosely, and it is worth distinguishing the argument here from two things it is often
confused with.

**It is not the claim that rich countries caused everything.** Historical responsibility is real, and it
does not explain the whole of any particular disaster. A flood in a poorly governed city is not caused by
emissions in the way that a heat wave is.

**It is not the claim that adaptation should be redistributed from rich to poor within countries.** The
argument is narrower: that the distribution of protection should follow the distribution of risk, which
it currently does not.

The practical test of a just policy is not its rhetoric but its allocation: who is protected first, who
is compensated first, and who is consulted before the decision is made.

## The case against, and why unjust policy fails

### The case against the argument, stated by its opponents

A chapter on justice that does not engage the economic case against its own policy conclusions is
propaganda. The case is worth stating in its strongest form.

Tol's survey of the economic literature estimates the welfare cost of climate change at a small
fraction of global income, and finds that the estimates have not risen over time as the science has
sharpened [@tol2018]. If that is right, then the moral urgency of large transfers rests on distribution
and on low-probability high-impact tails rather than on aggregate damage, and the case for the transfers
has to be made in those terms.

The second objection is about incentive. A framework that assigns liability for historical emissions
asks countries to accept obligations for acts that were legal when they were done and that produced the
industrial base everyone now uses. The objection is not that the history is disputed; it is that a
liability rule with no statute of limitations is a rule no future emitter would accept, and that a rule
nobody accepts changes nothing.

The third objection is empirical: the record of large international transfers is not encouraging. The
gap between what was promised and what arrived at the Adaptation Fund and the Green Climate Fund is the
evidence, and it cuts both ways — it shows the need is real, and it shows the instrument is weak
[@unfccc2022].

None of these refutes the argument of this chapter, which is narrower: that two inequalities are
distinct, and that domestic allocation determines whether adaptation works. But a reader who wants to
disagree with the chapter should now know where to start, and the chapter's own position is that the
burden-of-proof lies with whoever proposes a transfer, not with whoever doubts it.

### Why unjust policy fails on its own terms

There is a pragmatic argument for justice that does not depend on moral commitment.

Adaptation requires public cooperation. A heat plan requires people to answer the phone and open the door. An evacuation requires people to leave their homes and trust the destination. A relocation requires people to consent. None of these are achieved by a policy that the affected population experiences as unfair.

The empirical record supports this. Flood defences that protect one neighbourhood and not another generate resistance to the whole programme. Compensation schemes that exclude renters generate the perception of corruption, which reduces compliance with everything else.

Justice is not a constraint on effective policy. It is a condition of it.

The same country can be a hero or a villain depending on the accounting basis.

**Territorial** — emissions produced inside the border. Favours countries that import manufactured goods.

**Consumption-based** — emissions embodied in what is consumed. Favours countries that export manufactured goods.

**Cumulative** — the historical stock. Favours countries that industrialised late.

**Per capita** — divided by population. Favours countries with large populations.

Any claim that a country is "responsible for X per cent" of the problem is meaningless until the basis is named. When you see such a claim, look for the basis before the number.

## The counter-case, and what it produced

### Why the polluter-pays principle is hard to apply

The principle is simple and its application is not, and the difficulty is worth understanding because it
explains why climate finance is slow.

**Attribution.** Who emitted what, over what period, and on what basis? Every answer requires a choice
that someone will dispute.

**Causation.** Emissions are cumulative and dispersed. A tonne emitted in 1970 and a tonne emitted in
2020 have different effects on the temperature, and the difference depends on the lifetime of the gas.

**Capacity to pay.** A principle based on historical emissions would assign obligations to countries that
are now poor and to countries that are now rich but were not when the emissions occurred.

**Sovereignty.** A state cannot be compelled to pay without consent, and consent is what is being
negotiated.

The result is a regime that operates on the basis of voluntary pledges rather than assessed obligations.
That is not a failure of the principle; it is a feature of an international system without an enforcement
mechanism.

### The counter-case: when the argument produced money

The justice argument is not powerless. The Adaptation Fund, established in 2001, has disbursed several hundred million dollars to adaptation projects in developing countries, funded by a levy on clean development mechanism projects. It is small relative to need and it is real.

The Green Climate Fund, established in 2010, has committed billions, with a mandate to balance mitigation and adaptation and to prioritise the most vulnerable.

These are not trivial. They are also several times below the estimated need, and the gap is the argument [@unfccc2022; @ipcc2022a].

### The adaptation that is not funded because it is not visible

One more mechanism of injustice is administrative, and it is rarely named.

Adaptation spending is allocated to projects: a seawall, a drainage scheme, an early warning system,
a desalination plant. The projects that can be written as projects get funded. The interventions that
work through existing institutions — a health clinic's heat protocol, a school's evacuation drill, a
municipality's register of vulnerable residents — are harder to procure and therefore harder to fund.

The result is a bias toward capital projects and against institutional capacity, which is the bias
identified in Chapter 9 and which has a distributional consequence. Capital projects protect assets;
institutional capacity protects people. In places where the exposed population has few assets, the
capital bias systematically underinvests in what would save them.

This is a justice argument that does not require a claim about historical emissions. It requires only
the observation that the funding rules favour the interventions that protect property, and that the
people most at risk often have little property.

## The colonial accounting

### The colonial accounting

A complete account of the two distributions has to include a history that the standard emissions
accounting omits.

Many of the countries with the lowest cumulative emissions were, within living memory, the sites of
extraction organised for the benefit of the countries with the highest. The infrastructure that would
have reduced their vulnerability — drainage, water supply, housing standards — was not built, in part
because the colonial economy had no use for it.

This is not a claim that colonial history explains a particular flood. It is a claim about the initial
distribution of adaptive capacity, which is itself a form of inherited disadvantage.

The accounting difficulty is that the harm is not attributable to a quantity that can be measured in
tonnes. It appears instead as a difference in the stock of infrastructure, institutions, and fiscal
space — which is exactly the difference that determines how many people die when the same hazard
arrives.

### The country that is both

The two-inequality framing is clearest in countries that are simultaneously large emitters and highly
vulnerable, and Indonesia is one of them.

Indonesia is among the largest emitters in the world when land-use change is counted, and it is among the
most exposed to sea-level rise, heat, and changing rainfall [@ipcc2022a]. It has a per-capita emissions level well below
the rich countries and a cumulative contribution that is small relative to the industrialised world.

Three positions follow, and they are not consistent with each other.

**As a vulnerable country**, Indonesia is a claimant: it has a moral case for adaptation finance and for
loss and damage.

**As an emitter**, it is a contributor: it has obligations to reduce, particularly on land use.

**As a middle-income country**, it is neither the poorest nor the richest, and it is excluded from many
concessional instruments by income thresholds that do not reflect its exposure.

The case shows why the simple rich-poor framing fails. Most of the world's vulnerable people live in
countries that are also emitters, and the policy that serves them is not a transfer from a rich bloc to a
poor one but a set of domestic decisions about land use, energy, and protection — supported, where
relevant, by international finance.

## What a just programme looks like

### What a just adaptation programme looks like in practice

The argument would be abstract without a description of the thing it recommends, so here is one.

**A register, not a target.** Identify the people at risk by name and address, not by category. A
programme that targets "vulnerable groups" reaches no one; a programme with a list reaches the people on
it.

**Protection in proportion to risk.** The neighbourhoods with the highest exposure get the first
investment, not the ones with the loudest council representation.

**Conditions on support.** Compensation after an event supports a move or a rebuild to standard, not a
rebuild in place.

**Consultation before the decision.** The people affected are consulted on the design of the intervention,
not informed of it after the budget is committed.

**Reporting on distribution.** The programme publishes who received what, which is the only way to detect
capture.

None of these is radical. All of them run against the ordinary incentives of a bureaucracy, which rewards
visible projects, measurable outputs, and political quiet.

### The adaptation that protects the wrong thing

A distributional observation that follows from the accounting above.

Adaptation finance is measured in dollars, and dollars buy assets. A project that protects a port, a road,
or a power station is easy to appraise, easy to finance, and easy to report. A project that protects
people — a heat plan, a register, a clinic protocol — is harder on all three counts.

The result is a systematic bias in what counts as adaptation, and the bias has a distributional
consequence: the assets that get protected are the ones that have owners, and the people who get
protected are the ones who have advocates.

This is not a conspiracy. It is the ordinary operation of an appraisal system that values what can be
counted. The correction is not to stop protecting assets but to build appraisal methods that can count
what a heat plan prevents — which requires, at minimum, a mortality baseline and a register, which is to
say the interventions that are hardest to fund are also the ones that generate the evidence for their own
funding.

## Who pays, who is counted late, and who is not counted

### The cost of being counted late

A further mechanism, which connects this chapter to the accounting in Chapter 8.

The distribution of harm is measured after the event, and the measurement determines the compensation. A
household whose loss is not counted is a household that is not compensated, and the counting is done by
instruments that favour the legible.

Three biases recur.

**The property bias.** Losses are counted as damage to assets. A tenant who loses everything counts for
nothing.

**The formal-sector bias.** Lost wages are counted; lost informal income is not.

**The urban bias.** Assessments reach the accessible and the mapped. A remote village is undercounted.

The consequence is that the official loss figure is a distribution as well as a total, and the distribution
is skewed toward those who had more to lose and better records.

### The adaptation that is paid for by the wrong budget

A fiscal mechanism that produces injustice without anyone intending it.

Adaptation spending is often drawn from a budget that also funds services for the poor. A government that
reallocates health or education spending to build a sea wall has protected assets and reduced services, and
the people who lose the services are the people who were most vulnerable.

The mechanism is invisible in a climate finance report, which records the adaptation spending and not the
reallocation.

The remedy is a rule that adaptation spending must be additional, not reallocated, and a reporting
requirement that tracks the source of the funds. Both are unglamorous and both are rarely implemented.

### The instrument that is not a transfer

One proposal recurs in the justice literature and deserves a short account: the climate debt mechanism.

The idea is that a country with a historical emissions debt issues a claim that is recognised in
international law, and the claim is settled through a schedule of payments or of investments.

The objections are legal and practical.

**Sovereign immunity.** A state cannot be compelled to pay a debt it does not acknowledge.

**The basis of the claim.** Each accounting basis produces a different number, and the choice of basis is
itself contested.

**Moral hazard.** A mechanism that pays for harm may reduce the incentive to avoid it.

The alternatives in practice are voluntary pledges, which are insufficient, and litigation, which is
slow and uncertain. The current system is a hybrid, and its performance is what the loss and damage fund
demonstrates.

## The jurisdiction that is not a state

### The jurisdiction that is not a state

A legal development worth recording: the climate litigation that has succeeded, and why.

Cases in the Netherlands, Germany, and elsewhere have established that a government's climate policy can be
reviewed by a court against a standard of care, and that a failure to act can be unlawful.

The basis of the successful cases is not a specific emissions target. It is a general duty of care, derived
from constitutional rights, human rights law, or tort.

The significance for this chapter is that the courts have become a route for the justice argument when the
legislative route is blocked, and that the route is slow and uncertain and sometimes effective.

The limitation is that litigation produces a judgment, not a budget. A government ordered to act must still
find the money and the political coalition.

### The adaptation that the market will not provide

An economic observation, which follows from the public-good structure of adaptation.

Adaptation has the structure of a public good in many of its most important forms: a flood defence protects
everyone behind it, a warning system serves everyone who can receive it, a drainage system protects a
catchment.

Public goods are underprovided by markets, which is why adaptation is a public expenditure problem. The
corollary, which is less often stated, is that the level of adaptation a society gets is determined by its
tax capacity and its willingness to spend.

That is a distributional fact. A poor country with weak tax capacity will adapt less, at the same level of
need, and the difference is not attributable to the severity of its hazard. It is attributable to the
fiscal space it has, which is the subject of Chapter 8 and the argument for international finance.

### The measure that is a complaint

A small institutional detail with a large effect: the grievance mechanism.

A household that believes it has been excluded from compensation, or that a payment was captured, needs a
route to complain that is not the same as the process that excluded it.

Three designs have worked.

**An independent panel** with the authority to reverse a decision.

**A public register** of who received what, which allows neighbours to see the distribution.

**A community liaison** from the affected population, paid to represent it.

The mechanism matters because capture is invisible without it. A distribution that follows existing
hierarchies looks normal to everyone inside the hierarchy, and the grievance is the only signal that
something is wrong.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that the two climate inequalities are distinct, that the accounting basis determines the political conclusion, and that domestic injustice undermines adaptation effectiveness.

The first claim is falsified by showing that the two distributions coincide: that the countries with the largest cumulative emissions are also, in rank order, the countries with the highest climate-attributable harm per capita. They do not coincide at the top, but a demonstration that the rank correlation exceeds 0.9 across the whole distribution would collapse the distinction.

The second claim is arithmetic: change the accounting basis and the ranking of obligations changes. The book's own box does the arithmetic; a basis that left the ranking unchanged across every plausible choice would refute the claim [@matthews2015; @heede2014].

The third claim is the one most likely to be wrong, and its falsifier is a comparison of programmes. If adaptation spending distributed regressively — concentrated on the better-off — produced the same reduction in vulnerability as an equal sum distributed progressively, then equity would have no independent effect on effectiveness. The evidence is indirect, drawn mostly from settings other than climate adaptation, and the chapter should be read as arguing a mechanism rather than reporting a measured effect [@tol2018].
