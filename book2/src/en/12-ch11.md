# Knowledge: Forecasts That Do Not Arrive

::: lead
The forecast was correct. That is what makes the Ahr valley failure worth studying: the science worked, the models worked, the meteorologists worked — and 134 people died in a valley where the water arrived at night with no order to leave.
:::

## The Ahr valley, July 2021

In mid-July 2021, a slow-moving low-pressure system stalled over western Europe and produced rainfall that exceeded the local record by a wide margin. The European Flood Awareness System issued warnings days in advance. The German weather service issued warnings. The forecast was, by any technical standard, a success (@kreienkamp2021).

In the Ahr valley, a narrow river valley in Rhineland-Palatinate with villages built close to the water, the flood arrived at night. More than 130 people died in Germany and Belgium. Houses were destroyed, roads disappeared, and the valley's warning system — sirens in some places, mobile alerts in others — reached some residents and not others.

The subsequent investigations identified the failure precisely: the information existed, but it did not become an instruction. Residents received weather warnings, not evacuation orders. Many did not know what a "severe weather warning" meant for their particular street. Some had experienced floods before and believed they knew what to expect. The water came higher than any local memory (@kreienkamp2021).

This is not a story about ignorance. It is a story about the gap between a forecast and a decision.

## The four links of a warning

A warning system has four links, and it fails if any one of them fails.

**Detection.** Instruments and models produce a prediction. This is the link that gets the funding and the scientific attention.

**Delivery.** The prediction reaches the people at risk. Delivery is a function of communications infrastructure, but also of whether people are reachable — a phone that is charged is different from a phone that has a signal.

**Understanding.** The message is comprehended. This requires the message to be in the right language, at the right level of technical detail, and framed in terms of what the recipient should do. "100 mm of rain expected" is a measurement. "Leave the valley before 18:00" is an instruction.

**Action.** The recipient is able to act. This requires transport, a destination, the ability to leave work, and — for many people — the belief that leaving is worth the cost.

Most investment goes to link one. Most failures occur at links three and four (@wmo2022).

## Message design: what the evidence says

The research on warning message design is consistent on several points.

**Specific instructions outperform descriptions.** "Evacuate to the school on the hill" produces more action than "flooding is expected."

**Repetition and multiple channels.** A message received once, on one channel, is less likely to be acted on than the same message received repeatedly through several channels, including one trusted person.

**Trust matters more than accuracy.** People act on warnings from sources they trust. A technically perfect warning from an unknown agency may be ignored, while an imperfect warning from a trusted local figure may be acted on.

**Fear alone does not work.** Messages that induce fear without providing a clear action produce paralysis or denial rather than evacuation (@wmo2022).

**Language and literacy.** In Indonesia, warnings must reach communities speaking hundreds of local languages, with varying literacy. Pictograms, flags, drums, and mosque loudspeakers are not primitive fallbacks; they are appropriate technology.

::: {.box title="Box 11.1 — Testing a warning" kind="note"}
A warning system can be tested before it is needed. Five questions.

1. **Coverage.** What share of the at-risk population receives the message within the required time? Measure this, do not assume it.
2. **Comprehension.** Can a random sample of residents state what they are supposed to do? Test it.
3. **Action capacity.** Do the people who understood the message have the means to act? A warning to evacuate is meaningless for a household with no vehicle and no accessible shelter.
4. **False alarm tolerance.** What happens to trust after a false alarm? Systems that never issue false alarms are systems that wait too long.
5. **Last mile.** Who knocks on the door of the person with no phone? If the answer is "nobody", the system does not reach the people most likely to die.
:::

## Bangladesh: the warning that became routine

The contrast with Bangladesh is instructive, because Bangladesh's system succeeds at exactly the links where Ahr failed.

The Cyclone Preparedness Programme operates through 76,000 volunteers in coastal villages. Warnings are transmitted through multiple channels — sirens, megaphones, mosque announcements, radio, and volunteers going door to door. The volunteers know who lives where, including the people who cannot read and the people who will not leave without their livestock (@wmo2022).

The system works because it is social before it is technical. The last mile is a person, not a message.

The 2020 Cyclone Amphan killed 26 people in Bangladesh after about 2.5 million were evacuated (@sammonds2021). That outcome is not attributable to better meteorology than Germany's. It is attributable to a delivery and action system that has been built, tested, and used repeatedly.

## Local knowledge is not folklore

There is a tendency to treat local knowledge as charming but unscientific. The evidence does not support that condescension.

The *smong* tradition on Simeulue encoded the signs of a tsunami accurately enough to save almost the entire population in 2004 (@mcadoo2006). Farmers in the Sahel read rainfall onset and soil moisture with an accuracy that satellite products have struggled to match. Fishing communities in Aceh know the behaviour of the sea before a tsunami better than any sensor network.

The reason is not mysticism. It is that local knowledge is derived from many observations of a specific place over a long period, which is exactly what a scientific monitoring programme does — just with different instrumentation and a different record-keeping method.

The failure mode is when a modern system displaces local knowledge without replacing its function. A community that stops reading the sea because a buoy is watching has lost a capability if the buoy fails.

## Climate literacy as a practical skill

The last link in the chain is the ability to interpret a forecast. This is a skill, not a body of facts, and it can be taught.

The skills that matter are unglamorous: reading a probability statement correctly ("a 30 per cent chance of flooding" does not mean the flood will be small); understanding what a return period means ("a one-in-100-year flood" is not a flood that will not recur for a century); distinguishing a forecast from a projection; and knowing which agency to trust for which kind of warning.

These are teachable in a few hours, and they are not taught in most school systems.

## The counter-case: when warnings were ignored and it did not matter

Not every ignored warning produces catastrophe, and acknowledging this is necessary for credibility.

In many places, evacuation warnings are issued and a large share of the population stays, and the event turns out to be less severe than forecast. This is not irrational behaviour; it is a rational response to a system that has cried wolf.

The design problem is real: a system that issues too many warnings loses credibility, and a system that issues too few kills people. The resolution used in well-designed systems is to separate the levels of warning clearly, so that the highest level is rare and unambiguous, and to use the lower levels for information rather than evacuation.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that warning failure is primarily a delivery and action problem rather than a detection problem, and that social systems outperform technical ones at the last mile.

It would be wrong if places with advanced detection systems but weak social delivery had lower death tolls than places with modest detection and strong delivery, controlling for hazard. The Ahr valley and Bangladesh comparison suggests the opposite.
