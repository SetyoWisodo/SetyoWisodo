# Knowledge: Forecasts That Do Not Arrive

::: lead
The forecast was correct. That is what makes the Ahr valley failure worth studying: the science worked, the models worked, the meteorologists worked — and 134 people died in a valley where the water arrived at night with no order to leave.
:::

## The Ahr valley, July 2021

In mid-July 2021, a slow-moving low-pressure system stalled over western Europe and produced rainfall that exceeded the local record by a wide margin. The European Flood Awareness System issued warnings days in advance. The German weather service issued warnings. The forecast was, by any technical standard, a success (@kreienkamp2021).

In the Ahr valley, a narrow river valley in Rhineland-Palatinate with villages built close to the water, the flood arrived at night. More than 130 people died in Germany and Belgium. Houses were destroyed, roads disappeared, and the valley's warning system — sirens in some places, mobile alerts in others — reached some residents and not others.

The subsequent investigations identified the failure precisely: the information existed, but it did not become an instruction. Residents received weather warnings, not evacuation orders. Many did not know what a "severe weather warning" meant for their particular street. Some had experienced floods before and believed they knew what to expect. The water came higher than any local memory (@kreienkamp2021).

This is not a story about ignorance. It is a story about the gap between a forecast and a decision.

## The four links of a warning

A warning system has four links, and it fails if any one of them fails.

**Detection.** Instruments and models produce a prediction. This is the link that gets the funding and the scientific attention.

**Delivery.** The prediction reaches the people at risk. Delivery is a function of communications infrastructure, but also of whether people are reachable — a phone that is charged is different from a phone that has a signal.

**Understanding.** The message is comprehended. This requires the message to be in the right language, at the right level of technical detail, and framed in terms of what the recipient should do. "100 mm of rain expected" is a measurement. "Leave the valley before 18:00" is an instruction.

**Action.** The recipient is able to act. This requires transport, a destination, the ability to leave work, and — for many people — the belief that leaving is worth the cost.

Most investment goes to link one. Most failures occur at links three and four (@wmo2022).

## The 2004 tsunami: the warning that existed and did not travel

The Indian Ocean tsunami of December 2004 is the case that established the modern consensus on warning
systems, and its details are worth reconstructing because they show each link failing in sequence.

**Detection existed, in the Pacific.** The Pacific Tsunami Warning Center detected the earthquake and
recognised the tsunami risk within minutes. It issued messages to the countries it was mandated to warn,
which did not include the Indian Ocean states.

**No delivery system existed in the region.** There was no Indian Ocean warning centre, no agreement on
who would call whom, and no list of recipients. Some officials learned of the tsunami from news reports.

**Understanding was absent.** Coastal populations in Aceh, Sri Lanka, and Thailand had no frame for
what a receding sea meant. Where the knowledge existed — as on Simeulue — people ran [@mcadoo2006].
Where it did not, people walked out to look at the exposed seabed.

**Action capacity was absent.** Even where warnings arrived, there was no pre-identified high ground, no
evacuation route, and no drill.

The death toll was approximately 227,000 across fourteen countries [@wmo2022]. The subsequent investment in the
Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System addressed detection and delivery, and only partly addressed
understanding and action — which is why the system is now technically excellent and socially uneven.

## The last mile is a person

The phrase "last mile" is used in the sector to describe the gap between a warning issued and a
household acting. It is worth taking literally.

In a village with no television, no smartphone, and unreliable radio, the warning arrives through a
person: a volunteer with a megaphone, a mosque loudspeaker, a teacher, a fisherman who has a radio. The
system is only as good as the number of people who will physically go and knock.

Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme is built on precisely this insight, with 76,000 volunteers
whose job is to be the last mile [@wmo2022]. The system is expensive in people and cheap in technology,
which is the opposite of most warning investments.

The alternative — a beautiful app that reaches the 40 per cent of the population with smartphones — is
not a warning system. It is a warning system for the people who are already easiest to reach.

## Message design: what the evidence says

The research on warning message design is consistent on several points.

**Specific instructions outperform descriptions.** "Evacuate to the school on the hill" produces more action than "flooding is expected."

**Repetition and multiple channels.** A message received once, on one channel, is less likely to be acted on than the same message received repeatedly through several channels, including one trusted person.

**Trust matters more than accuracy.** People act on warnings from sources they trust. A technically perfect warning from an unknown agency may be ignored, while an imperfect warning from a trusted local figure may be acted on.

**Fear alone does not work.** Messages that induce fear without providing a clear action produce paralysis or denial rather than evacuation (@wmo2022).

**Language and literacy.** In Indonesia, warnings must reach communities speaking hundreds of local languages, with varying literacy. Pictograms, flags, drums, and mosque loudspeakers are not primitive fallbacks; they are appropriate technology.

::: {.box title="Box 11.1 — Testing a warning" kind="note"}
A warning system can be tested before it is needed. Five questions.

1. **Coverage.** What share of the at-risk population receives the message within the required time? Measure this, do not assume it.
2. **Comprehension.** Can a random sample of residents state what they are supposed to do? Test it.
3. **Action capacity.** Do the people who understood the message have the means to act? A warning to evacuate is meaningless for a household with no vehicle and no accessible shelter.
4. **False alarm tolerance.** What happens to trust after a false alarm? Systems that never issue false alarms are systems that wait too long.
5. **Last mile.** Who knocks on the door of the person with no phone? If the answer is "nobody", the system does not reach the people most likely to die.
:::

## Bangladesh: the warning that became routine

The contrast with Bangladesh is instructive, because Bangladesh's system succeeds at exactly the links where Ahr failed.

The Cyclone Preparedness Programme operates through 76,000 volunteers in coastal villages. Warnings are transmitted through multiple channels — sirens, megaphones, mosque announcements, radio, and volunteers going door to door. The volunteers know who lives where, including the people who cannot read and the people who will not leave without their livestock (@wmo2022).

The system works because it is social before it is technical. The last mile is a person, not a message.

The 2020 Cyclone Amphan killed 26 people in Bangladesh after about 2.5 million were evacuated (@sammonds2021). That outcome is not attributable to better meteorology than Germany's. It is attributable to a delivery and action system that has been built, tested, and used repeatedly.

## The forecast that arrives as a number

A recurring failure in warning systems is the mismatch between what a forecast contains and what a
household needs to decide.

A meteorological service produces a probability: a 60 per cent chance of rainfall above a threshold, a
storm surge of 1.5 metres, a wind speed of 120 kilometres per hour. A household needs to know: should we
leave, when, and where to?

The translation between the two is not automatic, and it is rarely anyone's job. The result is that a
warning is issued, the population receives a number, and nothing happens — not because the warning was
wrong, but because it did not answer the question the household was asking.

Three design principles follow from the evidence.

**Lead with the action.** The first line of a warning should be what to do, not what is happening. "Leave
the valley before 18:00" precedes "150 mm of rain expected".

**Give a time, not a probability.** People act on deadlines. A probability is useful for planners, not
for households.

**Repeat through a trusted channel.** A single message from an unknown agency is weaker than the same
message repeated by a known local figure.

The Bangladesh system is built on these principles, which is why it works: the message is an instruction,
delivered by someone the household knows, repeated until it is acted on.

## Local knowledge is not folklore

There is a tendency to treat local knowledge as charming but unscientific. The evidence does not support that condescension.

The *smong* tradition on Simeulue encoded the signs of a tsunami accurately enough to save almost the entire population in 2004 (@mcadoo2006). Farmers in the Sahel read rainfall onset and soil moisture with an accuracy that satellite products have struggled to match. Fishing communities in Aceh know the behaviour of the sea before a tsunami better than any sensor network.

The reason is not mysticism. It is that local knowledge is derived from many observations of a specific place over a long period, which is exactly what a scientific monitoring programme does — just with different instrumentation and a different record-keeping method.

The failure mode is when a modern system displaces local knowledge without replacing its function. A community that stops reading the sea because a buoy is watching has lost a capability if the buoy fails.

## Climate literacy as a practical skill

The last link in the chain is the ability to interpret a forecast. This is a skill, not a body of facts, and it can be taught.

The skills that matter are unglamorous: reading a probability statement correctly ("a 30 per cent chance of flooding" does not mean the flood will be small); understanding what a return period means ("a one-in-100-year flood" is not a flood that will not recur for a century); distinguishing a forecast from a projection; and knowing which agency to trust for which kind of warning.

These are teachable in a few hours, and they are not taught in most school systems.

## The counter-case: when warnings were ignored and it did not matter

Not every ignored warning produces catastrophe, and acknowledging this is necessary for credibility.

In many places, evacuation warnings are issued and a large share of the population stays, and the event turns out to be less severe than forecast. This is not irrational behaviour; it is a rational response to a system that has cried wolf.

The design problem is real: a system that issues too many warnings loses credibility, and a system that issues too few kills people. The resolution used in well-designed systems is to separate the levels of warning clearly, so that the highest level is rare and unambiguous, and to use the lower levels for information rather than evacuation.

## Why people do not evacuate, and why it is usually rational

The most common policy complaint after a failed evacuation is that people ignored the warning. The
research on evacuation behaviour finds that the refusal is usually rational, given the information and
the constraints the household faces.

Five reasons recur.

**They have nowhere to go.** An evacuation order without a destination is an instruction to sleep
outside. Shelters that are distant, unwelcoming, or do not accept animals are not destinations.

**They cannot leave.** No vehicle, no money for transport, a disability, a dependent relative, or a
job that does not permit absence.

**They have been burned before.** A warning that produced nothing last time reduces the credibility of
this one. This is the false-alarm problem, and it is a design failure, not a public failure.

**They fear the aftermath.** In some settings, leaving the house means it will be looted, or that the
family's claim to the property will be weakened.

**They have accurate local knowledge that the official warning lacks.** Sometimes the household knows
the water does not reach that street, and is right.

The policy implication is that evacuation rates are a function of destination quality, transport
provision, and credibility — all of which are design choices.

## A warning is a public good

The economics of warning systems explains why they are underprovided, and the explanation is the same
one that runs through the whole book.

A warning is a public good: non-excludable and non-rival. Once issued, everyone can use it, and one
person's use does not reduce another's. Public goods are underprovided by markets, which is why
meteorological services are publicly funded almost everywhere.

The last mile, however, is not a public good in the same sense. It requires people, drills, and local
organisation, which are club goods or common-pool resources. The underprovision is therefore at the
relay, not at the sensor.

That is the diagnosis, and it explains a pattern: countries with good meteorological services and weak
local preparedness, which is most countries. The sensor is funded because it is a public good with a
clear owner. The relay is not funded because it belongs to no one.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that warning failure is primarily a delivery and action problem rather than a detection problem, and that social systems outperform technical ones at the last mile.

It would be wrong if places with advanced detection systems but weak social delivery had lower death tolls than places with modest detection and strong delivery, controlling for hazard. The Ahr valley and Bangladesh comparison suggests the opposite.

## What a national meteorological service owes the public

If warnings fail at the last mile, part of the remedy lies with the institutions that issue them, and it
is not only a matter of better models.

**Publish in the language of the user.** Not the language of the capital. In Indonesia this is a
practical requirement, not a courtesy.

**Set thresholds jointly with the agencies that act.** A warning threshold agreed with the health
service, the transport authority, and the schools is more likely to trigger action than one set by the
meteorological service alone.

**Name the action and the authority.** "Evacuate" must come from someone with the legal power to order
it, and the public must know who that is.

**Report the misses as well as the hits.** A service that publicises every correct warning and no false
alarm trains the public to distrust the next warning.

**Fund the relay, not only the sensor.** The cheapest part of the system is the instrument. The most
expensive, and the most neglected, is the human network that carries the message the final kilometre.

## What a warning system costs

Numbers matter for the argument that warning is cheap, so here is the arithmetic as it appears in the
evaluations that exist.

A national meteorological service in a middle-income country operates on a budget in the tens of millions
of dollars a year, covering observation networks, models, staff, and dissemination. A national early
warning system for multiple hazards adds a similar order of magnitude.

Set that against the cost of a single event. The Pakistan 2022 floods caused US$14.9 billion in direct
damage [@pakistan2022]. The Texas 2021 freeze caused losses estimated in the hundreds of billions. A
single avoided major event pays for a warning system many times over.

The comparison is not quite fair, because a warning system does not avoid all damage, and because the
counterfactual is unobservable. But the order of magnitude is not in doubt: warning is one of the
cheapest risk-reduction instruments available, and it is chronically underfunded relative to its return.

The reason is the same one that runs through this book. A warning system's benefit is an event that did
not happen, and no budget process rewards an agency for an absence.

## The warning that was too early

A final case is worth recording because it cuts against the chapter's enthusiasm for warning.

In some events, warnings are issued days in advance, and the population does not act because the
forecast is uncertain and the cost of acting is immediate. When the event then does not materialise at
the warned severity, trust falls.

The design response is not to withhold warnings, which kills people. It is to separate the levels: a
low-level advisory that carries no expectation of action, and a high-level order that is rare and
unambiguous. The public learns to distinguish them, and the high level retains its force.

This is a solved problem in engineering terms and an unsolved one in most national systems, which tend to
issue one kind of warning for everything.

## The warning that a community wrote itself

A final case that illustrates the chapter's argument at the smallest scale.

In several coastal communities in Indonesia and the Pacific, the warning system is a drum, a bell, or a
loudspeaker mounted on a pole, and the protocol is a set of beats that everyone knows. The system costs
almost nothing, has no failure mode that requires electricity, and works because it is embedded in the
community's routine.

The limitation is range and precision. The strength is that it is understood, maintained, and used.

The design lesson is not that technology is unnecessary but that a system should have a fallback that does
not depend on the technology. A megaphone with a charged battery and a person who knows which houses to
visit is the last mile, and it is cheap.

## The message that was understood and not believed

A case that isolates the "understanding" link: the 2011 Christchurch earthquake in New Zealand.

The earthquake was preceded by a smaller event in September 2010 that caused damage but no deaths. The
population had been through a serious earthquake, had been told the aftershocks would continue, and had
developed a degree of fatalism. When the February 2011 event struck, the response of many people was to
shelter rather than to evacuate, which was appropriate for the earthquake but not for the structures they
were in.

The lesson is not that the population was irrational. It is that a prior event can calibrate a population's
response in a way that is wrong for the next event, and that the warning message must account for the
calibration.

The design implication is that warnings should be specific about what is different this time, not only
about what is dangerous.
