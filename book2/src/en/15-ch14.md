# Uncertainty: Waiting for Certainty Is Also a Decision

::: lead
Every adaptation decision is made with incomplete information. The only genuine choice is whether the decision is made deliberately or by default. A default is still a decision, and it is usually the more expensive one.
:::

## Three kinds of uncertainty

The word "uncertainty" is used for three different things, and the difference determines what can be done about it.

**Aleatory uncertainty** [@lempert2003] is irreducible randomness. The dice are fair and the outcome is unknown. A particular storm will or will not hit a particular coast, and no amount of research will tell us which.

**Epistemic uncertainty** is lack of knowledge that research can reduce. How much will sea level rise by 2100 under a given emissions pathway? The range has narrowed over the last decade, and it will narrow further.

**Deep uncertainty** is the case where the models themselves are contested, or where the future is determined by social choices that have not yet been made. What will the population of a coastal city be in 2070? That depends on migration, fertility, and policy, none of which are predictable in a way that is useful for engineering.

Most adaptation decisions face all three at once. A seawall design requires a storm return period (aleatory), a sea-level projection (epistemic), and an assumption about the assets behind it (deep).

The practical consequence is that no single "best estimate" is adequate. The relevant question is not what will happen, but what should be done given that several futures are possible.

## The deep uncertainty problem, in one number

Sea-level rise is the case where the difference between epistemic and deep uncertainty matters most,
because the range is wide and the decision has a long life.

The IPCC's assessed range for 2100 under a high-emissions scenario spans roughly half a metre to about
a metre, with a low-likelihood high-impact tail beyond that [@ipcc2021]. A structure designed to the mid-point is
inadequate at the top of the range and overbuilt at the bottom.

Three approaches to this problem are in use, and they are not exclusive.

**Design for the upper bound.** Expensive, and it produces structures that are overbuilt for the
likely future. It is the approach of the Netherlands, which can afford it.

**Design for modularity.** Build a structure that can be raised in stages as the observed sea level
rises. The initial cost is lower, and the option to add height is preserved.

**Design for retreat.** Zone the land behind the structure so that it can be abandoned at a defined
trigger. The structure buys time, and the zoning buys the option to use it.

The third is the cheapest and the least politically attractive. It requires a government to say, in
advance, that a place will eventually be given up — which is the subject of Chapter 12.

## Why adaptation is not a one-time purchase

A plan that assumes the climate of 2050 is a plan that will be wrong by 2060. Adaptation is a process,
and the process has to be institutionalised.

Four features of an institutionalised process recur in the places that manage it well.

**Triggers.** A defined observation — sea level, temperature, rainfall — that activates a defined
response. The trigger is agreed in advance, so that the decision is not made under pressure.

**Review cycles.** A statutory review, every five or ten years, of the risk and the response.

**Funding continuity.** A dedicated revenue stream, not an annual competition.

**Legal standing.** The obligation to adapt is written into law, so that it survives a change of
government.

The Netherlands has all four. Most countries have none, and rely instead on projects.

## Robust decision-making

The dominant approach to this problem in the last two decades is **robust decision-making**: rather than choosing the option that performs best under the most likely future, choose the option that performs acceptably across a wide range of futures (@lempert2003).

The procedure is straightforward in principle.

1. Define the decisions available.
2. Define the scenarios, including ones that are unlikely but not impossible.
3. Evaluate each decision across all scenarios.
4. Identify the decisions that fail badly in any scenario — these are the fragile ones.
5. Prefer decisions that perform acceptably everywhere, even if they are not optimal anywhere.

The result is often a portfolio rather than a single intervention: a modest levee plus a retreat plan plus an insurance scheme, rather than one large structure.

## Option-preserving adaptation

A related principle is that adaptation should preserve options rather than foreclose them.

A seawall built to a fixed height forecloses the option of retreat, because the assets behind it become more valuable and harder to abandon. A decision to zone a floodplain as open space preserves the option of using it for storage now and for development later if the risk turns out to be lower than feared.

The principle is easier to state than to apply, because it cuts against the political economy of infrastructure. A large visible project is easier to fund than a zoning decision that produces no photograph.

## An example: the drainage decision

To make the abstraction concrete, consider a decision that every city faces.

A city's drainage system was designed for a 10-year storm in 1985. The observed rainfall record now
suggests that what was a 10-year storm is a 6-year storm, and the city must decide what to build.

Three options.

**Replace to the old standard.** Cheap, and it produces a system that fails more often than the design
intended. The failures are visible and the decision is easy to defend in the year it is made.

**Replace to the new standard.** Expensive, and it produces a system that is adequate for the current
record and possibly inadequate for the 2050 record.

**Replace to a standard plus a staged upgrade path.** More expensive than the first, cheaper than
building for 2050 now, and it preserves the option to upgrade when the observed record justifies it.

The third option is the robust choice, and it requires two things that cities rarely have: a trigger
defined in advance (the observed rainfall threshold at which the upgrade is authorised) and a funding
mechanism that survives the interval between decisions.

The option that is usually chosen is the first, because it is the cheapest in the budget year and
because the failure it produces will occur in a later administration.

## The cost of delay

Delay is often presented as prudence: wait for better information before committing resources.

The argument fails in cases where the cost of waiting exceeds the value of the information. This is the case for most adaptation decisions, for three reasons.

**Assets have long lives.** A bridge, a drainage system, or a housing estate built now will still be in place in 2070. The decision locks in exposure.

**Damage accumulates.** Every year of delay transfers losses to the people affected this year, who do not get those years back.

**Options close.** A floodplain that is built over cannot be returned to storage cheaply. A community that disperses cannot easily be reassembled.

The formal version of this argument is the **real options** approach, which values the ability to defer or adjust a decision, and which shows that deferral is only valuable when the information expected is decisive and the cost of reversal is low (@lempert2003). For most adaptation decisions, neither condition holds.

## Scenarios are not predictions

A recurring confusion in public argument is between a scenario and a forecast, and it produces both
false alarm and false comfort.

**A scenario** is a coherent description of a possible future, constructed to explore the consequences of
a set of assumptions. It is not a prediction, and its purpose is comparison, not prophecy.

**A forecast** is a statement about what is most likely to happen, with a probability attached.

Climate projections are scenarios. The high-emissions scenario is not a prediction that emissions will be
high; it is a description of what the climate would do if they were. The policy question — which
scenario are we on? — is answered by emissions data, not by the models.

The confusion matters because it allows both errors: dismissing a scenario as unrealistic when it is
merely conditional, and treating it as a forecast when it is not.

## Adaptation as a sequence, not a project

The most useful reframing is that adaptation is not a project with an end date. It is a sequence of decisions, each of which should preserve the ability to make the next one.

This means the unit of planning is not "the seawall" but "the coastal protection pathway": a sequence of measures, triggered by observed conditions, with the triggers specified in advance.

The pathway approach has a practical advantage. It allows a government to commit to a direction without committing to a single expensive project, which makes the politics easier and the engineering more flexible.

The Netherlands has used this approach for river management: a series of measures, each triggered by observed water levels, with the decision rules set in advance and reviewed periodically.

## The precautionary principle, restated

The precautionary principle is often dismissed as a counsel of paralysis, and it is often invoked badly.
A more defensible version is available and it is more useful.

**Weak precaution:** uncertainty is not a reason for inaction when the potential harm is serious and
irreversible. This version is widely accepted and is embedded in the climate convention.

**Strong precaution:** a lack of certainty requires the proponent of an activity to prove it is safe. This
version is contested, and it is difficult to apply to adaptation, where the "activity" is protecting
people.

The version that fits this chapter is narrower: **when a decision is irreversible, uncertainty argues for
preserving options rather than for delay.** A levee that forecloses retreat is irreversible; a zoning
rule that preserves the floodplain is not. The precautionary choice is the reversible one.

That reframing converts a vague principle into a test that can be applied to a specific proposal: which
option can be undone if the projection turns out to be wrong?

## What uncertainty does not justify

There is a rhetorical pattern in which uncertainty is used to justify inaction: the projections disagree, therefore we should wait.

This is a misreading of what the uncertainty is about. The disagreement is usually about the magnitude of a change, not its direction. No serious projection has sea level falling, or global temperature declining, under any emissions pathway.

The decision-relevant question is therefore not "will the climate change" but "how much, how fast, and what should we do given the range." That question has answers, and they are robust across the range.

::: {.box title="Box 14.1 — Four questions for an uncertain projection" kind="note"}
When a projection is presented, ask:

1. **What is the range?** A single number without a range is a red flag.
2. **What drives the range?** Emissions scenarios, model structure, or natural variability? Each has different implications.
3. **What is robust across the range?** The actions that work under all scenarios are the ones to take first.
4. **What would change the decision?** If no plausible value would change the decision, the uncertainty is not decision-relevant, and the debate about it is a distraction.
:::

## The number that decides a seawall

To show how the abstract argument applies, consider the arithmetic of a single decision.

A city must choose between a seawall designed for a 1-in-100-year event and a phased programme of
drainage, zoning, and a smaller wall designed for a 1-in-30-year event with an upgrade path.

The first costs more, protects more, and forecloses the option of retreat behind it, because the land
becomes valuable.

The second costs less, protects less in the tail, and preserves the option, because the land behind a
1-in-30-year wall remains less attractive to develop.

The comparison cannot be resolved by the projections alone, because the projections are a range. It can
be resolved by asking which choice is worse if the upper end of the range is right. Under the upper end,
the first option is adequate for longer and the second requires earlier, larger decisions. Under the
lower end, the first is overbuilt and the second is adequate.

The robust choice depends on the cost of being wrong in each direction, which is a political question
about who bears the loss. That is the honest answer, and it is more useful than a single recommended
design.

## The counter-case: when waiting was right

Not every delay is a failure. Some adaptations were correctly deferred because the information was genuinely insufficient and the cost of a wrong commitment was high.

Early proposals for large sea walls around Jakarta were deferred for years, partly because the subsidence rate was not well understood. Committing to a fixed design before the subsidence data existed would have produced a structure with the wrong height.

The distinction is between **deferring a decision** and **deferring action**. Deferring a decision while monitoring — installing the instruments, defining the triggers, preparing the designs — is prudent. Deferring action while doing none of those things is a decision for the status quo.

## Why insurance is not adaptation

A confusion that recurs in policy is the treatment of insurance as an adaptation measure, and it deserves
a direct rebuttal.

Insurance transfers financial loss. It does not reduce the physical hazard, the exposure, or the
vulnerability. A household that is fully insured and flooded every three years is a household that is
financially cushioned and physically at risk.

The distinction matters because insurance is often counted toward adaptation finance, which inflates the
reported total without changing the risk. A climate finance accounting system that counts an insurance
premium as adaptation is measuring a transfer, not an adaptation.

The useful role of insurance is narrower and real: it converts a large uncertain loss into a small
certain cost, which allows a household or a government to plan. That is a financial service, and a
valuable one, and it is not the same as reducing the risk.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that uncertainty should be managed through robust, option-preserving sequences of decisions rather than deferred until certainty arrives.

It would be wrong if adaptation decisions made after long delays consistently outperformed early decisions, controlling for cost. The evidence on accumulated losses and foreclosed options points the other way.
