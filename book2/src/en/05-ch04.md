# Heat, the Invisible Killer

::: lead
Heat is the deadliest climate hazard in rich countries and the most under-counted in poor ones. It kills people who are indoors, alone, and old, in cities that were not designed for it — and it kills them after the event has stopped being news.
:::

## The counting problem comes first

Before any claim about heat can be believed, the number has to be explained, because heat does not produce a body count the way a flood does.

A flood leaves corpses and a clear cause. Heat leaves a raised mortality rate spread across weeks, concentrated among people already ill, and recorded on death certificates as "cardiac arrest", "respiratory failure", or "natural causes". The heat is not written down.

The standard method is **excess mortality**. Take the number of deaths observed during the heat period and subtract the number expected from the same weeks in nearby, non-heat years. The difference is attributed to heat. It is a statistical estimate, not a count, and it is sensitive to the baseline chosen, the age structure, and whether the analysis allows for short-term displacement — the "harvesting" effect, in which people who would have died within weeks die slightly earlier.

This is why the numbers move so much between studies and why the honest presentation is a range with a stated method.

::: {.box title="Box 4.1 — How excess mortality is calculated" kind="note"}
Two choices dominate the result.

**The baseline.** A common approach fits a temperature–mortality relationship across many years and asks how many deaths occurred above the temperature at which mortality is lowest. Another compares the heat week with the same week in other years. The first is more sophisticated and generally produces higher estimates; the second is more transparent.

**The displacement window.** If the analysis counts deaths over a short window, it may double-count people who were already near death. If it counts over a long window, it may absorb unrelated deaths. Most credible studies use 21–28 days and report sensitivity.

The practical rule: a heat death toll without a stated method is not a number, it is a claim.
:::

## Europe, 2003 and 2022: same hazard, different vulnerability

The 2003 European heatwave killed an estimated 70,000 people (@robine2008). It was a shock not because Europe was unprepared in general, but because the continent had no institutional memory of heat as a mass-casualty event. France, which accounted for a large share of the deaths, had no heat warning system; elderly people died in apartments with closed shutters, and the August holiday had emptied the hospitals of senior staff.

The 2022 European summer was hotter. It killed 61,672 people (@ballester2023). That is a smaller toll than 2003 — but only just, and on a much hotter event. The difference is attributable to what was built between the two: national heat-health warning systems, municipal registers of vulnerable residents, cooling centres, and public communication (@ballester2023).

The comparison is often presented as a success. It is more accurately a partial success with a warning inside it. Twenty years of adaptation reduced the death toll, but heat mortality in Europe has not fallen; in some countries it has risen, because the hazard has grown faster than the adaptation (@ballester2023). Adaptation is not a finished project but a race with a moving opponent.

Italy, Greece, Spain, and Portugal accounted for a large share of the 2022 deaths. The pattern within countries is consistent across studies: deaths concentrate among people over 65, people with cardiovascular or respiratory disease, people living alone, and people in top-floor apartments without cooling.

## The physiology: why wet-bulb matters

The human body cools by evaporation. Sweat evaporates, taking heat with it. The rate at which this works depends not on air temperature alone but on how much water the air can still absorb — which is what **wet-bulb temperature** measures.

At 100 per cent relative humidity, a wet-bulb of 35 °C is the theoretical limit at which a healthy adult at rest can no longer cool down (@sherwood2010). Above that, core temperature rises even without exertion, and death follows within hours.

The figure is a theoretical ceiling for a resting adult, and physiological work since suggests that even lower wet-bulb values become dangerous for older people, for people doing physical work, and for people without access to water or shade. The practical threshold for a labourer in the sun is much lower than 35 °C.

This matters for how heat is communicated. A dry 45 °C in a desert is survivable with water and shade, while humid heat at a lower temperature is not. A humid 35 °C in a tropical city is not, and it is harder to explain to a population that has lived with such heat for generations without wet-bulb language.

## The British Columbia heat dome

In late June 2021, a high-pressure ridge settled over the Pacific Northwest. Lytton, British Columbia, reached 49.6 °C, a Canadian record, and then burned down (@bccoroners2022).

The provincial coroners' service later attributed 619 deaths to the event (@bccoroners2022). The detail of that report is what makes it useful.

About two-thirds of the dead were 70 or older. Fifty-six per cent lived alone. Ninety-eight per cent died indoors. Most had no air conditioning, and many had windows that opened only a few centimetres. The event was not a failure of outdoor warning; it was a failure of the indoor environment and of social contact.

The policy implication is specific. Heat plans that assume people can go to a cooling centre miss the population that dies. The people who died were not mobile, not connected, and in many cases not aware they were in danger. What reaches them is a phone call, a neighbour, or a home visit — not a public alert.

::: {.box title="Box 4.2 — What a heat plan actually has to contain" kind="note"}
1. **A register.** Names, addresses, and phone numbers of people at highest risk, updated before summer, held by the municipality and by the health service.
2. **A trigger.** Temperature thresholds that activate the plan automatically, with a lower threshold for humid conditions.
3. **Outreach.** Calls or visits to everyone on the register, not a public announcement.
4. **Cooling access.** Places that are open, free, reachable on foot or by transport, and welcoming — a library or a mosque works better than a dedicated "cooling centre" that nobody enters.
5. **A communication line.** Simple, repeated, and in the languages people actually speak, including what to do and who to call.
:::

## Ahmedabad: a city that decided

In May 2010, a heatwave in Ahmedabad, India, killed more than a thousand people in a few days. The city's response was not a sea wall or a new power plant. It was a plan.

The Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, adopted in 2013, combined a forecast-triggered warning system, a register of vulnerable residents, adjusted working hours for outdoor labour, free drinking water at public places, and training for health workers. A pilot evaluation found that mortality during a subsequent heatwave was lower than would have been expected from the temperature alone (@hess2018).

The cost was small relative to infrastructure. The hard part was institutional: convincing the city that heat was a health emergency rather than weather, and building the register, which requires knowing who lives where.

## Heat and inequality inside the city

Heat is not distributed evenly across a city, and neither is the ability to escape it.

Surface temperature varies by several degrees between neighbourhoods, tracking tree cover, building density, and the amount of asphalt. The neighbourhoods with the least tree cover are frequently the ones with the lowest incomes, because tree cover follows historical investment (@hoffmann2012).

Air conditioning converts heat into a bill. Households that cannot pay it do not use it, or use it less, and the physiological risk returns. In cities where cooling demand is growing fast, the grid itself becomes a heat vulnerability: a heatwave drives peak demand, the grid fails, and the failure removes cooling precisely when it is needed.

This is the Texas 2021 chain from Chapter 2, run in reverse season.

## The counter-case: where heat deaths fell

Not every hot place kills. Some of the hottest cities in the world have low heat mortality, and the reasons are instructive.

In parts of South Asia and the Sahel, heat mortality is low in part because the population is young, because outdoor work is scheduled around heat, because vernacular architecture — thick walls, courtyards, cross-ventilation, sleeping on roofs — manages heat without electricity. These are adaptations built over generations, and they are not romantic: they are the product of repeated exposure and adjustment.

The danger is that modern construction abandons them. A concrete apartment with a single west-facing window and no cross-ventilation is hotter than the mud-brick house it replaced, and it requires electricity to be habitable. Development that ignores vernacular heat management transfers a physiological risk onto the grid.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that heat mortality is primarily a function of social and built-environment vulnerability rather than of temperature alone, and that modest, cheap interventions — registers, calls, cooling access — can move the death toll substantially.

It would be wrong if heat mortality tracked temperature closely across cities with very different income levels, housing stock, and social organisation, with the social variables adding nothing once temperature is controlled for.

The evidence goes the other way. Two European summers with similar temperatures produced death tolls twenty years apart that differ by an order of magnitude in the same countries, and the difference tracks what was built in between.
