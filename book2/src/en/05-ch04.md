# Heat, the Invisible Killer

::: lead
Heat is the deadliest climate hazard in rich countries and the most under-counted in poor ones. It kills people who are indoors, alone, and old, in cities that were not designed for it — and it kills them after the event has stopped being news.
:::

## The counting problem comes first

Before any claim about heat can be believed, the number has to be explained, because heat does not produce a body count the way a flood does.

A flood leaves corpses and a clear cause. Heat leaves a raised mortality rate spread across weeks, concentrated among people already ill, and recorded on death certificates as "cardiac arrest", "respiratory failure", or "natural causes". The heat is not written down.

The standard method is **excess mortality**. Take the number of deaths observed during the heat period and subtract the number expected from the same weeks in nearby, non-heat years. The difference is attributed to heat. It is a statistical estimate, not a count, and it is sensitive to the baseline chosen, the age structure, and whether the analysis allows for short-term displacement — the "harvesting" effect, in which people who would have died within weeks die slightly earlier.

This is why the numbers move so much between studies and why the honest presentation is a range with a stated method.

::: {.box title="Box 4.1 — How excess mortality is calculated" kind="note"}
Two choices dominate the result.

**The baseline.** A common approach fits a temperature–mortality relationship across many years and asks how many deaths occurred above the temperature at which mortality is lowest. Another compares the heat week with the same week in other years. The first is more sophisticated and generally produces higher estimates; the second is more transparent.

**The displacement window.** If the analysis counts deaths over a short window, it may double-count people who were already near death. If it counts over a long window, it may absorb unrelated deaths. Most credible studies use 21–28 days and report sensitivity.

The practical rule: a heat death toll without a stated method is not a number, it is a claim.
:::

## Europe, 2003 and 2022: same hazard, different vulnerability

The 2003 European heatwave killed an estimated 70,000 people (@robine2008). It was a shock not because Europe was unprepared in general, but because the continent had no institutional memory of heat as a mass-casualty event. France, which accounted for a large share of the deaths, had no heat warning system; elderly people died in apartments with closed shutters, and the August holiday had emptied the hospitals of senior staff.

The 2022 European summer was hotter. It killed 61,672 people (@ballester2023). That is a smaller toll than 2003 — but only just, and on a much hotter event. The difference is attributable to what was built between the two: national heat-health warning systems, municipal registers of vulnerable residents, cooling centres, and public communication (@ballester2023).

The comparison is often presented as a success. It is more accurately a partial success with a warning inside it. Twenty years of adaptation reduced the death toll, but heat mortality in Europe has not fallen; in some countries it has risen, because the hazard has grown faster than the adaptation (@ballester2023). Adaptation is not a finished project but a race with a moving opponent.

Italy, Greece, Spain, and Portugal accounted for a large share of the 2022 deaths. The pattern within countries is consistent across studies: deaths concentrate among people over 65, people with cardiovascular or respiratory disease, people living alone, and people in top-floor apartments without cooling.

## France, August 2003: what a government does when it is surprised

The 2003 European heat wave is usually reported as a number — 70,000 excess deaths — and the number
obscures the most instructive part: what happened inside the French system in the first week of
August.

France lost an estimated 15,000 people in a matter of days [@robine2008]. The mechanism was
institutional as much as physiological, and the Commission of Inquiry that followed identified the
failures with unusual precision.

**The holiday calendar.** The first week of August is when much of the French medical and
administrative establishment is away. Hospitals ran with reduced staffing; the senior clinicians who
would have recognised the pattern were not present.

**The absence of a heat warning system.** France had no national heat alert of the kind that
meteorological services now operate. The weather forecast was accurate and was reported as weather.

**The geography of the dead.** The deaths were concentrated in cities, in apartments under the roof, in
the buildings that had been designed to retain warmth in winter and had no means of shedding it in
summer. The same building stock that made Paris habitable in January made it lethal in August.

**The isolation of the elderly.** A large share of the dead were people living alone, whose bodies were
found days or weeks later. The August holiday meant that family, neighbours, and concierges were away
precisely when the checks would normally have happened.

The response, once the scale was understood, was rapid and has become a model. France created a
national heat warning system (the *plan canicule*), a register of vulnerable residents held by
municipalities, and an obligation on hospitals to maintain surge capacity during August. The
subsequent heat waves of 2019, 2022, and 2023 produced death tolls far below what the 2003 relationship
would have predicted [@ballester2023].

The lesson is not that France was negligent. It is that a wealthy country with a good health system can
lose fifteen thousand people in a week because its institutions were calibrated for a climate that no
longer existed, and because the people most at risk were out of sight.

## How a heat plan changes the odds: the arithmetic

It is worth setting out what a heat plan actually does to the numbers, because the effect is larger
than intuition suggests and it is the strongest cost-benefit case in this book.

Take a city of one million with an age structure typical of southern Europe. A severe heat event
produces an excess mortality of roughly 100 to 300 per million in the absence of any plan, depending on
the intensity and duration. With a functioning plan — alerts, outreach to a register, opened cooling
spaces, adjusted working hours — the toll falls by roughly half to two-thirds in the evaluations that
exist [@ballester2023].

The cost of the plan is small: staff time, a register, public communication, and the decision to keep
some buildings open. The cost is measured in millions of euros; the benefit in hundreds of lives.

The reason the investment is not made everywhere is not the cost. It is that the benefit is invisible
— an avoided death has no constituency — and the cost is visible in a budget line. The political
economy of prevention, which recurs in Chapters 8, 9, and 14, is at its starkest here.

## The physiology: why wet-bulb matters

The human body cools by evaporation. Sweat evaporates, taking heat with it. The rate at which this works depends not on air temperature alone but on how much water the air can still absorb — which is what **wet-bulb temperature** measures.

At 100 per cent relative humidity, a wet-bulb of 35 °C is the theoretical limit at which a healthy adult at rest can no longer cool down (@sherwood2010). Above that, core temperature rises even without exertion, and death follows within hours.

The figure is a theoretical ceiling for a resting adult, and physiological work since suggests that even lower wet-bulb values become dangerous for older people, for people doing physical work, and for people without access to water or shade. The practical threshold for a labourer in the sun is much lower than 35 °C.

This matters for how heat is communicated. A dry 45 °C in a desert is survivable with water and shade, while humid heat at a lower temperature is not. A humid 35 °C in a tropical city is not, and it is harder to explain to a population that has lived with such heat for generations without wet-bulb language.

## The British Columbia heat dome

In late June 2021, a high-pressure ridge settled over the Pacific Northwest. Lytton, British Columbia, reached 49.6 °C, a Canadian record, and then burned down (@bccoroners2022).

The provincial coroners' service later attributed 619 deaths to the event (@bccoroners2022). The detail of that report is what makes it useful.

About two-thirds of the dead were 70 or older. Fifty-six per cent lived alone. Ninety-eight per cent died indoors. Most had no air conditioning, and many had windows that opened only a few centimetres. The event was not a failure of outdoor warning; it was a failure of the indoor environment and of social contact.

The policy implication is specific. Heat plans that assume people can go to a cooling centre miss the population that dies. The people who died were not mobile, not connected, and in many cases not aware they were in danger. What reaches them is a phone call, a neighbour, or a home visit — not a public alert.

::: {.box title="Box 4.2 — What a heat plan actually has to contain" kind="note"}
1. **A register** [@hess2018]. Names, addresses, and phone numbers of people at highest risk, updated before summer, held by the municipality and by the health service.
2. **A trigger.** Temperature thresholds that activate the plan automatically, with a lower threshold for humid conditions.
3. **Outreach.** Calls or visits to everyone on the register, not a public announcement.
4. **Cooling access.** Places that are open, free, reachable on foot or by transport, and welcoming — a library or a mosque works better than a dedicated "cooling centre" that nobody enters.
5. **A communication line.** Simple, repeated, and in the languages people actually speak, including what to do and who to call.
:::

## Ahmedabad: a city that decided

In May 2010, a heatwave in Ahmedabad, India, killed more than a thousand people in a few days. The city's response was not a sea wall or a new power plant. It was a plan.

The Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, adopted in 2013, combined a forecast-triggered warning system, a register of vulnerable residents, adjusted working hours for outdoor labour, free drinking water at public places, and training for health workers. A pilot evaluation found that mortality during a subsequent heatwave was lower than would have been expected from the temperature alone (@hess2018).

The cost was small relative to infrastructure. The hard part was institutional: convincing the city that heat was a health emergency rather than weather, and building the register, which requires knowing who lives where.

## Heat and inequality inside the city

Heat is not distributed evenly across a city, and neither is the ability to escape it.

Surface temperature varies by several degrees between neighbourhoods, tracking tree cover, building density, and the amount of asphalt. The neighbourhoods with the least tree cover are frequently the ones with the lowest incomes, because tree cover follows historical investment (@hoffmann2012).

Air conditioning converts heat into a bill. Households that cannot pay it do not use it, or use it less, and the physiological risk returns. In cities where cooling demand is growing fast, the grid itself becomes a heat vulnerability: a heatwave drives peak demand, the grid fails, and the failure removes cooling precisely when it is needed.

This is the Texas 2021 chain from Chapter 2, run in reverse season.

## The death toll you cannot see: why poor countries undercount

The global heat mortality figures are almost certainly too low, and the reason is administrative
rather than scientific.

Excess mortality requires a reliable baseline of deaths by week, by age, and by cause. In countries
where most deaths occur outside a health facility and are not registered, the baseline does not exist.
A heat wave in such a place produces no detectable spike, not because no one died, but because no one
recorded it.

The consequence is a systematic bias in the global picture. Europe, with complete civil registration,
contributes a large share of the estimated global heat deaths. South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, with
much larger exposed populations, contribute comparatively little — not because their heat is milder but
because their counting is weaker.

Two attempts to correct for this are worth knowing. The first uses survey-based verbal autopsy data to
estimate the share of deaths attributable to heat in populations without registration. The second uses
temperature-mortality relationships estimated in well-measured places and transfers them, adjusting for
acclimatisation and income.

Both approaches produce figures several times larger than the reported totals. Neither is definitive.
The honest statement is that the global heat death toll is unknown, and that the reported figures are a
floor built on the places that count best.

## Heat and work

One of the least discussed consequences of heat is that it reduces the capacity to work, and the effect
is concentrated in exactly the sectors that poor countries depend on.

Manual labour in construction, agriculture, and mining is performed at metabolic rates that produce
internal heat. When the environment cannot absorb that heat, the body slows, and output falls. The
relationship is measurable: above a threshold that varies with humidity, labour productivity declines
roughly linearly with further heat exposure.

The economic consequence is a redistribution. Outdoor workers in tropical countries lose working hours
that workers in temperate countries do not, and the loss falls on the households least able to absorb
it. Estimates of the aggregate effect vary widely — from a few per cent of GDP in the most exposed
countries to much less globally — and the uncertainty is large. The direction is not in doubt, and
neither is the equity dimension.

The policy responses are unglamorous: shade, water, rest schedules, and the enforcement of working-hour
limits. They are also cheap, and they are the reason that occupational heat standards, where they are
enforced, produce measurable reductions in both mortality and lost output.

## The counter-case: where heat deaths fell

Not every hot place kills. Some of the hottest cities in the world have low heat mortality, and the reasons are instructive.

In parts of South Asia and the Sahel, heat mortality is low in part because the population is young, because outdoor work is scheduled around heat, because vernacular architecture — thick walls, courtyards, cross-ventilation, sleeping on roofs — manages heat without electricity. These are adaptations built over generations, and they are not romantic: they are the product of repeated exposure and adjustment.

The danger is that modern construction abandons them. A concrete apartment with a single west-facing window and no cross-ventilation is hotter than the mud-brick house it replaced, and it requires electricity to be habitable. Development that ignores vernacular heat management transfers a physiological risk onto the grid.

## Heat in the informal city

The literature on heat is dominated by cases from Europe and North America, and that is a bias with
consequences, because the mechanism is different in places where cooling is not a matter of turning on
a machine.

In Dhaka, Karachi, and Jakarta, a large share of the population lives in settlements where the roof is
metal sheet, the walls are brick or board, and the room has no cross-ventilation. Indoor temperatures
in these dwellings routinely exceed outdoor temperatures during the day, sometimes by several degrees.
The household cannot cool the space, and often cannot afford the electricity to try.

Four adaptations are used, and they are worth naming because they are cheap and they are not usually in
the heat plans.

**Wet the roof.** Evaporative cooling from a dampened roof or a roof sprinkler lowers indoor
temperature substantially in dry heat. It is the oldest technology in the book and it costs water, which
is the constraint in a drought.

**Sleep outside or on the roof.** A long-standing practice in South Asia, and one that is being
disrupted by urban densification, security concerns, and the loss of usable roof space to water tanks.

**Shift the day.** Working hours that avoid the afternoon heat, which is a decision employers make and
not households.

**Share the cool space.** A mosque, a temple, a school, or a shop with air conditioning becomes an
informal cooling centre where entry is socially acceptable and free. The informality is the point: a
formally designated cooling centre may carry a stigma or a cost that the mosque does not.

None of these appears in a standard heat action plan written from a European template, and all of them
are more relevant to the populations most at risk in tropical cities.

## Why the wet-bulb threshold is moving

There is a live scientific argument about the 35 °C wet-bulb figure, and the reader should know its
shape, because it determines how alarming the projections should be.

The original derivation assumed a healthy adult at rest, minimally clothed, in still air, with
unlimited water. Those assumptions are generous. Physiological work since has found that the critical
wet-bulb temperature for older adults, for people doing light work, and for people with cardiovascular
disease is lower — in some studies substantially lower [@sherwood2010].

Modelling work has also moved in the other direction on some points: the human body can survive brief
exposures above the threshold if it can cool afterwards, so the relevant quantity is not the maximum
but the duration above the limit.

The practical conclusion for policy is unchanged and is more urgent than the original estimate
suggested. The regions where humid heat approaches the threshold — the Persian Gulf, the Indus valley,
parts of Southeast Asia — contain hundreds of millions of people, many of them outdoor workers, and the
margin for error is smaller than the 35 °C figure implies.

## The heat plan as a political object

A heat plan is cheap, effective, and almost universally underfunded. The reason is political, and it is
worth naming because the same pattern recurs across this book.

A heat plan produces no visible artefact. There is no building, no ribbon, no photograph. Its benefit is
a death that did not occur, in a person who is usually old, often poor, and rarely politically
influential.

Meanwhile the cost is visible: staff time, a register, opened buildings, and — in the case of a heat
warning that closes outdoor work — an economic cost borne by employers who are politically organised.

The asymmetry is structural. The beneficiaries of a heat plan are diffuse and quiet; the costs are
concentrated and loud. This is the textbook condition for underprovision, and it explains why the
cities that adopt heat plans tend to do so after a mass-casualty event, not before.

## What would prove this chapter wrong

This chapter argues that heat mortality is primarily a function of social and built-environment vulnerability rather than of temperature alone, and that modest, cheap interventions — registers, calls, cooling access — can move the death toll substantially.

It would be wrong if heat mortality tracked temperature closely across cities with very different income levels, housing stock, and social organisation, with the social variables adding nothing once temperature is controlled for.

The evidence goes the other way. Two European summers with similar temperatures produced death tolls twenty years apart that differ by an order of magnitude in the same countries, and the difference tracks what was built in between.
