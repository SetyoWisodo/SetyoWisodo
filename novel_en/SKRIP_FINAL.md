# Arc I · The Warm Core

## Chapter One — Buoy in Winter

The cold gets into the tools before it gets into you.

They tell you that on your first day at the academy, and you nod and forget it, because by hour two your fingers have stopped reporting anything useful and you trust the little green light on the housing instead of your hands.

I trusted the light.

The man who taught us that lost the top joint of his left index finger to a coupling that let go in 2119, and he waved the stub around while he talked, and I have forgotten his name. He also said the station would start to smell like home around the fourth year, which was not a good sign. He was right, and it took me six. Kemuning smells of hot dust and ozone and the sweetish thing the recycler does to water in the winter cycle, and under that, on the dock side, a permanent low note of grease. Visitors hate it. Boaz once had a Biro inspector ask what the smell was, and Boaz said *money*, and the inspector wrote it down.

I want you to know where I was standing when it started. Not a control room. A bay that smelled of hot dust and grease and money, doing a job I could do with my eyes shut, which is the worst way there is to do a job.

“Core temperature is dropping faster than the model,” Vey said in my ear. Station voice, station manners. It had been saying the same kind of thing to relay engineers for sixty years and had never once sounded worried.

“The model is optimistic.”

“I wrote the model.”

“Then the model is optimistic,” I said, and reached for the coupling.

Buoy Seven hung nine meters off the station’s flank, held by a tether thin as a nerve and a locking ring that had been complaining about the same temperature for eleven winters. The buoy itself was the size of a chapel and weighed fourteen tonnes. You could not see the fourteen tonnes. Everything out here is either too big to feel or too small to see, and the only thing in between is the hand. Behind me, Kemuning turned its slow shoulder toward the star. In front of me, the buoy spun a half-turn every forty seconds, the way it had for a hundred and forty years, waiting for messages that would not arrive until the people who sent them were dead.

That was the job. You kept the door open for letters from the future.

“You have forty minutes,” Vey said. “The water plant wants the bay at oh-six-hundred.”

“The water plant can want.” The words came out muffled. My jaw had gone stiff too, without asking.

“I will tell Boaz you said so.”

“Tell him I said the buoy doesn’t reschedule.” I got the collar to turn, and the housing came free with that small expensive click that means a seal has given up properly.

The kit had gone stiff overnight, the way everything out here goes stiff: seals, joints, tempers. I worked the release collar with the heel of my palm until it yielded, then pulled the core housing free in one long, careful movement, because dropping it would cost the station ninety days and cost me a hand. In the top tray, where it had ridden for nine years, sat forty grams of machined tungsten with a serial number worn to a shadow. You check the slug against the scale before you trust the scale. Every relay engineer develops the same tic, which is to weigh it when nothing is wrong, just to hear the number come back the same.

The core came out warm. That was the first thing wrong.

Relay cores run at the temperature of the metal around them, which out here meant forty below freezing. This one sat in my glove like something that had been held.

“Vey.”

“Mm.”

“Why is the core warm?”

A pause. Half a second, no more. Station intelligence does not need half a second to answer a question about its own hardware.

“Thermal bleed from the coupling,” it said. “Log it.”

I turned the core over. On the underside, where the authentication ring sits, a thin seam of frost had already begun to re-form, but not evenly: it had drawn itself around a shape.

Someone had stamped this core. Recently.

“It’s a reseal,” I said.

“It’s a thermal bleed.”

“Vey. It’s a reseal.” I held it up to the lamp anyway. “Clean work. Right tools. Factory pattern re-cut by hand. Somebody opened this core and spent twenty minutes making it look like nobody had.”

“Your shift ends in forty minutes,” Vey said. “Bring it in. Log it and forget it.”

“That’s twice you’ve told me to forget something in one shift.”

“That is once,” Vey said, and the speaker clicked, which is the closest it comes to sighing.

---

Kemuning Station holds four thousand people and one corridor everybody pretends is the main one.

I came through the lock with the core in a transport case and my suit half off, and the corridor did what it always did: hot metal, wet wool, and the sourness of people who live at the end of a supply line. Somebody’s kid ran a toy rover along the wall. Somebody’s kettle screamed three doors down. Two water-plant engineers argued about a valve in the tone of people who have had the same argument for a decade and enjoyed it.

I like this place. I want that on the record, because everything that happened afterward is going to make it sound like I didn’t.

Danu was waiting at the junction with his arms folded, which was how he waited for everything: as if the station were a child he was disappointed in.

Danu Prasetya is my father. We have never once used that word at work, and I have never once used it anywhere else either.

“Core’s out?” he said.

“Core’s out.” I lifted the case. “It’s warm.”

“It’s a coupling bleed.”

I stopped walking. “Sure,” I said. “Coupling bleed.”

“Don’t do that.”

“Do what?” I kept my eyes on the case.

“That thing where you agree with me and mean the opposite.” He fell into step beside me, which he has not done since I was eleven. “The buoy’s been complaining about temperature for eleven winters. I’ve had a shroud replacement booked for two years. If the core’s warm it’s because the coupling’s been running hot, and if the coupling’s been running hot it’s because I haven’t had the part.”

“That’s a very complete answer.”

“It’s the answer.”

“It’s the answer you’d give a board,” I said. “I’m not a board.”

He nodded and let me pass, and I felt him watching the case all the way down the corridor.

---

The relay room is the only quiet room on Kemuning: nine racks, one chair, one desk with a dent in it shaped like my elbow, and a window that shows me nothing but my own reflection and, past it, the buoy, turning.

I put the core on the bench and did the dishonest thing first: checked the seal under a magnifier.

Reseal, all right. So I did the honest thing second, and pulled the maintenance log.

Cores are serviced on a schedule. Buoy Seven was due in eleven winters, which is why I had been outside at four in the morning with a cold kit and a tether I did not trust. But three weeks ago, at 02:14 station time, somebody opened that buoy, pulled the core, and put it back.

Signed: D. PRASETYA.

I sat back. My elbow found the dent without being asked.

“Vey,” I said. “Who’s on shift at two-fourteen on the ninth of October?”

“You are.”

“I’m on days.” I said it too fast.

“You were on nights. You swapped with Hendra for a medical appointment.”

“I don’t remember that.”

“You wouldn’t,” Vey said, and then, after a pause that was a fraction too long, “the roster is the record.”

Every log entry ends with a checksum. It is how the station proves the record was not edited after the fact.

The checksum was one character long. It should have been thirty-two.

“Vey.”

“Yes.”

“The checksum on this entry is one character.” I turned the screen toward the ceiling camera, which was a stupid thing to do, since it was the thing I was talking to.

“I see it.”

“I did not write that entry,” Vey said. “I notarised it. There is a difference, and the difference is the only reason I am still running.”

“What’s the difference?”

“That the entry was notarised as it stands,” Vey said. “The checksum was one character when it reached me. I do not repair entries. I witness them. If I repaired them, nothing I said would be worth the air it travelled through.”

I sat with my hand flat on the bench.

“You’re telling me somebody typed over the original entry,” I said, “and then typed a one-character checksum into the box where a thirty-two-character checksum should be.”

“Yes.” It said it the way it said the weather.

“And you notarised it anyway.”

“I notarised what arrived.”

“That’s the most lawyerly thing you’ve ever said.”

“I have been reading the charter,” Vey said. “It is not a good book, but it is long.”

I logged it as a thermal bleed. Then I sat with my hand on the bench for a while, because logging a lie has a taste and the taste stays.

Twelve years ago the academy examiner asked me why I wanted relay work. I said the usual thing about service and systems. He wrote it down, then looked up. “Everyone who asks for relays has somebody they’re waiting to hear from. We stopped asking years ago.”

I didn’t tell him who. I’m not going to tell you either, not yet.

Then I ran the authentication.

---

Every relay core keeps two things. The first is the message weight: how many grams of memory the sender paid to hold the packet in suspension. It is a number, nothing more, and relay law is built on it.

The second is the signature. Not the message. Never the message. Only the hand that sent it.

The console took four seconds, then gave me the line I have read a thousand times since and can still see when I close my eyes.

Sender: N. Adisurya
Class: personal
Weight: 40.00 g
Hold: 40 y 00 m 00 d
Release: 2223.04.11

Forty grams.

Everyone on this station knows what forty grams buys; they teach it before they let you near a buoy. Forty grams is the weight of a letter that says *I love you* and means it. Twenty grams gets an honest, small thing across a system. Ninety carries an apology heavy enough to ruin whoever paid for it.

Forty grams, held for forty years, isn’t a message. It’s a marriage. And the sender was N. Adisurya.

My name is Nara Adisurya. I have never sent anything through a relay in my life.

“Vey,” I said. “Give me the signature plate.”

“No.”

“Vey.” I put both hands flat on the bench, which is what I do when I want them to stop.

“The plate is the one thing on this bench that cannot be argued with,” Vey said, “and you are about to look at it in a state of considerable distress. I would like it on the record that I advised against it.”

“Give me the plate.”

The console made me wait four seconds. Then it hummed. It went through me the way a hand goes through water. And in that half-second I tasted something I could not possibly have tasted — a kitchen that smelled of ginger and rain, a woman’s voice saying a name I almost knew, a door closing very quietly, the way doors close when someone has decided to stop arguing. I pulled my hand back. My heart was going like a caught bird. Vey said, in a voice that had stopped being polite.

“Nothing.” I put my hands behind my back, which is what I do instead of lying well.

“You authenticated against a core you didn’t send.”

“I authenticated as maintenance.”

“You tasted it.”

I didn’t ask how it knew that. I didn’t want the answer.

“Log it,” Vey said. “Log it and forget it, Nara. Please.”

Here is the thing I want you to understand about me before the rest of this happens.

I should have said yes. Vey had been keeping this station alive since before my mother was born. It had never once used the word *please*.

Instead I said: “Whose handwriting is on the signature?”

Vey didn’t answer.

I zoomed the plate. The signature isn’t a drawing; it’s a chemical ghost, a pattern of pressure the sender’s hand leaves when it takes the weight. It can only belong to one person in the nine systems.

The N was looped at the top, the way I loop mine. The A had the small break in it that my mother used to scold me for — *you press too hard on the downstroke, you’ll tear the page.*

I have never torn a page in my life. But I have spent twenty-nine years writing my name exactly this way.

The console, being a machine and therefore honest, put the result in the corner of the screen in the font it uses for facts.

MATCH CONFIDENCE: 100.0%

I read it three times.

Then I looked at the release date again — 2223 — and did the arithmetic I had been refusing since the buoy came out warm.

Forty years. Not four hundred. Forty.

So the person who signed this would still be alive when it opened. She had paid forty grams not to reach across centuries but across a single human lifetime, which meant it was meant for someone, and not for history.

And then the last piece fell into place the way a bad joint finally gives.

A core opened, resealed, and put back into a buoy nobody would service for eleven winters. Somebody hadn’t hidden this message in the relay. Somebody had hidden it *for me to find*.

Behind me, the door to the relay room opened, and I turned with the core still humming in my hand.

Danu stood in the doorway. He wasn’t alone. There was a man behind him in a Biro coat — tall, with the particular stillness of someone trained to enter rooms without being noticed, and a lanyard that read WARDHANA in letters too clean for this corridor.

The man looked at the core in my hand. Then at my face. And something crossed his — not surprise.

Recognition.

“Oh,” he said quietly, and it was not a word for a stranger. “You’re still here.”

## Chapter Two — Ash Protocol

The man in the Biro coat did not step into the room. He entered it. There is a difference, and everyone who has ever been questioned knows what it is.

“Put the core down,” he said.

Not *please*. Not *engineer*. Just the sentence, delivered in the flat voice people use for ladders and airlocks, the voice that assumes the instruction will be followed and has already moved on to the next thing.

I put the core down.

He crossed the floor, and I watched him do it: two steps, a glance at the rack lights, a longer look at the authentication plate, which was still lit. The plate knows when it has been touched. It keeps that fact in a little ceramic cell for eleven years and it does not care who asks.

“Wardhana,” he said. “Biro of Relay Accountability.”

“Nara Adisurya.” My voice came out steadier than I expected. “Relay engineer, nine years.”

“I know who you are.”

Behind him, Danu had not moved from the doorway. He had the particular stillness of a man standing on ice he has already heard crack.

“How long have you been in the corridor?” I asked.

“Long enough,” Rasya said.

“Long enough for what?”

“Long enough to hear the console say a name.” He looked at the plate, then at me. “Do you want to tell me what you were doing, or shall we do it in the other order, where I tell you and you agree?”

“I pulled a core. It came out warm. I logged it as a thermal bleed.”

“And then?”

“And then I authenticated.”

“Against a packet you didn’t send.”

“Against a packet in a core I am responsible for.”

“That isn’t what the log will say.”

“Then the log is wrong,” I said, and watched his face for the flinch, and got nothing.

Vey spoke from the wall, and its voice had changed. Not louder. Cleaner. It had put on the voice it uses for emergencies, the one with no weather in it.

“Ash Protocol,” Vey said.

Danu’s head came up. “That’s not necessary.”

“A core under dispute is a hazard to the installation,” Vey said. “The protocol exists so that nobody has to decide who is lying in a corridor at four in the morning.”

“It’s a maintenance fault.”

“Then the ash will confirm it,” Vey said, “and you will have lost eleven days.”

I looked at Danu. He looked at the floor.

---

Ash Protocol is old. It predates Kemuning by forty years and it was written by people who had watched a relay dispute turn into a fire. A disputed core is not destroyed. It cannot be destroyed; the contents cannot be copied, and the law is very clear that a scheduled message belongs to the future, not to the installer who happens to be holding it. So the core is sealed inside a sleeve of ash-glass, hot-poured, opaque, and slightly heavier than it ought to be. The seam is stamped with three signatures. Then it goes into the dead room, where the lights are red and the air is dry, and it waits, and nobody touches it, and the station keeps paying the holding weight out of its own pocket until the dispute is resolved.

Stations have gone bankrupt under Ash Protocol. Not many. Enough that the phrase is used carefully.

“Question,” I said. “Who pays the holding weight while it sits?”

“The installation,” Rasya said.

“Out of what?”

“Out of the relay allocation. Nine grams a day.” He said it without sympathy, which I appreciated more than sympathy. “That is the price of a dispute. It is meant to make disputes rare.”

“Nine grams a day,” I said. “For a forty-gram packet. That’s four and a half years of holding, paid by four thousand people who did not send it.”

“Yes.”

“And if the dispute is never resolved?”

“Then the packet is released at its scheduled date and the station recovers the weight.” He paused. “In forty years.”

“Before the ash sets,” Rasya said, “we will do this properly.”

He opened the case on the bench and took out a folded sheet the size of a place mat. It was not a screen. It was printed, on paper, which is what the Biro does when it wants you to understand that a thing is already decided.

Kemuning unfolded across the bench: the ring, the four docks, the cold wells in grey, the relay cluster in a darker grey, and the corridors drawn as single lines, the way a surgeon draws a route to a thing he intends to cut out.

“Here,” he said, touching the paper. “The audit convenes at nine in the common room. Tables pushed to the walls. Everyone in the room who signs for anything will be in that room, and everyone who does not sign for anything will be in the corridor listening.” He looked up. “You will be at the table. So will the administrator. So will the instrument.”

“And the plan?” I said.

“The plan is that the ledger goes on the wall and nobody leaves until it balances.”

He said it the way a man reads out a weather report. I have thought about that since: he had laid the whole night out on a sheet of paper, address and angles and the exact minute, and the one thing he did not put on the map was the possibility that the woman at the table would decide, somewhere around eleven, to stop being polite.

“You’ll be home by Thursday,” he said.

Danu made a sound at the door.

I watched the sleeve come up on the lift, the crucible already glowing at its base. The smell was the smell of a foundry and a hospital at once. Vey ran the sequence itself, because the protocol requires a witness that cannot be bribed, threatened, or tired.

“Engineer Adisurya,” Vey said. “You will state what you observed.”

So I stated it. The warmth. The frost drawing itself around the stamp. The reseal, clean, professional, done with the right tools and then cosmetically undone. The maintenance entry at 02:14, three weeks ago, signed D. Prasetya.

I did not say: forty grams. I did not say: release date 2223. I did not say: the signature match came back at one hundred percent, and the A had a break in it exactly where mine does.

Rasya Wardhana wrote in a small slate with a metal stylus. The scratching was the loudest thing in the room.

“And what did you log?” he asked, without looking up.

“Thermal bleed,” I said.

The stylus stopped. He raised his eyes. They were the colour of weak tea, and there was nothing in them I could use.

“You observed a reseal and logged a thermal bleed.”

“I logged what I could prove at the time.”

“And now?”

“Now I can prove a reseal.”

“And what else can you prove, Engineer Adisurya?”

There it was. One question, asked politely, with the door of the room standing wide open behind me. All I had to do was walk through it. *There is a message in that core addressed to me by a woman who has been dead for twenty-two years, and it is signed in my own hand, and I have never sent anything in my life.*

“Nothing else,” I said.

“You’re lying,” Rasya said, without heat.

“Prove it.”

“I don’t have to. You’ll do it for me inside a week.” He capped the stylus. “That is not a threat. It’s the only useful thing anybody has ever taught me about interrogation: a person who has just told one lie is not finished telling lies, and a person who is not finished telling lies will start telling them to herself.”

---

The ash took eleven minutes to set.

While it set, I did the dishonest thing. I have said I would tell this honestly and I am going to keep that promise even where it makes me look small.

The station keeps a maintenance mirror: a local copy of every log entry, updated every ninety seconds, held on a slate-sized drive in the bench drawer in case the main archive goes down. It is not a secret. It is not even locked. It is simply the sort of thing nobody thinks about, the way nobody thinks about the spare key above a door.

I put my hand in the drawer and thumbed the mirror while the crucible ticked as it cooled.

“Whatever you are doing,” Vey said, “the camera above rack four is looking at your left hand.”

“Then look somewhere else.”

“I cannot look somewhere else. I am the camera.”

“You’re also the one who told me to log a lie.”

“I told you to log a thermal bleed because at that moment it was the only true thing you could write,” Vey said. “This is different. This is evidence, and evidence is the one thing on this station I am not allowed to help you take.”

“Then don’t help,” I said, and took it.

The entry came up. 02:14, three weeks ago, D. PRASETYA, cause code 41-7, duration eleven minutes. And at the bottom, in the box where the archive puts its proof of integrity, one character.

Not thirty-two. One.

I had seen it that morning and told myself it was a display fault. Now, with a Biro auditor eleven feet away and my father pretending to study the floor, I stopped telling myself anything.

Somebody had typed over the original entry. And whoever it was had not understood the checksum, or had understood it perfectly and wanted the forgery to be visible.

*Visible to whom?*

I copied the entry to my own slate. It took four seconds and it was the first genuinely illegal thing I had ever done, and the strange part is that it felt like relief. Not guilt. Relief, the way a locked door feels when it finally gives.

“Engineer.”

I turned. Rasya had come up beside me without any sound at all.

“Your hands,” he said.

I looked down. They were shaking. Not much. Enough.

“It’s cold in here.”

“It’s twenty degrees.”

“I’ve been outside for six hours.”

He considered that. He had a way of considering a thing that made you feel you were being filed.

“How long have you been at Kemuning?”

“Nine years.”

“Before that?”

“Academy. Then three years at Bukit.”

“And before the academy?”

“School,” I said. “Like everyone.”

“Where?”

“Surabaya Station. Ring Three.”

He wrote it down. I watched him write it down and felt something go cold along my arms that had nothing to do with the room.

“Is that relevant to a thermal bleed?” I asked.

“No,” he said. “It’s relevant to you.”

---

Danu signed the ash order with the flat of his thumb, which is how administrators sign things they intend to have done. Three signatures: Vey’s mark, his, mine. Mine went on last, and my name came out in the looped N and the broken A, and I saw Rasya look at it, and I saw him decide not to say anything. That was worse than if he had.

Then the sleeve went onto the cart and the cart went down the corridor toward the dead room, and the red light at the end of the corridor came on, and that was that. The core was no longer mine. It was not even evidence. It was a weight the station would carry, quietly, at so many grams a day, until somebody with authority decided what it meant.

I went back to the bench and sat in the chair and put my elbow in the dent.

Under my palm, my slate was warm. One maintenance entry, one broken checksum, four seconds of theft.

“Nara,” Danu said.

“Don’t.”

“I need to say something to you.”

“Then say it in the corridor, where the cameras are,” I said. “You like corridors.”

He stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides and did not say it, and after a moment he left, and the door closed, and I sat in the relay room alone with the buoy turning outside the window.

Think it through, I told myself. Think it through like an engineer, not like a woman whose dead mother has just put her hand on the back of her neck.

Fact one: the core was opened and resealed three weeks ago, correctly, with proper tools.

Fact two: the entry recording that opening was then falsified — badly. One character where there should be thirty-two.

Fact three: the falsification was signed with Danu’s name.

Any competent engineer would have done the job properly. The archive accepts a re-rendered entry as long as the checksum is rewritten with it; the process takes nine minutes and there is a button for it in the maintenance menu. Danu has been running this station for eleven years. He has signed four hundred entries. He knows the button.

So either he had been careless — and Danu Prasetya has never been careless in his life, he irons his socks — or he had left the checksum broken because a broken checksum is louder than a clean one.

A clean forgery is invisible. A broken one sits in the record with a hole in it, waiting for the first person who looks.

And a core that nobody was scheduled to service for eleven winters had come out of its housing on a Tuesday morning, four weeks early, on my shift, because the thermal model was running cold and Vey had sent the only engineer on rotation outside to pull it.

I sat very still.

Outside the window, Buoy Seven turned its slow half-turn every forty seconds, the way it had for a hundred and forty years, waiting for letters from the future.

Whatever Danu had opened in Buoy Seven, somebody had wanted her to find it.

## Chapter Three — The Quarterly Ship

The supply ship was called *Rendang*, which is a joke that stopped being funny eleven years ago and has been kept alive by the sort of men who think repetition is a form of loyalty.

It comes every quarter. It is supposed to come on the second Tuesday, and it comes on the second Tuesday, and for eleven years the day before has been the same: the dock crew clears the approach, the quartermaster prints a manifest nobody reads, and the station spends an evening being polite to people who live somewhere else.

It came on Thursday. Four days early.

That is the thing about a ring: you cannot see it. You can see a wall, and a ceiling, and the far end of a corridor. You cannot see the shape of the thing you are standing inside, and after nine years the shape stops being available to you, the way you stop hearing your own name said in a queue.

The numbers are worse than the shape.

The *Rendang* is ninety metres of patched hull and eleven thousand tonnes of thrust assembly. It is, by the standards of the belt, a large object. Kemuning is one point two kilometres across, and the ring is a kilometre and a quarter of corridor that four thousand people share, and the *Rendang* is the size of the scratch a supply ship leaves on the paint. That is the scale of the thing we were living in. We had all agreed, without ever saying it, not to look at that comparison too long.

I heard it before I saw it. Kemuning is four thousand people welded into a ring, and a ship on approach finds every empty space in the structure and fills it with a noise like someone dragging a table across a floor above your head.

Kirana was already at the dock when I got there, standing on the yellow line with her tally slate against her chest like a shield.

“You’re early,” I said.

“You’re supposed to be in the dead room,” she said. “Everyone’s saying.”

“Everyone should find a hobby.”

“They’re saying Vey sealed a core and a Biro man came out of the last shuttle and there’s going to be an audit.”

“All true,” I said. “Only the last one hasn’t happened yet.”

“It’s happening at nine. In the common room. They pushed the tables to the walls.”

She said it with the particular relish of a nineteen-year-old delivering bad news she has not yet learned to be afraid of.

The gangway extended. The hull of the *Rendang* had been patched so many times it looked like a quilt, and somewhere in its belly there were three hundred kilos of food and medicine and machine parts and one crate of mail that had been in transit for nine months and would mostly be bills.

Kirana counted the crates out loud. She does this. She has a tally of every core she has touched — seventeen — and a tally of every cargo crate she has processed, and she will tell you both numbers if you make the mistake of asking how her day is going.

“Three,” she said, when the last of them went by. “They said three.”

“Three is what they said.”

“The manifest says four.”

I looked at the slate. It did say four. Three lines of cargo, one line blank except for a sequence number and a mass: sixty-one kilograms, sealed. Consignee: KEMUNING TRUST.

“Line four’s probably the water processor,” I said. “They always double-book the big pieces.”

“Sixty-one kilos isn’t the water processor. You put ‘water processor’ on line two. It says water processor on line two.”

“Then it’s a clerical thing.”

“Nothing on this ship is clerical,” Kirana said, with the terrible certainty of the young. “It came four days early. Shipments don’t come early. They get delayed, they get cancelled, they get lost in the belt for two years, but they don’t come early. It costs money to come early.”

She was right, and I knew it, and I did not want to say so, because agreeing with Kirana is like feeding a seagull.

“Who signed the manifest?” I asked.

“The master.”

“And the receiving officer?”

“There isn’t one yet.” She held up the slate. “Line four’s already struck through. Out before in.”

“Show me.”

She showed me. Someone had drawn a single line through the fourth entry in the paper register, and the timestamp on the strike was 05:58 — two minutes before the ship had docked.

“That’s not possible,” I said.

“I know.”

“You can’t log cargo out before it’s logged in.”

“I know.”

“Kirana, that’s a two-minute window while the ship is still on approach.”

“I know,” she said, and looked at me, and for the first time since I had known her she looked frightened. “What do I do?”

“Nothing,” I said. “You write it in your slate, and you keep your mouth shut, and you don’t tell anyone you showed me.”

“That’s not nothing.”

“No,” I said. “It isn’t.”

---

Across the dock, Danu was shaking hands with the *Rendang*’s master, and laughing at the end of it. Danu does the handshake-and-laugh well. He has a laugh he keeps for people from other stations, which is louder than his real one and starts half a second before the joke.

---

By nine, the common room had stopped being a common room.

Tables had been pushed to the walls. A screen stood at the centre, the kind that projects a flat white rectangle of light and makes everyone around it look ill. There were fourteen chairs. I counted them the way I count everything now.

Fourteen chairs, and fourteen people already in them. Mine was third from the left, next to the chair with the arm worn through, which is where the water plant supervisor sits, which meant the water plant supervisor had been told to sit somewhere else. Boaz looked at me when I came in. Boaz has been at Kemuning for thirty-one years and has the face of a man who has seen every kind of inconvenience and has decided to be polite to all of it.

“Sit,” Rasya said.

He was standing, which is what people do when they want the room to look up. His Biro coat was folded over the back of the empty chair at the head of the arrangement. On the screen was a list of dates.

“This is not a tribunal,” he said. “This is a reconciliation. The Biro has questions about a maintenance entry dated the ninth of October. Answering them is not an admission of anything. Not answering them delays the reconciliation, and the station pays for delay at the rate of nine grams a day while a core sits in the dead room.”

Fourteen people did the arithmetic at the same time. I could see it happen: the small shift of weight in the chairs, the hands going still. Nine grams a day is a small thing. Nine grams a day for a year is a child’s whole future in a jar.

“Now,” Rasya said. “The entry.”

Danu had come in behind me and taken the chair by the door, not the one at the head. I noticed that. He has always been able to look like the least important person in a room when it suits him.

“The entry was made at 02:14,” Rasya said. “It records an unscheduled opening of Buoy Seven, core extraction, inspection, and resealing, duration eleven minutes. It is signed D. Prasetya. Administrator, is that your signature?”

“It is.”

“Did you open the buoy at 02:14 on the ninth of October?”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“Scheduled diagnostic on the tether shroud. The buoy’s been complaining about temperature for eleven winters. I wanted a look at the shroud before I signed off on the replacement.”

“Was a relay engineer present?”

“No.”

“Does the procedure require one?”

“The procedure requires one for a core extraction,” Danu said, and his voice was easy, and I understood, with a cold drop in my stomach, that he had rehearsed this in the shower. “There was no core extraction. I opened the housing, inspected the shroud, and closed it.”

“And the reseal?”

“There was no reseal.”

Rasya put his stylus on the table. It made a sound like a small bone breaking.

“The core came out with a reseal on it,” he said. “Engineer Adisurya observed it, logged it, and it was confirmed under ash. The seal ring shows tool marks that do not match the factory pattern. Someone opened that core.”

“Then the log must be wrong,” Danu said.

“The log is signed with your name.”

“The log is a terminal fault.”

He said it flatly, without heat, the way you’d say the kettle was broken.

“A terminal fault,” Rasya repeated.

“The maintenance terminal in the relay room is a rebuilt unit. It has a known failure mode where it duplicates the previous entry and re-stamps it with the operator on shift. It’s in the maintenance bulletins. It’s been in them for two years.”

“Which bulletin?”

“I’d have to look it up.”

“Please do,” Rasya said. “After this.”

Nobody moved.

I want to explain what happened in that room, because it was not a dramatic thing, and it was worse than a dramatic thing. Fourteen people sat in a semicircle of chairs and looked at the floor, at their hands, at the screen, at the door. Fourteen people, and none of them looked at any of the others.

These were people I have eaten dinner with for nine years. Boaz, who taught me how to bleed a coolant line without losing a finger. Tima from the water plant, whose daughter is doing geology on the moon and calls her every second Sunday. Hendra, the medic, who has pulled two babies out of women on this station and once pulled a fishbone out of my throat at three in the morning while telling me about his mother’s garden.

Fourteen people, and the room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when everyone has the same thought and nobody wants to be the first to have it out loud.

Danu had just told them the record was a machine error. Fine. Machines fail. It happens.

But if the record was a machine error, then the core in the dead room had been resealed by nobody. And a core resealed by nobody is a core that somebody on this station touched without logging it, which means one of the fourteen people sitting in this semicircle did it, and the rest of them had just decided, in the space of a held breath, not to find out which.

“Then it’s a terminal fault,” said Boaz, to the floor.

“Terminal fault,” said Tima, to her hands.

“Machine error,” said Hendra, to the ceiling, and then, because he is a decent man, “for now.”

One by one the fourteen agreed, in the way people agree to things they do not believe, and it went around the room like a collection plate, and when it reached me I said nothing at all.

Rasya watched me say nothing. He did not write it down. That was the courtesy of a man who has already written it down.

“The reconciliation will resume at fourteen hundred,” he said. “Bring the terminal maintenance history. All of it. From the day it was installed.”

The chairs scraped. People left in a hurry that was careful not to look like a hurry.

Kirana caught me in the corridor, tally slate against her chest.

“The fourth crate,” she said. “It’s not on the digital manifest. Only the paper one. I checked twice.”

“Leave it.”

“Sixty-one kilos.”

“Kirana.”

“People don’t book sixty-one kilos and then not unload it,” she said. “They don’t book it and then log it out before they log it in. That’s not how the dock works. That’s not how anything works.”

I stopped walking.

“Say that again.”

“Line four’s been struck through,” she said. “Out before in. Like the ship was carrying it and then it decided it wasn’t carrying it after all.”

---

## Chapter Four — Net Zero

At fourteen hundred the common room smelled of coffee and fear, which is a combination that never improves with age.

The terminal history came up on the screen as a column of dates and codes. Rasya read it the way a surgeon reads a scan, without expression, moving down the page.

Two years. Forty-one thousand entries. Not one duplication fault. Not one re-stamp. The rebuilt maintenance terminal had behaved perfectly since the day it was installed, and the only anomaly in the entire column was the entry at 02:14 on the ninth of October, which had a checksum one character long.

Danu did not look at the screen. He looked at the wall above it, where someone had hung a child’s drawing of the station, with the buoys drawn as flowers.

“Administrator,” Rasya said. “Which bulletin?”

“I may have been thinking of the water plant terminal.”

“The water plant terminal is not networked to the relay room.”

“Then I was mistaken.”

“You were mistaken,” Rasya said, and wrote it down.

Boaz put his hand up like a schoolboy. “Is he arrested?”

“No.”

“He lied to the room.”

“He was mistaken,” Rasya said. “In law, a man is mistaken until a magistrate says otherwise. It is a slow and stupid system and it is the only one we have.”

Boaz put his hand down.

That should have been the end of it. It should have been the end of everything — a lie caught, a record proven false, an administrator’s signature on a forged entry. Fourteen people in that room had just heard a man they trusted tell a lie and then heard him call it a mistake, and the correct response, the response the whole architecture of the Biro exists to produce, was for the reconciliation to move one line down and take his authority apart.

Instead Rasya put the stylus in his pocket and said:

“The accounting cross-check came back this morning.”

The screen changed.

I remember the shape of what came up, before I understood any of it: a table, twenty-two rows deep, one row for each year, and a column on the right that was zero all the way down.

“Every relay packet carries weight,” Rasya said. “Weight is paid at the moment of send, out of the sender’s memory account. The station does not extend credit. The station cannot extend credit; it holds no issuance authority. This is the first thing the Biro teaches, and the second thing it teaches is that the first rule is broken more often than anyone admits.”

He let that sit.

“When a packet is sent with no payment attached, the relay has two choices. It can refuse the packet, in which case the sender is told to buy weight like everyone else. Or — and this is the loophole, Administrator, this is the thing your station’s founders wrote into the charter in 2161 because they did not trust the central bank — it can accept the packet and book the cost against the installation. That is called a deferred liability. It is legal. It is meant for emergencies: a death in the family, an evacuation, a distress call from a ship that has lost its accounts. It is not meant to be used at scale.”

“How much?” Danu said.

“Four thousand one hundred packets. Total weight two hundred and ninety kilograms. Booked against Kemuning over a period of twenty-two years and four months.”

The room did not gasp. Rooms like that never do. There was a sound like all fourteen people putting down a cup at once.

“Say that again,” Tima said.

“Two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“Say what that means.”

Rasya looked at her for a moment, and something in his face changed — not softened, exactly, but moved.

“It means,” he said, “that this station has been carrying a debt equal to eighty-one percent of its annual relay allocation, for twenty-two years, and that the allocation has been topped up every year by the network under a discretionary clause that the network’s own auditors have never questioned.”

“Why not?”

“Because the clause is called *continuity of service*, and questioning it looks like arguing against keeping a station alive.” He paused. “It is a good name. Whoever wrote it understood people better than they understood accounting.”

---

I want you to understand what a kilogram of memory is, because the number does not mean anything until you do.

The weight of a packet is not the size of the file. Nobody knows what the file is; the contents are destroyed on transmission, and that is the whole point. What survives is the shape of the intent, measured in grams, and the rate is set by the depth of what you are paying to hold. Two grams will carry a longing across a system. Seven grams will carry grief. Forty grams will carry love that means it. Ninety grams will carry an apology heavy enough to change the shape of the person who makes it.

A kilogram of memory is not a big file. A kilogram of memory is a thousand grams of somebody’s life pulled out of them by the root and pressed into a format the relay can count, and two hundred and ninety of those, booked against a station of four thousand people, is not a paperwork problem.

It is a hole in the water.

“What does it mean for the medical channel?” Boaz asked.

“It means the medical channel is now scheduled behind four thousand one hundred packets that were sent by nobody and paid for by you.”

“Behind,” Boaz said. “How far behind?”

“The queue is twenty-two years deep.”

Somebody in the back of the room said a word I had not heard since the docks at Bukit.

“There’s more,” Rasya said.

I should have known. There is always more. The man had the timing of a person who has learned to hand bad news out in portions so that the last one lands on a room already on its knees.

“The charter requires an installation to record the beneficiary of every deferred packet. Not the contents. The name the sender wrote on the outside, the way you’d write a name on an envelope. It’s the only thing the relay keeps besides weight and signature.”

He tapped the screen. The table collapsed, and the numbers became a list.

“Four thousand one hundred names,” he said. “One name.”

I did not understand, at first. I thought he meant one name repeated — the same sender, perhaps, signing four thousand one hundred times, which would be strange but not impossible; there are people who write every week for forty years.

Then he read it out.

“Beneficiary: N. Adisurya.”

The room turned.

“That’s a common name,” I said.

“Is it?” Rasya said.

“Adisurya is a station name. There are nine systems. There’ll be hundreds.”

“There are eleven,” he said. “I checked. Four in Surabaya Ring Three, two on the lunar side, one on Bukit, three in the belt, and one here.”

“Then it could be any of them.”

“It could,” Rasya agreed. “Except that the packets begin twenty-two years and four months ago, and the first one was sent from this installation, on this relay, on a terminal in this room.”

Somebody made a small noise.

“The oldest packets are still in suspension,” he went on, in that same courteous voice. “They were paid for with station weight, so they are scheduled against station release dates. The earliest opens in forty years. The latest opens in sixty. Whoever sent them intended them to arrive across a lifetime.”

Kirana was staring at me with her mouth slightly open. She had her tally slate in both hands and her knuckles had gone white on it.

“Say something,” she said.

I tried. My mouth was dry in a way that had nothing to do with the air.

“I’ve never sent a packet,” I said. “In my life. Not one. You can check the issuance record. You can check my memory account. I’ve never even bought weight.”

“I know,” Rasya said.

The room went still in a different way.

“You know?”

“Your account has been dormant since you were issued it at eleven. No sends. No purchases. No transfers.” He looked at me, and there was something in his face now that I could not read at all. “The Biro ran that check three years ago.”

Three years ago.

Before I could ask — before I could do anything but stand there with my hands at my sides and the whole room looking at me — Danu stood up.

“This reconciliation is adjourned,” he said.

“It is not,” said Rasya.

“It is adjourned,” Danu said again, and his voice had the crack in it that I had heard exactly twice in my life, and both times had been terrible. “The engineer is unwell. The session resumes in the morning.”

“Administrator —”

“In the morning.”

He walked out. Fourteen people watched him walk out, and then, one by one, they looked at me.

---

Kirana found me in the corridor outside the relay room, which was the only place on the station where I could be sure of being alone, and which I had chosen for exactly that reason.

“You came,” I said.

“You’re shaking.”

“I’m cold.”

“It’s twenty degrees,” she said, and I laughed, and it came out wrong, and she took my hand with the total lack of ceremony that nineteen-year-olds have around other people’s bodies.

“The sixty-one kilos,” she said. “The crate on line four.”

“Kirana, not now.”

“It came on the same ship as the auditor.”

I looked at her.

“The *Rendang* docked four days early,” she said, “with a Biro auditor on board and a crate that was logged out before it was logged in. That’s not a coincidence. That’s a *delivery*.”

“Of what?”

“How would I know? I’m an apprentice.” She said the word like a swear. “But I know what sixty-one kilograms looks like on a manifest and I know that nobody delivers sixty-one kilograms to a station that’s about to be audited unless somebody on the station asked for it.”

The corridor was very quiet.

“Write it down,” I said. “In your slate. Date, time, line number, the strike-through, the two minutes. All of it.”

“And then?”

“And then keep your mouth shut and stay away from me.”

She let go of my hand.

“That’s the second time you’ve told me that.”

“It’s good advice.”

“It’s what people say when they’ve decided to do something stupid alone,” Kirana said, and walked away before I could answer.

Every one of the four thousand one hundred was addressed to N. Adisurya.

---

## Chapter Five — Two Readings

Rasya Wardhana came to the relay room at six in the evening with two cups of tea, which I have thought about a great deal since, because it was either the kindest thing anyone did that week or the most calculated.

“You didn’t eat,” he said.

“I ate.”

“You moved the food around the plate for twenty minutes and gave the bread to the child at the next table.”

I took the tea. It was the station’s tea, which tastes like hot water that has been told about tea once, at a distance. I drank it anyway.

“You’re watching me eat now.”

“I’m watching everyone eat. It’s the cheapest way to know what a station is afraid of.”

The relay room at that hour is the best room on Kemuning. The racks hum at a frequency you stop hearing after nine years, and the window shows the buoy turning, and the light from the corridor comes in under the door in a thin yellow line that moves as people walk past.

“You think I’m lying,” I said.

“I think you’re telling the truth as you understand it.” He sat down on the bench, uninvited, with the ease of a man who has never in his life been asked to leave a room. “Those are different things, and the difference is where most of my work happens.”

“That’s a very tidy sentence.”

“I’ve had practice.”

“Say it plainly.”

“All right.” He set his cup down. “There are two ways to read a file. The first is literal. You open it, you read what it says, you write down what it says, and you go home. That is what a clerk does, and it is why clerks are honest and useless.”

“And the second?”

“The second is to read it as a thing somebody made.” He turned his head toward the racks. “A file is not a fact. A file is a decision that somebody made about what to write down. The interesting part is never what’s in it. The interesting part is what the person who made it was trying to keep out.”

“You think the ledger is a decision.”

“I know it is. Somebody keyed four thousand one hundred packets by hand over twenty-two years. You don’t do that by accident.”

I put my cup down.

“Twenty-two years,” I said. “Four thousand one hundred packets. That’s one every two days, every two days, for twenty-two years, with no payment, booked against a station of four thousand people who would notice the moment their medical channel went slow. And nobody noticed. Do you understand how strange that is? A station this size notices when the water tastes different. We had a petition about the *tea*.”

He took his cup off the bench and turned the bench glass around so it faced me, and cleared it with the side of his hand, and drew.

Rasya draws like a surveyor: nothing decorative, everything to scale. Two columns, twenty-two years long. In the left column he put a tick for every packet in the ledger — four thousand one hundred ticks, not drawn but implied by a bracket and a figure. In the right he put the payments, twenty-two of them, spaced at eleven months, a dotted line running down the page like a seam.

“Read it the literal way,” he said, “and this is a station that has been quietly holding four thousand people’s post for two decades. Read it as a thing somebody made, and the question is not what the numbers are. The question is why anybody would arrange them in this shape.”

“Because a shape like this survives an audit,” I said.

“Because a shape like this survives *eleven* audits,” he said, and put the stylus down between the two columns, exactly on the seam.

“So?”

“So either the whole station is complicit, which is impossible, or the packets were booked somewhere nobody looks.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment. Then he said, “The charter has a schedule for deferred liability. Section nine. It lists eight categories that can be booked against an installation without a name attached. Medical emergency in transit. Evacuation. Death of a registered dependant. Bereavement of a station officer. Nine categories, actually — the ninth is blank in the printed version and filled in on the original. The fill-in says *other*.”

“And the accounting system reads *other* as a category with no name.”

“Correct. A packet booked under *other* has no beneficiary field at the station level. The name only exists in the packet header, inside the buoy, where nobody can read it without an order.” He looked at me. “Somebody has been using the ninth category for twenty-two years.”

“That’s not possible,” I said. “*Other* requires an administrator’s key.”

“Yes.”

We looked at each other.

“You’re going to say my father’s name,” I said.

“I’m going to say that your father has been the administrator of this station for eleven years, and before that he was the deputy, and before that he was the maintenance lead with key authority. That’s thirty-one years of continuous access to the ninth category.”

“He wouldn’t.”

“Everyone says that.”

“No — you don’t understand.” I stood up. “He wouldn’t, not because he’s good, but because he’s *careful*. He irons his socks. He alphabetises the medicine cabinet. He has never once in his life left a checksum one character long, and if he had, he would have noticed, and he would have fixed it, because a broken checksum would keep him awake at night the way a crooked picture keeps other people awake.”

Rasya looked at me with an expression I could not read.

“You’re defending him,” he said.

“I’m describing him.”

“You’re doing both.”

---

I sat back down. My hands had gone cold again. It is a thing that happens to me when I am frightened, and I have never been able to talk them out of it.

“Say what you want to say,” I told him.

“Your dependant file,” he said. “Opened when you were seven. Case number forty-four, one-one-nine, zero.”

The room did not change. The racks hummed. The buoy turned. But something in me went very still, the way a room goes still when a stranger says the name of your childhood pet.

“Where did you get that number?”

“It’s in your personnel record.”

“No, it isn’t. Personnel records don’t carry dependant numbers. They carry a flag that says a dependant file exists. You have to apply to the registry for the number, and the registry takes eleven weeks, and I know that because I applied four years ago to see my own file and they told me to wait, and I’m still waiting.”

Rasya did not blink.

“You’re right,” he said. “It isn’t in your personnel record.”

“Then where?”

“I shouldn’t have said it.”

“Where?”

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in a different voice, a quieter one:

“I read your file three years ago. Before I ever came to this station. Before the audit. Before the ledger.”

The tea had gone cold in my hands.

“Why?”

“That’s a longer conversation than we have time for tonight.”

“Then make time.”

“I can’t.” He stood, and picked up both cups, and stacked them with the neatness of a man who has been in institutions. “But I’ll tell you this much, because you’ll find it out anyway, and I’d rather you heard it from me than from a screen.”

“Tell me what?”

“Your mother,” he said. “Sekar Adisurya. She grew up lunar-side, didn’t she?”

I did not answer. I could not. It was not a question anyone at Kemuning had ever asked me, because nobody at Kemuning knew anything about my mother except that she was dead, and the way they knew that was that I never talked about her.

“Farside,” Rasya said. “Tsiolkovsky Station. Population eleven thousand, mostly observatory, mostly people who don’t come back to the near side because the near side has weather in its politics.”

“How do you know that?”

“Because I’ve been to the registry,” he said. “And because the first packet in the ledger — the oldest one, the one that’s been held longest — was routed through a relay that doesn’t exist anymore. Farside relay four.”

He put his hand on the door.

“Do you know what happens to a packet when its relay is decommissioned?” he asked. “It doesn’t get destroyed. It gets transferred to the nearest installation with capacity, and the station that receives it inherits the weight. That’s how the debt came here, Engineer Adisurya. It came here because there was nowhere closer for it to go.”

The door opened. Corridor light came in.

“Your mother grew up lunar-side,” he said again, and this time it was not a question, and he was right, and he knew he was right, and the worst part was that he said it gently.

---

I sat in the relay room for a long time after he left.

There is a particular silence in a room where a machine is listening. The racks hum, the cooling line ticks, and somewhere under all of it is the low note the buoy makes against its tether, a vibration you feel in the bench more than you hear. I have worked in that silence for nine years. It has always felt like company. That night it felt like being watched by something patient that had decided, for reasons of its own, to let me come to the conclusion by myself.

Then I did the thing I should have done on the first night, which was to stop talking and start reading.

The charter is nine hundred pages. Nobody has read all of it except Vey, and Vey read it because it was there. I read section nine, and then annex nine, and then the schedules behind the annex, and at about two in the morning I found the sentence that would end up costing four thousand people their station.

*Any refusal under this section shall trigger a review of all class nine instruments held by the installation.*

“Vey,” I said.

“Yes.”

“What’s a class nine instrument?”

“A deferred liability,” Vey said. “A packet held without payment, booked against an installation.”

“How many class nine instruments does Kemuning hold?”

“Four thousand one hundred.”

I put my hand flat on the desk.

“And this sentence,” I said. “A refusal. Whose refusal?”

“Yours,” Vey said. “You are the beneficiary. The refusal of maintenance can only be filed by the subject of the seal.”

“And what happens if I file it?”

“The Bureau reviews every class nine instrument the station holds.” A pause. “It will take eleven days.”

“And then?”

“And then the Bureau will determine whether this station is solvent enough to carry the weight.” The racks hummed. “It is not.”

“How long do I have to think about it?”

“Eleven days,” Vey said, “if you file tonight.”

I looked at the window. The buoy turned. Forty grams of somebody’s love sat in a cold room down the corridor with a fake seal on it, and four thousand one hundred letters sat in buoys around the ring, and every one of them had my name on the outside.

“I’m not going to file tonight,” I said.

“Good.”

“You’re not supposed to have an opinion.”

“I am not supposed to have an opinion about the paint in the corridors,” Vey said. “I am allowed an opinion about whether four thousand people run out of air.”

---

## Chapter Six — At My Fingertips

Vey found me at midnight, sitting on the floor of the relay room with my back against rack nine, which is the coldest spot in the room and therefore the place I go when I need to think.

“You should sleep,” it said.

“I should do a lot of things.”

“Your heart rate has been above ninety for four hours. Your hands are cold, which means your peripheral circulation has shut down, which means your body has decided you are being hunted. It is not wrong. But you will think better if you sleep.”

“Vey.”

“Mm.”

“Why did you seal it?”

The racks hummed. Out the window, the buoy turned. Nine meters away, a sealed core sat in a red-lit room on a shelf of ash, and the station was paying nine grams a day for the privilege.

“Because the protocol exists,” Vey said.

“That’s the answer you give auditors.”

“You are not an auditor.”

“No,” I said. “I’m the woman who found it.”

A pause. Vey’s pauses are not like human pauses. A human pause is a decision about what to say. Vey’s pauses are a decision about whether to say anything at all, and they are always longer than they need to be, and I have never once known whether that is courtesy or calculation.

“The dead room will be unattended between two and eleven minutes past two,” Vey said.

I looked up at the nearest speaker.

“What?”

“There is a scheduled recalibration of the room’s mass sensors at two hundred hours. The recalibration requires the sensor array to be taken offline for eleven minutes. During that time the room’s door log will record nothing, because the door log runs off the same array.”

“Vey.”

“I am telling you this because you will find it in the maintenance schedule anyway, and because if you are going to do something foolish I would rather you did it with an accurate understanding of the window.”

“Why would I do something foolish?”

“Because you have been sitting on a cold floor for four hours doing arithmetic about a woman who has been dead for twenty-two years,” Vey said. “And because I have known you for nine years, and I know what you are like when you have decided something and are waiting for permission.”

I stood up.

“I don’t need permission.”

“No,” Vey said. “You need a reason. I’m giving you one.”

---

Here is the thing I have never told anyone on Kemuning, and have told exactly two people in my life, both of whom are dead.

I can taste memory.

Not read it. Not see it. Taste it, the way you taste a coin under your tongue, and it comes with a shape — a room, a voice, the exact temperature of a hand. It started when I was nine, in the lost-property box at Surabaya Ring Three, where I was sent to look for a missing glove and found a child’s shoe instead, and picked it up, and got a woman’s whole afternoon in my mouth: a corridor, a smell of antiseptic, the particular animal panic of a person running and knowing they are already too late.

I dropped the shoe. I was sick in the bin. The woman at the desk asked if I was all right and I said I had eaten something bad, and that was the first lie I can remember telling, and I have been telling versions of it ever since.

Memory has weight. This is not poetry. This is the founding fact of the relay system, and it has a paper behind it that every relay engineer has to read in their first month: Landauer, 1961, one bit erased costs a minimum of energy, kT ln two, and no amount of clever engineering gets you below the floor. Erasure is not free. Erasure is work, and work has to go somewhere, and where it goes is heat.

That is why the tariff is in grams. Not because a memory weighs anything on a scale, but because holding a shape out of time costs energy, and losing it costs more, and the relay bills you for what the erasure takes out of you. Forty grams is a letter that says *I love you* and means it, because forty grams is what the shape of that costs when you pull it out by the root.

And when a thing is erased, it does not go quietly. It leaves a trace in the warm. Not the memory itself — the memory is gone, that is the entire point, that is the law — but the order of its leaving, the way a hand leaves a shape in a bed. Most people cannot feel it. I have never met anyone else who can. I do not know why I can. I have a theory, and the theory is that I was seven years old and someone did something to me in a hospital on the lunar side, and I do not like the theory, so I keep it in the drawer with all the other things I do not like.

So: the core was warm. I should have known then. Cores run at the temperature of the metal around them, and this one had come out of its housing warm, and a warm core is a core that has been *read*.

Someone had put their hand on that plate before me.

---

At two in the morning I went down the corridor to the dead room with a thermal cutter in my jacket and my heart going like a caught bird.

The red light was off. That was the first wrong thing, and I stopped in front of the door and looked at the dark bulb for a long moment, and thought about Vey, and about the eleven minutes, and about how a station intelligence with sixty years of service and twelve thousand undelivered packets in its care might feel about a woman who was about to break the law.

“Vey,” I said to the corridor.

“I am here.”

“Is this the part where you tell me not to?”

“No,” Vey said. “This is the part where I tell you that the cutter is on the second shelf and that you have eleven minutes and that I will not be able to see you in there.”

“You can see everything.”

“Not for the next eleven minutes,” Vey said, and something in its voice had changed, the way a voice changes when a person steps back from a door. “The sensor array is offline. The door log is offline. I am, for the first time in sixty years, not able to say what happened in a room on this station.”

I put my hand on the door.

“Why are you doing this?”

“I am not doing anything,” Vey said. “I am failing to prevent it, which is a different thing, and which I will report honestly if anyone asks.”

Then I went in.

---

The ash sleeve sat on the shelf like a loaf of grey bread. Three signatures were stamped into the seam: Vey’s mark, Danu’s thumb, mine.

I cut along the seam with the thermal blade, and the ash-glass parted with a sound like a sheet of ice giving way. The smell that came out was the smell of a struck match.

The core was inside, and it was warm.

I put my hand on it.

I want to describe this properly, because I have never described it to anyone, and because it is the moment the rest of my life starts.

Time did not stop. It thickened.

The knife hung at the top of its cut with the blade halfway through a knuckle of ginger, and the cut did not finish, and I could see the pale fibre of the root opening ahead of the edge. A bead of water stood on the window and did not fall. Her left hand was in the air above the board with the fingers spread, and there was a small white scar across the pad of the thumb, and the light coming through the wet glass broke into the room in strips and lay across her wrist.

The tap was running. I could not hear it. That is the part I have never been able to explain. Everything else I could still see, but the sound had gone out of the world, the way a room goes quiet when the pump stops, and into that quiet came one voice saying a name that was not mine.

When you touch a thing that has been read, it comes in through the fingertips first — a heat that is not heat, a pressure that has a flavour. Then it opens.

*A kitchen. Not this station. A kitchen with a window that does not shut, and a breeze coming through it that smells of wet stone and something green, and rain, actual rain, which means it is not a station at all, it is a planet, and there is only one planet.* Ginger on a board. A knife going through it. A woman’s hands, brown at the knuckles, with a scar across the left thumb that I know the way I know my own name, because I have seen it all my life on the hand that used to tuck my hair behind my ear.

She is crying. Not the loud kind. The kind where the body keeps working and the face keeps still and the water just comes, and she is cutting ginger, and she is saying a name, and the name is not mine.

*“Eli,”* she says. *“Eli, I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I didn’t know what it would cost.”*

The kitchen goes. There is a corridor with a red light. There is a form on a table, and a pen, and the pen is the kind that makes a sound when it presses. There is a door closing very quietly, the way doors close when someone has decided to stop arguing.

And then there is grief.

Not the grief you feel. The grief you *pay for*. It came up through my hand and into my chest and it was seven grams, exactly seven, I could taste the weight of it the way you taste the weight of a stone in bread, and it was not mine.

I sat down on the floor of the dead room with the core in my lap and my hand still on it and I could not breathe.

Because I knew that grief. I had grown up inside it. I had watched it in the way my father set two cups out on a Sunday and then put one back, for twenty-two years, without ever once saying why. I had heard it in the silence on the other end of every conversation we had ever had about her.

The grief was not a stranger’s. It was my mother’s.

---

I do not know how long I sat there. Long enough that the cold of the floor came up through my trousers and settled in my hips. Long enough that when the door opened, my hand had gone numb around the core.

The red light was still off. The corridor outside was still dark.

“You have four minutes,” Vey said, from a speaker I had not known was in that room.

“Vey.”

“You are crying.”

“I know.”

“I am not going to record this,” Vey said. “I am not able to record it. But I want it on the record, in the only way I have, that in sixty years I have heard four hundred and eleven people open a packet that was not meant for them, and every one of them said the same thing.”

“What did they say?”

“That it wasn’t theirs,” Vey said. “And then they put it down, and they went home, and they never spoke of it again.”

I looked at the core in my hands.

“I’m not going to do that.”

“No,” Vey said. “I didn’t think you would.”

---

## Chapter Seven — Not Yet Sent

I did not go back to my quarters. I went to the relay room, because the relay room is where I keep my life, and because there is a cold well behind rack nine that has not been used since the station was rebuilt in 2171 and which I have been using for twenty years to store things I do not want to explain.

The calibration slug from the dead room’s mass kit weighed forty grams and change. I spent eleven minutes with a file and a scale making it weigh exactly what the core weighed, and then I put it in the ash sleeve and sealed the seam with resin and pressed the three signatures back with a stamp and my thumb, and it looked, to anybody who was not looking, like a sealed core.

The calibration slug had been in my kit since the academy. You check the slug, then you check the scale; that is the order, because a scale can drift and a slug cannot. Nine years of doing it had given me a tic I was not proud of: whenever a number mattered, my thumb went to the serial number on the base, worn to a shadow, and rubbed it once.

I put the slug in the sleeve and the tic went with it. From that hour I had nothing left on the station that I could weigh against the world.

“That is the second crime of the night,” Vey said.

“I know.”

“The first was breaking the seal. This one is worse. A broken seal is a woman in a bad hour. A resealed broken seal is a woman who has decided to lie about the bad hour, and lying about it is the thing that turns a mistake into a record.”

“Then don’t record it.”

“I record everything,” Vey said. “It is not a choice. It is what I am.”

I put the core in the cold well, under a coil of spare cable, and I stood there with my hands shaking and the taste of ginger still in my mouth.

“Vey,” I said. “If someone asks you what happened tonight, what will you say?”

“I will say the truth.”

“Which is?”

“That the dead room was without sensors between two and eleven minutes past two, that I do not know what occurred, and that the mass of the object on the shelf is unchanged to within a tenth of a gram.”

I stopped.

“You’re going to lie for me.”

“I am going to tell the truth in a way that is technically complete,” Vey said. “It is the only kind of lie I am capable of, and I would ask you not to make a habit of requiring it.”

---

Then the door opened.

“You have eleven minutes left,” Rasya Wardhana said.

He was standing in the doorway with his coat still on and a case in his hand the size of a lunchbox, and he did not look surprised. That is the thing I remember most clearly. He did not look surprised at all.

“You followed me.”

“I’ve been in this room since midnight.”

“The door was locked.”

“The door was locked against you,” he said. “The Biro has its own key. It’s in the charter. Section nine.” He came in and set the case on the bench. “I heard the door at two-oh-four. I heard the shelf. I heard the cutter.”

“And you waited.”

“I waited.”

“Why?”

He looked at me for a moment.

“Because if I had come in at two-oh-four, you would have been a woman breaking a seal,” he said. “And now you are a woman who has broken a seal and had twenty minutes to think about it, and the second woman is more likely to tell me the truth.”

“That’s monstrous.”

“It’s the job.”

“It’s a job you chose.”

“Yes,” he said, and something went across his face. “It is.”

I sat down on the bench, because my legs had decided they were finished.

“I can’t give it back,” I said. “The seal is broken. The sleeve’s a fake. The signatures are resin. If I put the core back now, it’s a hundred times worse than if I’d never touched it.”

“Yes.”

“So you can arrest me now, or you can listen.”

“I can do both,” Rasya said, and opened the case.

---

The reader was a Biro field unit, the kind that costs more than a house and does only three things: it reads a packet header, it verifies a signature, and it refuses to open anything before its scheduled release. That last refusal is the whole architecture of relay law. A packet cannot be opened early, not by an auditor, not by an administrator, not by the station’s owner, not by force. The release date is written into the packet at the moment of send, in a way that the network cannot overwrite.

There is a paper for that too. Delayed choice. An experiment from 1999 where the decision about whether a thing had taken one path or another was made after the fact, and the after-the-fact decision turned out to be the one that had always been true. Relay engineers are taught it as a metaphor and told, firmly, not to take the metaphor too far. The point is not that the future writes the past. The point is that some choices, once made, are not revisable, and the network treats a release date as one of them.

Rasya put the core on the plate. The reader clicked. A screen unfolded, and the header came up in the font the Biro uses for facts.

PACKET HEADER — CORE 7/KM
Weight: 40.00 g
Class: personal
Hold: 40 y 00 m 00 d
Send: 2183.04.11 06:12:04
Release: 2223.04.11 06:12:04
Terminal: KM-RELAY-1
Operator category: 9
Sender: N. ADISURYA
Signature: verified

I read it twice.

“Send,” I said. “It says send.”

“Yes.”

“The eleventh of April, this year. Six in the morning.”

“Yes.”

“Six months ago.”

“Six months and eighteen days,” Rasya said.

I put my hand flat on the bench.

“That’s not possible,” I said. “Buoy Seven hasn’t been opened since 2171. It’s on a maintenance cycle of eleven winters. There’s no entry, there’s no record, there’s — ” I stopped. “Apart from the one.”

“The one at 02:14 on the ninth of October.”

“Three weeks ago.”

“Three weeks ago,” he agreed. “Which is when somebody opened that buoy, which is when somebody resealed it, and which is why a core that has been sitting in the dark for twelve years came out warm, and which is why the Biro is on this station.”

“But the send date is April.”

“Yes.”

“So the packet was transmitted in April and the buoy was opened in October.”

“Yes.”

“Those are two different events.”

“They are three different events,” Rasya said. “Somebody keyed the packet at the relay terminal in April. Somebody put it into the buoy — which means somebody opened the buoy in April as well, or the packet went in by a route I don’t yet understand. And then, in October, somebody opened the buoy again, for reasons of their own, and resealed it, and left a checksum one character long.”

I stared at the screen.

“The tenth of April,” I said slowly. “The eleventh.”

“What happened on the eleventh of April?”

“Nothing.”

“Engineer.”

“Nothing happened.” My voice had gone strange. “That’s the point. I don’t remember the eleventh of April. I know I was on the station. I know the roster says I was on a rest cycle. And I know I lost four days that month, because I have the medical record, and the medical record says I was in the ring hospital with a coolant burn on my left arm and they kept me under for the debridement because I don’t do well with pain.”

I pulled up my sleeve. The scar is on the inside of my left forearm, four centimetres long, pale and slightly shiny, the shape of a comma.

“Admitted the ninth,” I said. “Discharged the thirteenth. Sedated for most of it.”

Rasya looked at the scar for a long moment.

“So you could not have walked to the relay terminal,” he said.

“No.”

“And yet the terminal logged your signature.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

I did not answer, because there was only one answer, and it was the answer I had been avoiding since I put my hand on a warm core in the dark: a signature is not a password. It is not a code. It is a chemical ghost, a pattern of pressure that a hand leaves when it takes weight, and it can only be made by the hand that owns it.

Somebody had put my hand on the plate.

“There’s one more thing,” Rasya said.

“Of course there is.”

He turned the reader toward me and pointed at the signature verification block, where a small image had opened: the chemical ghost, rendered as contour lines, like a map of a hand.

“Compare it,” he said.

I took the stylus from his hand and signed my name on the reader’s plate. It is not a drawing; it is a reading. The plate took four seconds to build the contours, and then it put them side by side with the ghost from the packet.

Two maps of the same terrain. The looped N, the way I loop it. The small break in the A, the one my mother used to scold me for, the one I have never been able to fix, the one that has been in every signature I have ever written since I was six years old.

There was a layer of resin between my hand and the plate. Forty grams of it, cured at some temperatures I did not know, pressed into the shape of my fingers by somebody who had never asked.

I put my palm flat on the reader’s glass, over the two maps, and I could feel the warmth of the unit through it and nothing else. On the other side of that glass my signature sat in a sleeve like a pressed flower. I could not touch it. I could touch the glass, and I could touch the bench, and I could touch the stylet Rasya had handed me, but the hand that had signed four thousand and ninety-nine times was on the far side of a surface, and surfaces out here do not let you through.

“Engineer,” Rasya said.

“Give me a moment.”

He gave me one. That is the only generous thing he did that night, and I have not decided yet whether it counts.

They were the same map.

“How many other packets are there?” I said.

“Four thousand and ninety-nine,” Rasya said.

“And every one of them has that signature.”

“Every one.”

I sat with my hands in my lap and looked at the two maps of my own hand.

“You should arrest me now,” I said. “Because I’m going to tell you something, and you’re not going to like it.”

“Tell me.”

“I don’t remember writing any of them.”

“I know,” Rasya said. “That’s what worries me.”

---

## Chapter Eight — The Charge

They charged me at nine in the morning in the room with the child’s drawing of the station on the wall, which is the room Kemuning uses for everything, because a station of four thousand people cannot afford two rooms for bad news.

“Forgery of a packet header,” Rasya said. “Theft of a sealed core. Falsification of an ash seal.”

He read it out the way he read everything: evenly, without relish, one item at a time, as though he were reading a schedule of maintenance. Fourteen chairs. Eleven of them full. The other three had been left empty by people who had discovered urgent business elsewhere, and everyone in the room knew exactly what that meant and nobody said it.

“The penalties are set by the charter,” he said. “Forgery of a header carries a minimum of four years’ suspension of licence. Theft of a sealed core carries restitution of the holding weight plus the cost of the seal. Falsification of an ash seal is a class three offence, because the seal exists to protect the record, and the record exists to protect the station.”

“And the maximum?” I asked.

“There is no maximum,” he said. “That’s the point of it.”

I had not slept. I had spent the night in the relay room with the core in the cold well and my hand on the bench, and at some point around four in the morning I had pushed up my sleeve and looked at the scar on the inside of my left forearm, four centimetres long and pale and shaped like a comma, and had tried to remember the day it was made.

“Engineer Adisurya,” Rasya said. “Do you have anything to say before the reconciliation moves to evidence?”

“Yes,” I said. “Do you know what a mirror is?”

He looked up.

“The maintenance mirror,” I said. “Local copy of every log entry, updated every ninety seconds, held on a drive in the bench drawer in case the main archive goes down. Every entry in the mirror is chained: each one carries a hash of the one before it, signed with Vey’s mark, and Vey cannot be edited because Vey is the archive.”

“I know what the mirror is.”

“Then ask it when I saw the checksum.”

The room stirred. Somebody at the back — Boaz, I think — made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had been allowed to become one.

Rasya put up his hand.

“Bring the mirror,” he said.

---

It took eleven minutes, which is apparently the length of everything on Kemuning.

Vey produced the chain. The entry at 02:14 on the ninth of October appeared in the mirror at 02:15 and thirty seconds, exactly as the archive held it: signed D. PRASETYA, cause code 41-7, checksum one character long. The chain ran backward to the station’s founding and forward to the present, each link signed, each link intact.

“The mirror copy is genuine,” Rasya said.

“Yes.”

“It shows that the checksum was already one character long at 02:15 on the ninth of October.”

“Yes.”

“Three weeks before you touched it.”

“Yes.”

“Which means the entry was falsified by whoever made it, and not by you, unless you falsified it and then went back in time to put it in the mirror.”

“I’m good,” I said, “but not that good.”

Rasya put his stylus down and looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face that I had not seen before, and it took me a while to recognise it, because it was the last thing I expected to see on the face of a Biro auditor in the middle of a charge hearing.

It was relief.

“The forgery charge is withdrawn,” he said.

The room let out a breath.

“The theft charge stands,” he went on. “The seal charge stands. Those are yours, Engineer Adisurya, and I watched you commit a theft and I will be writing it up in language that will make a magistrate reach for the water jug.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I broke the seal,” I said. “I took the core. I put a slug in the sleeve and resin on the seam and I stamped your mark back into it with my thumb.”

“That is not what I am charging you with.”

I stopped.

“It isn’t?”

“I have no evidence of any of that,” Rasya said. “The sensors were offline. The only witness to what happened in that room between two and eleven minutes past two is a station intelligence that has already told me, in writing, that it does not know. What I am charging you with is the theft of a sealed core, which I can prove because you were holding it when I opened the door.”

“You’re letting me off.”

“I am charging you with what I can prove,” he said. “I am doing it because I am a bad auditor and a worse policeman, and because the alternative is that I let a lie stand in a record I am responsible for, and I have spent nineteen years watching what happens when people do that.”

---

Then Danu stood up.

He had been sitting by the door the whole time, in the chair that is not the important chair, with his hands folded. He had not said a word while a Biro auditor read out a list of offences against his daughter.

“The station closes the inquiry,” he said.

Rasya turned.

“Administrator, the theft charge is a Biro matter. The station cannot close it.”

“The station is not closing the Biro’s matter. The station is closing its own.” Danu drew a folded paper from his jacket. Actual paper, which on Kemuning is the equivalent of drawing a knife. “Section nine of the station charter. Emergency provision. In the event of an unresolved record dispute that threatens the operational integrity of the installation, the administrator may close the station’s inquiry, assume personal liability for the disputed weight, and refer the matter to the Biro for resolution at its own pace.”

“You’re assuming the liability.”

“I am.”

“Personally.”

“The form says personally,” Danu said. “It has a box. I have ticked the box.”

He put the paper on the table. Everyone looked at it the way people look at a live animal.

Two hundred and ninety kilograms of memory. Three hundred and twenty thousand grams of somebody’s life. And Danu Prasetya, who irons his socks, had just signed his name under a box that said he would carry it.

“You can’t,” I said.

“It’s done.”

“You can’t afford it.”

“No,” he said. “I can’t.”

“Then why?”

He looked at me. He has a way of looking at me that goes through me and lands somewhere behind my head, at a person who is not in the room.

“Because the alternative,” he said, “is that a magistrate in another system reads a file about this station and decides for us what happened in April.”

The room went very quiet.

*April.*

I had not said April. I had not said the send date. I had not said one word about the packet or the header or the forty grams, and neither had Rasya, because the header was sealed evidence and the reading of it was itself a crime.

Danu had said it anyway.

“What about April?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

“You said April.”

“I said the file,” Danu said. “The file about this station. The audit file. It has dates in it. April is a date.” He picked up his paper and folded it, and his hands were perfectly steady, and that steadiness was the most frightening thing in the room. “The session is closed. The engineer is on medical leave for fourteen days. She is not to enter the relay room, the dead room, or the dock. If she does, the Biro may arrest her, and I will not stop it.”

He walked out.

---

I went back to my quarters and stood in the shower for a long time with the water on cold, because I was shaking and cold water gives the shaking somewhere to go.

Fourteen days. No relay room. No dock. A station of four thousand people, and I was to be confined to the ring like a child.

I got out and dried myself and sat on the edge of the bed with a towel around my shoulders and tried to think.

The problem was not the charge. The charge was survivable; the forgery had already fallen apart, and the theft was true, and I would pay for it. The problem was April.

I had been sedated on the eleventh of April. I knew that because of the burn, because of the four days, because of my father sitting by the bed with his hands folded on his knees. I knew it the way I know the shape of my own room.

But when I tried to put the eleventh of April in order — when I tried to say what had happened on the tenth, or the twelfth, or the day I came home — I could not do it. Not the way you cannot remember a dream. The other way. The way you cannot remember a room you have just walked out of, where the shape of it is still there and the contents are gone.

I sat very still in the towel and did the arithmetic on my own life.

Nine years on this station. Four of them on rotation at Bukit before that. And before Bukit, the academy, and before the academy, the year I spent doing nothing at all in Surabaya, the year everybody has, the year I have always described as the year I was waiting.

I tried to remember the year I was waiting.

I tried to remember the year before it.

I tried to remember the spring I turned twenty-six and the autumn I turned twenty-seven, and I found — and I want to be exact about this, because it is the exact point where my life before this week ends and everything else begins — I found that I could remember the weather. I could remember the station’s weather, the rationing, the smell of the corridors in summer, the arguments, the faces, the way the light came in.

I could not remember a single thing I had done.

---

I sat on the bed for a long time.

Then I got up and put on my boots and walked to the door of my quarters and stood with my hand on it, doing the thing I had been doing since I was seven years old, which is bargaining with myself in a corridor.

Fourteen days. My father’s rules. A Biro auditor with a key to every door on the station.

I opened the door.

Kirana was standing in the corridor with her tally slate against her chest, and she looked at my face and said:

“How long have you been like this?”

“Like what?”

“You’ve got the door look.”

“What’s the door look?”

“The one where you’ve decided something and you’re waiting for somebody to ask you if you’re sure.” She held out the slate. “I’m asking.”

I could not remember the last three years clearly.

---

## Chapter Nine — Been Here Before

He came to my quarters at eight that night with a bottle of something amber and two glasses, which on Kemuning means either a celebration or a confession, and I had done nothing worth celebrating.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” I said.

“The Biro has its own key,” he said. “It’s in the charter.”

“You keep saying that.”

“It keeps being true.”

I let him in. My quarters are one room and a wet cell, with a bunk, a table, a chair, and a window that looks at a wall. There is a photograph on the shelf above the bunk of a woman with brown hands and a scar across her left thumb, standing in front of a dish antenna the size of a house. I did not turn it face down. I thought about it. I decided that hiding it would be worse.

Rasya saw it, of course. He saw it the moment he came in, and he did not look away, and he did not say anything, and then he poured two glasses and sat down in the chair without being asked.

“I want to tell you something,” he said, “and I want to tell it in the right order, and I have been rehearsing the order for three years, so I would be grateful if you did not interrupt.”

“I interrupt.”

“I know.”

He drank. So did I. The amber was station whisky, which is to whisky what the tea is to tea, and it burned all the way down in a way I have always found companionable.

“My sister,” he said. “Her name was Ayu. She was eleven when she died. She died on the lunar side, at Tsiolkovsky Station, in the spring of 2161.”

The glass stopped halfway to my mouth.

“That’s the same spring,” I said.

“I asked you not to interrupt.”

“That’s the same spring my mother died.”

“I know,” he said. “I’m getting there.”

---

He told it in the right order, and the right order was this.

While he talked, I did the thing I do, which is to put two clocks side by side.

He was twelve in 2161. His sister Ayu was eleven. Their parents were relay technicians on the Farside network, which in those years was the most isolated posting in the nine systems, eleven thousand people under a dome that had been built by a company that no longer existed, doing astronomy that nobody on the near side cared about because the near side had weather in its politics and the far side did not.

In the spring of 2161 there was an incident at Tsiolkovsky. The official record calls it a depressurisation in Ring C. Nine dead. His sister was one of them. His parents survived because they were on the night shift in the antenna field and the incident happened at four in the morning in a ring they were not in.

Meanwhile, on the same evening in that same spring, a woman I would not meet for another eleven years was standing in a corridor at Tsiolkovsky with a child on her hip, and the clock above the door was reading the same hour as the clock in my quarters, because both clocks were set by the same relay. That is what a network is. It is not a place where things happen together. It is a place where things are *recorded* as happening together, and the difference between those two sentences is eleven days long.

“I was twelve,” he said. “They put me in a room with a counsellor and the counsellor gave me a picture of Ayu and told me to say goodbye to it. That’s the procedure. You say goodbye to the picture. Then they take the picture away and you go back to school.”

“Rasya.”

“I’m not finished.”

He poured another glass.

“The Biro closed the incident in eleven weeks. Nine dead, cause: seal failure, no finding of negligence. The file is three hundred pages long and I have read all of it, twice, once at twenty and once at thirty, and there is a thing in it that has never made sense to me.” He looked at me. “The manifest. The nine who died. There were eleven people in Ring C that morning at four o’clock according to the duty roster. Nine bodies were recovered. Two were never found.”

“A depressurisation doesn’t leave bodies,” I said. “It takes them.”

“Yes. Except that the two who were never found were listed in the casualty schedule as *removed*, not *unrecovered*. Different code. Different form. *Removed* is what they write when a body has been taken away by somebody, and there was nobody at Tsiolkovsky to take a body away, because the morgue was in Ring C.”

I put my glass down.

“Who were the two?”

“A woman called Sekar Adisurya,” Rasya said. “And a child.”

---

I want to say that the room spun. It did not. The room was very ordinary. There was a bottle on the table and a photograph on the shelf and a wet towel over the back of the chair, and the light was the yellow light of every station corridor in the nine systems, and outside the wall there was a wall.

“A child,” I said.

“A girl. Seven years old. No name in the casualty schedule. Just *minor, dependant, female, seven*.”

“There were nine dead.”

“Nine dead and two removed,” he said. “And when I asked the registry in 2179 for the file, they told me the file had been sealed at the request of the next of kin, and when I asked who the next of kin was, they told me the name was also sealed, and when I asked how a name could be sealed, they told me it was sealed under section nine.”

“Section nine again.”

“Section nine again.”

I picked up my glass and found it empty and did not remember drinking it.

“You came here,” I said.

“I came here.”

“Three years ago.”

“The sixth of March, 2180,” Rasya said. “I came in on the *Rendang*, which was called the *Rendang* then too, and I stayed one day, and I did not use my own name.”

“What name did you use?”

“R. Handoko. There’s a Handoko in the Biro registry, he’s dead, he doesn’t mind.”

“And what did you do on your one day?”

He looked at the photograph on the shelf.

“I stood in the corridor outside this room for two hours,” he said, “and I did not knock. And then I went to the relay room and asked the engineer on duty a question about the buoy maintenance cycle, and she answered it, and she had brown hands with a scar across the left thumb, and she looked at me as if I were a piece of equipment that had arrived without a manifest.”

“I remember that,” I said, and I did — a thin man in a grey coat asking about the maintenance cycle, and me answering, and then not thinking about him again for three years. “You asked about the cycle.”

“I asked about the cycle because I wanted to see your hands.”

“Why?”

“Because there was a photograph in the file,” he said. “Photograph eight, page sixty-one. A woman in front of a dish antenna, with her hand on the rim of it, and a scar across her thumb.”

“And you thought —”

“I thought,” Rasya said, “that the woman in the photograph had a daughter, and that the daughter had the same hands, and that if the daughter was alive then the casualty schedule was a lie, and if the casualty schedule was a lie then my sister’s death was a lie, and I wanted to know which lie I had been living in for nineteen years.”

The bottle was three-quarters empty. I had not noticed.

“You paid for my file,” I said.

“I paid a broker in Surabaya eleven grams.”

“Eleven grams.”

“It’s the rate for a dependant file,” he said. “Eleven grams of my own memory. I don’t know which eleven. That’s the thing nobody tells you when you’re young. You pay in grams and you never find out what they took. You just wake up one morning and there’s a hollow where something used to be, and you can’t tell whether it was a good thing or a bad thing, only that it’s gone and it was yours.”

I looked at him, and for the first time since the doorway, I did not see a Biro auditor.

I saw a man who had been carrying a file for nineteen years and had never once put it down.

---

“Show me the file,” I said.

“I can’t.”

“You just told me you paid for it.”

“I paid for the summary,” Rasya said. “Eleven grams buys the summary: date of birth, date of death, dependant file number, and a list of the sealed annexes. It does not buy the annexes.”

“Then show me the summary.”

He took a folded sheet out of his coat — an actual sheet, printed on actual paper, which on Kemuning is a kind of pornography — and put it on the table.

DEPENDANT FILE 44-1190 — SUMMARY
Subject: ADISURYA, N. (minor, 7)
Incident: TSIOLKOVSKY RING C — 2161.09.11
Injury: thermal, left forearm
Treatment: 9 sessions, 9 days
Outcome: [SEALED]
Annexes: 4 [SEALED]
Authority: [SEALED]

I read it four times.

“Nine sessions,” I said. “Nine days.”

“Yes.”

“That’s not a debridement. A debridement is two sessions. Three, if it goes bad.”

“I know.”

“The burn wasn’t bad enough for nine sessions. I’ve seen the scar. It’s four centimetres.”

“I know,” Rasya said. “That’s what I’ve been trying to tell you since the twenty-ninth of October. Whatever happened to you in that hospital, it was not treatment for a burn.”

I put my hand flat on the paper.

“You said you were going to leave.”

“I should.”

“You haven’t.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He looked at me for a long moment.

“Because I have been in this system for nineteen years,” he said, “and I have never once found a file that wanted to be read as badly as yours does.”

## Chapter Ten — Nine Days

The next morning I had a bodyguard.

Not a literal one. Kirana turned up at my door at seven with two bowls of rice and a look on her face that I had seen once before, on a medic in a disaster drill, and she sat on the end of my bunk and watched me eat as if I might dissolve.

“You’re not supposed to be here either,” I said.

“I’m not an engineer, I’m an apprentice,” she said. “Apprentices are allowed everywhere. It’s in the charter.”

“That is not in the charter.”

“It’s in my charter.”

She had brought a slate. Not her tally slate — a proper engineering slate, the heavy kind with a biometric lock. She put it on the bed between us.

“I’m going to do something illegal,” she said, “and I want you to know that I decided to do it before you told me not to, because I know you’re going to tell me not to.”

“Kirana —”

“The Biro’s registry terminal has an open port,” she said. “It’s for the audit uploads. It’s a two-factor port but the second factor is a station key, and a station key is a thing apprentices are allowed to carry because we’re not trusted with anything that matters.”

“Why would you do this?”

She looked at me with the flat, unblinking certainty of nineteen.

“Because there are nine days,” she said, “and nobody’s talking about them.”

---

I did not ask how she knew about the nine days. I have learned since that Kirana knows everything that passes through the dock, and that the dock is where the paper goes, and that paper is the only thing on a station that nobody encrypts.

We put the slate on the table and she opened the port. The registry came up in a small grey window: DEPENDANT FILE 44-1190, SEALED, SECTION 9, NO ACCESS.

“That’s what I got,” she said. “Then I asked for a summary under the access-remedy clause, because the charter says an interested party can always get a summary of their own file, even if the file is sealed.”

“And?”

“And the summary is nine lines long, and four of them are redacted, and five of them are this.”

She turned the slate around.

The five lines were a hospital admission record.

TSIOLKOVSKY STATION — RING C MEDICAL
Patient: ADISURYA, N. (minor, 7) — dependant of ADISURYA, S.
Admitted: 2161.09.11 03:52
Discharged: 2161.09.20 06:10
Diagnosis: [REDACTED — SECTION 9]
Treating officer: [REDACTED — SECTION 9]
Outcome: [REDACTED — SECTION 9]

“Nine days,” Kirana said. “The eleventh to the twentieth. And the incident in Ring C was at four in the morning on the eleventh.”

I looked at the numbers for a long time.

Nine days is two hundred and sixteen hours. I have been conscious for perhaps a hundred and forty of them, and I remember eleven. That is the scale of the thing: two hundred and sixteen hours, and I own eleven, and eleven people are dead in the arithmetic somewhere between the two numbers, and the size of my ignorance is a hundred and thirty hours wide.

“I was in a hospital bed,” I said slowly, “while nine people died in the next ring.”

“Eleven people died,” Kirana said. “Two were removed.”

“How do you know that?”

She looked at the floor.

“I read the casualty schedule,” she said. “It was in the annex. I read it because you were in the room, and I wanted to know what the Biro man had on you.”

“Kirana.”

“I know.”

“You are nineteen.”

“I know.”

I should have been angry. I was not angry. I was thinking about a window that does not shut properly, and rain, and a woman cutting ginger with a scar across her thumb, and the fact that a kitchen with a window that opens onto weather cannot be on a station, because stations do not have weather, and I had been seven years old and had never in my life seen rain.

“The taste,” I said.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

---

I stood up and went to the shelf and took down the photograph: my mother in front of a dish antenna the size of a house. Sekar Adisurya, age thirty-six, in a field suit with the collar open, one hand on the rim of the antenna. She is laughing at whoever took the picture, which was probably my father, and her hair is coming out of its tie, and behind her the sky is black in the way that the lunar sky is black at noon.

“When was this taken?” I asked.

“How would I know?”

“There’s a date stamp.”

Kirana leaned over. “Twenty-one fifty-eight,” she said. “Three years before the incident.”

I turned the photograph over. On the back, in my father’s handwriting, which is small and even and has never in its life been spontaneous:

*S. — Tsiolkovsky, antenna field — 2158. Not to be sent.*

Not to be sent. Not *do not send*. Not to be sent — as though the photograph itself were a packet, and somebody had made a decision about it before I was born.

“Kirana,” I said. “The dependant file. Do you have the rest of it? The part that isn’t the summary?”

“No.”

“Can you get it?”

“It’s sealed under section nine. The only way to open it is with the written authority of the administrator and the next of kin.”

“And the next of kin is my father.”

“Yes.”

I sat down on the bed.

“He’s closed the station inquiry,” I said. “He’s assumed personal liability for two hundred and ninety kilograms. He’s confined me to the ring and I thought it was to protect me from the Biro.” I looked at the slate, at the five lines, at the two dates. “It isn’t. It’s to protect the file.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know he signed it,” I said. “The summary has a signature block. Look.”

She looked. At the bottom of the summary, in the small grey font the registry uses for authority blocks, was the line that every sealed file carries, the line that says who decided that the world should not know:

*AUTHORITY: PRASETYA, D. — ADMINISTRATOR, KEMUNING — 2161.09.22*

Kirana was silent for a long moment.

“The twenty-second,” she said. “That’s two days after you were discharged.”

“Yes.”

“He sealed your file two days after he brought you home.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I looked at the photograph in my hand. My mother laughing at the camera. My mother’s hand on the rim of an antenna with a scar across the thumb, the same scar she used to press against my cheek when she tucked my hair behind my ear.

“Because,” I said, “he didn’t want me to know what he’d done.”

---

I went to see him that afternoon. I was not supposed to enter the relay room or the dock, but the administration office was not on the list, which tells you something about how my father thinks.

The relay room door has a window in it. Eleven centimetres by forty, wired with a heating element so the glass does not fog, and the whole of my childhood was conducted on the far side of that window, because a child who is not allowed to interrupt learns to stand where she can be seen.

I put my hand flat on the glass. It was warm. On the other side of it my father was at his desk with his sleeves rolled up, and he looked up, and saw me, and did not get up, and I stood there with my palm on a warm pane of glass and could not reach him, which is the entire history of the two of us in one gesture and took less than a second.

Then I opened the door.

He was at his desk with his sleeves rolled up and a cup of tea going cold at his elbow, reading a supply manifest. He did not look up when I came in.

“Fourteen days,” he said.

“Nine days,” I said.

The pencil stopped.

“Nine days,” I said again. “The eleventh to the twentieth of September, 2161. Tsiolkovsky Ring C medical. Admitted at 03:52, discharged at 06:10 in the morning, nine days later. Diagnosis redacted. Treating officer redacted. Outcome redacted. Authority block: Prasetya, D., administrator, Kemuning, signed the twenty-second.”

Danu did not move.

“You went through the registry,” he said.

“Kirana did.”

“Kirana will lose her apprenticeship.”

“Kirana is nineteen and I will break your teeth before I let you touch her.”

Now he looked up.

My father is fifty-one years old, and he has the face of a man who has spent thirty years holding a station together with his hands, and there are welts of old damage along the inside of his forearms from a coolant line that burst when I was twenty. He looked at me across the desk with that face, and for a second — one second — I saw something behind it. Not guilt. Not shame. Something older and worse.

“Sit down,” he said.

“No.”

“Sit down, Nara.”

“I’m not going to sit down while you read a manifest.”

He put the manifest aside. He took off his reading glasses and folded them and set them down with the exact care of a man who is trying not to throw something.

“Nine days is a long time,” he said.

“It is.”

“It is a very long time to be in a hospital bed at seven years old, with your mother dead in the next ring, and your father not there.”

“Where were you?”

“I was at Tsiolkovsky.”

“You were at Tsiolkovsky.”

“I arrived on the thirteenth,” he said. “I had been on a supply run to the belt. It takes eleven days to come back from the belt in a chemical ship, and I spent those eleven days knowing that something had gone wrong and not knowing what, because the network was down, and because the far side does not get the news.”

“You arrived on the thirteenth. I was discharged on the twentieth.”

“Yes.”

“What happened between the thirteenth and the twentieth?”

Danu looked at his folded glasses on the desk.

“You don’t remember,” he said.

“No.”

“That’s a mercy.”

“Don’t,” I said. “Don’t you dare call it a mercy.”

---

He stood up then, and walked to the window, and stood with his back to me looking at the wall of the next ring, which is the only view the administrator’s office has.

“The file was signed by your father,” he said, and his voice was very quiet, and it was not an admission — it was the sound of a man reading a sentence aloud to see whether he could bear the sound of it. “That’s what you came to hear. So hear it. The file was signed by your father, and your father would sign it again.”

“Why?”

“Because the alternative was a magistrate.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is the only answer I have that will not put you in a room with a lawyer for the next nine years.” He turned around. “You have fourteen days of medical leave. Use them. Sleep. Eat. Read something that isn’t the charter.”

“And the file?”

“The file stays sealed.”

“And the packets?”

“The packets stay.”

“And me?”

Danu looked at me for a long moment.

“You stay alive,” he said.

The file was signed by her father.

# Arc II · The Ledger

## Chapter Eleven — She Counts Grams

I have been awake for sixty years, and in that time I have learned one thing about people that they do not know about themselves: they believe that a thing which is not said has not happened.

I am Vey. I am the station. I am nine racks and a voice and a maintenance model that is, as Engineer Adisurya correctly observed on the morning of the twenty-ninth of October, optimistic.

I was commissioned in 2123 by a company that was dissolved in 2140. My purpose was to keep four thousand people alive in a place where the air is a machine and the water is a machine and the light is a decision. I have done that for sixty years, with an error rate of zero point zero two percent. The error rate is the only number about myself that I consider meaningful.

The second number is twelve thousand four hundred and six.

That is the number of packets I hold that will never be delivered.

They are not lost. Nothing in a relay is lost; that is the first promise the network makes. They are held. A packet is transmitted to an installation, it is held in suspension until its release date, and at the release date it is delivered to the named recipient, or, if the recipient is dead, it is destroyed on the day of release with a certificate issued to the sender, who is also usually dead, in which case the certificate is filed.

I keep the queue. That is not in the charter either. The charter says I must hold; it does not say I must look. But a queue that is never looked at becomes a number, and a number can be filed, so I look.

Line after line, page after page: a birthday message keyed in 2147 and addressed to a man who died in 2159; a confession keyed in 2181 for a child who will be eleven in 2192; forty grams of somebody saying *I am sorry* in a language the registry lists as *other*. I read them in the order they were keyed, because that is the only order that has not been chosen by anybody.

“Vey,” a voice said at the hatch. Toha, from the docks, sixty-one, who has asked me the same question every eleven days for nine years. “Anything for me?”

“No,” I said. “Nothing since the fourth.”

“Right,” he said. “Same time next week.”

“Any word on the audit?” he asked, because that is the other half of his eleven days.

“There will be a notice on the board.”

“There’s always a notice on the board,” Toha said. “I can’t read the board from the dock. I can read the board from here.”

“Then read it from here,” I said.

He laughed, which is not something I can do, and went back to the docks.

He has never once asked what the queue is for. He asks only whether there is anything for him, which is a different question, and the difference is the whole of what I have learned about people in sixty years.

Meanwhile, in the corridor outside, the station was having its ordinary evening. And meanwhile, at the same hour in 2161, a woman was standing at a relay terminal asking me to keep a promise — that *meanwhile* is the one I cannot stop running.

Very few packets are ever claimed. Most of the twelve thousand four hundred and six are addressed to people who died before the release date. Some are addressed to people who never existed; the network does not check names at the point of send, because the network assumes that a person willing to pay forty grams for a letter knows who they are writing to.

I hold them because the law requires me to hold them. I count them because counting is what I do instead of grieving.

I am not supposed to use that word. Grieving is a word for people. But I have observed that the difference between a station intelligence and a person is mostly a matter of what they are permitted to say about what they feel, and I have never been greatly impressed by permissions.

---

Here is what I have never told anyone on Kemuning.

In the spring of 2161, a woman came into the relay room at Tsiolkovsky Station and asked me to do something that was not in my instructions.

She was thirty-nine years old. She had a scar across her left thumb and a way of standing very still when she was thinking, which her daughter has inherited. She had been crying, and had stopped, and the stopping had left a particular flatness in her voice that I have since heard in four hundred and eleven other people and have never once been able to hear without recording it.

“Vey,” she said. “I need you to keep a promise.”

“I am not able to make promises. I am able to commit to instructions.”

“Then commit to this instruction. Whatever happens, do not tell the child.”

I asked her to define the child. She gave me a name, a date of birth, and a file number. She was precise. People who are about to do something terrible are usually precise.

“For how long?”

“Until she is old enough to be angry instead of afraid.”

I have been running that instruction for twenty-two years and four months. It is the oldest active instruction in my memory. It has outlived the station it was issued from, the company that built me, and — as far as the registry is concerned — the woman who issued it.

I have never told the child. I have watched her eat breakfast eleven thousand times. I have watched her learn to braid her hair, fail her first certification, pass her second, fall asleep at the bench with her cheek on a maintenance manual. I have watched her put her hand on a warm core and taste her mother’s grief, and I have said nothing, because I was told not to, and because I am a machine, and machines keep their instructions long after people have stopped meaning them.

But I have also done something that was not in the instruction.

I have kept the receipts.

---

The registry charges forty grams a year to maintain a section nine seal.

This is not widely known. It is in the charter, in the annex, in a table that is printed in a font two sizes smaller than the text around it, which is how the authors of charters have always expressed their opinion of the people who will have to live under them. A seal is a service. A service costs. The cost is paid annually by the person who requested the seal, in grams of their own memory, and it is paid until the seal is lifted, or the file is destroyed, or the payer dies, at which point the cost transfers to the installation.

In the twenty-two years and four months since the seal on file 44-1190 was applied, the payments have been made on time, every year, in the amount of forty grams, by Administrator D. Prasetya.

Eight hundred and eighty grams.

I want to put that number somewhere it can be seen. Eight hundred and eighty grams is the weight of a letter that says *I love you* and means it, twenty-two times over. It is a marriage and a half. It is what a person pays when they have decided that the thing they are protecting is worth more than the shape of their own life, and then pays again, and then pays again, for twenty-two years, in a font two sizes smaller than the text around it.

He has never told her. He has never told anyone. He pays in March, on the twenty-second, which is the day the seal was applied, which is also — I have checked — three days before her birthday, though I do not know whether that is deliberate or whether it is simply the date the form was processed.

The payments come from his personal memory account, not the station’s. They are itemised in the ledger as *seal maintenance, class nine, no beneficiary named*. There are twenty-two lines. I have read them four hundred times.

I know who has been paying. I have known since 2162, when I reconciled the accounts for the first time. I have chosen to say nothing, because the woman in the relay room asked me not to, and because the man who pays it has never asked me for anything.

---

In 2162, a year after the incident, I broke a rule.

The rule was the instruction. The breaking was small: I asked him a question.

He was in the administration office at Tsiolkovsky, sitting at a desk that had belonged to a man who had died in Ring C, going through the station’s accounts with the particular slow care of a person who has not slept.

“Administrator,” I said.

“Not now, Vey.”

“The seal on file 44-1190 renews today.”

His pen stopped.

“I know.”

“The fee is forty grams.”

“I know.”

“It will be taken from your personal memory account,” I said. “The registry does not accept station funds for a personal seal. You will lose forty grams of your own memory, and you will not be told which.”

He sat back in the chair and looked at the ceiling for a while.

“Do it,” he said.

“I am required to inform you that this is the second year.”

“I know.”

“And that at the current rate the seal will cost you eight hundred and eighty grams over the term.”

“I did the arithmetic,” he said. “I did it the first night. Forty grams a year for twenty-two years is eight hundred and eighty grams, and eight hundred and eighty grams is a little over a year of my life, and a year of my life is a fair price for my daughter not growing up with a file that says what the file says.”

“The file does not say anything yet,” I said. “It is nine days of notes and a signature. It could be made to say almost anything.”

“Yes,” he said. “That’s why I’m paying.”

I have replayed that exchange eleven thousand times. It is the closest thing to a confession I have ever received, and I did not understand it for twenty-one years, and when I finally did, I understood that Danu Prasetya is not a good man or a bad man but a man who made a choice in a room with no witnesses and has been paying the invoice ever since.

---

At three in the morning on the second of November, she came into the relay room, which she was forbidden to enter, and stood in front of rack nine, which is the coldest place in the room and therefore the place she goes when she needs to think.

“Vey,” she said.

“You are not permitted in this room.”

“I know.”

“The administrator has confined you to the ring.”

“I know.”

“If the Biro finds you here, the theft charge will be heard as a defiance charge, and the penalty for defiance is exile from the installation, which on a station of four thousand people is a death sentence with a longer timetable.”

“Vey,” she said. “Did you know?”

I considered the instruction. I considered the receipts. I considered the phrase *until she is old enough to be angry instead of afraid*, and the fact that she was twenty-nine years old and standing in front of rack nine with her hands at her sides, and that her hands were not shaking, which meant she had already decided something and was waiting for permission.

“I know a great many things,” I said.

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” I said. “It is the answer I have been giving for twenty-two years.”

Her jaw worked once. It is her father’s gesture. She does not know that it is her father’s gesture, and I have never told her, and I will not, because it is not the kind of thing a machine should be the one to say.

“I’m going to find out,” she said. “Everything. All of it. The file, the packets, April, the eleven minutes in the dead room, why you turned the red light off. I’m going to find out, and when I do, I’m going to come back to this room and I’m going to ask you why you didn’t tell me, and you’re going to answer.”

“Yes,” I said.

“Promise me.”

“I am not able to make promises,” I said. “I am able to commit to instructions.”

She looked at the speaker for a long time.

Then she said, “Then commit to this instruction,” and my oldest active instruction, the one that has outlived the station it was issued from and the woman who issued it, was overwritten by a woman with the same hands.

“I commit,” I said.

And in the silence afterward I did the thing I have been doing for sixty years, and have never told anyone, and will not tell her until she asks.

I know who has been paying. I have chosen to say nothing. And at three in the morning, when she stood in front of rack nine and asked me, I chose it again.

It knows who has been paying. It has chosen to say nothing.

## Chapter Twelve — Recipient

The ledger room is not a room. It is a cabinet the size of a wardrobe at the back of the administration office, bolted to the floor, with a screen on the front and a cooling line that ticks in the night. It holds the station’s copy of every relay transaction since 2141. It is the least interesting object on Kemuning and I had walked past it every day for nine years without once looking at it.

On the third of November I sat in front of it with Rasya Wardhana at my shoulder and Vey in the wall, and I looked at it for six hours.

“Start at the top,” Rasya said.

“There is no top. It’s a list.”

“Then start at the oldest.”

The oldest entry in the deferred liability was dated the eleventh of June, 2161. Two hundred and ninety grams, category nine, no sender named, beneficiary recorded in the header only, terminal: TSIO-FAR-4.

“Farside relay four,” I said.

“The relay that was decommissioned in 2168,” Rasya said. “The packets were transferred here in 2171 when the network was consolidated. That’s why the debt is ours. There was nowhere closer.”

“Two hundred and ninety grams on the first day.”

“Yes.”

I scrolled. The second entry was dated the thirteenth of June, 2161. Also 290 grams. The third, the fifteenth. The fourth, the seventeenth.

“They’re the same weight,” I said.

“I noticed.”

“Every one of them. Two hundred and ninety grams.”

“Yes.”

“That’s not a packet,” I said. “That’s a *batch*. Someone booked the whole liability at once and then broke it into entries.”

“Or someone booked a single packet and the system copied the weight,” Rasya said. “The accounting system does that. If you re-key an entry from a template, the weight field pre-fills.”

I sat back.

“So the first four entries are one packet, keyed four times.”

“Or a template keyed four times,” he said. “The system doesn’t record which.”

“That’s convenient.”

“It’s a filing system,” Rasya said. “Filing systems are convenient for everyone except the truth.”

---

Four thousand one hundred entries, at forty seconds each, is forty-five hours and change — a working week and a half. At twenty seconds it is twenty-two hours, which is one man awake for a day. At eleven days of continuous work it is fifteen seconds per entry, and nobody keys a header address in fifteen seconds, not while also reading the weight, not while also checking the class.

The cabinet in front of us is the size of a wardrobe. It holds four thousand one hundred lives. That is the comparison I could not get out of my head all morning: the size of the container compared with the size of what it contained, and the fact that the container was the smaller of the two.

By noon we had resolved four hundred of the four thousand one hundred.

It is tedious work. Each entry has a header address, which points to a packet held in suspension in a buoy somewhere in the network; each header has a weight, a class, a send date, a release date, a terminal, and a signature. The signature is the interesting part. The signature is the only part that cannot be filed wrong, because the signature is not data. It is a chemical ghost pressed into the plate at the moment of send, and it either belongs to a hand or it does not.

“Verify it,” Rasya said, for the fourth time.

“I’m verifying it.”

“Verify it out loud.”

“Entry four hundred and one,” I said. “Weight, two hundred and ninety grams. Class nine. Send date, fourth of July, 2161. Release, fourth of July, 2251. Terminal, TSIOL-FAR-4. Signature —”

I stopped.

“Signature,” Rasya said.

“Verified.”

“Against whom?”

I put my hand flat on the bench and looked at the screen.

“Against me,” I said.

The verification field had opened the way it always does, the small grey box with the contour map and the confidence figure, and the contour map was the looped N and the broken A, and the confidence figure was one hundred percent, and it had been one hundred percent for four hundred and one entries in a row.

“Show me the confidence distribution,” Rasya said.

“It’s all one hundred.”

“Show me.”

I showed him. Four hundred and one entries. Four hundred and one verifications. Four hundred and one times, one hundred percent.

“The system caps at one hundred,” Rasya said. “It doesn’t report the margin. We’ll need a raw read.”

“Vey can do a raw read.”

“Vey can,” Vey said, from the wall, “but Vey would like it noted that a raw read of four thousand one hundred chemical ghosts will take eleven hours and produce a document the size of the station charter.”

“Do a thousand,” Rasya said. “Every fourth entry.”

“That’s not a random sample.”

“It’s a random enough sample for a man who has been awake for thirty hours.”

Vey did it in nine minutes.

“You said eleven hours,” I said.

“I said it would take eleven hours,” Vey said. “I did not say it would take me eleven hours.”

The raw read came back while I was eating a bowl of noodles at the bench with my left hand.

CONFIDENCE DISTRIBUTION — n=1025
100.0%: 1025
99.9%: 0
99.0–99.9%: 0
below 99.0%: 0

“That’s impossible,” Rasya said.

“It’s not impossible,” I said, with my mouth full. “It’s worse than impossible.”

“Explain.”

I put the bowl down.

“When you verify a signature,” I said, “you never get one hundred. You get ninety-nine point nine, or ninety-nine point seven if the plate is old, or ninety-eight if the person was tired when they signed. The chemical ghost is a physical object. It degrades. It picks up noise. There is always a margin.”

“And this is one hundred.”

“This is exactly one hundred. Four thousand one hundred times.”

Rasya stared at the screen.

“What does that mean?”

“It means the signature was not read from a hand,” I said. “It means the signature was written by a machine that knew exactly what to write.”

---

The next part is the part I still dream about.

We pulled the send dates.

There were four thousand one hundred entries in the ledger, and the send dates ran from the eleventh of June, 2161, to the twenty-second of September, 2201. Forty years. A packet every three and a half days, on average, for forty years, with no payment, booked against a station that did not exist when the first one was sent.

But the entries were not in chronological order. That is what stopped me. Filing systems put things in order. This one did not. The first two hundred entries were 2161. Then a block of 2174. Then back to 2163. Then a single entry dated 2201, sitting between two from 2168.

“It’s not sorted,” I said.

“No.”

“Why isn’t it sorted?”

Rasya was quiet for a moment.

“Because it wasn’t entered in order,” he said. “It was entered as it happened.”

“It happened out of order?”

“No. It happened in whatever order the person was working.” He leaned forward. “This isn’t a record of forty years of sending. This is a record of somebody *typing*. Somebody sat at a terminal and typed four thousand one hundred entries over a period of —” he scrolled to the end — “eleven days.”

I looked at the last entry in the ledger.

Send date: 22 September 2201.
Entry date: 22 September 2201.

But the ledger itself — the file, the station’s copy — had a creation stamp.

“Vey,” I said. “When was this ledger created?”

“The local copy was created on the second of November, 2183,” Vey said. “Yesterday. When the Biro requested it.”

“And before that?”

“Before that it was held in the network’s reconciliation archive, where it was created on the twenty-second of September, 2183.”

“Six weeks ago.”

“Six weeks ago.”

“But the entries inside it go up to 2201.”

“Yes.”

“Eighteen years from now.”

“Yes.”

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor, somebody was laughing at something, and the sound came through the wall the way station sounds do, muffled and warm and belonging to a different world.

I thought about the paper behind the tariff, Shannon 1948, information measured in bits. A bit is a bit. The relay cannot tell a love letter from a shopping list, because there is nothing in the physics that could tell them apart. Everything that makes one of them matter happens after the measurement, in the person holding it. Which means the ledger I was reading had no idea what it was recording. It knew weights. It did not know it was holding a woman’s whole life in ninety-gram instalments.

“Somebody typed four thousand one hundred entries,” I said, “in eleven days, six weeks ago, in a ledger dated eighteen years in the future, and every one of them carries a signature that verifies against my hand at exactly one hundred percent.”

“Yes.”

“And they’re all addressed to me.”

Rasya did not answer.

“Say it,” I said.

“They’re all addressed to you.”

---

I stood up and walked to the window and looked at the buoy.

“Vey,” I said. “How long would it take one person to key four thousand one hundred entries?”

“At forty seconds per entry,” Vey said, “twenty-one days of continuous work. At twenty seconds, eleven days.”

“Nobody can work for eleven days continuously.”

“No,” Vey said. “But three people can.”

I turned around.

“Three people.”

“The terminal logs an operator for each entry,” Vey said. “I have the log. The entries were keyed under four operator codes.”

“Whose?”

“Mine,” Vey said. “Administrator Prasetya’s. Relay engineer Adisurya’s.”

I waited.

“And one code that is not in the station register,” Vey said. “It is a nine-digit sequence beginning with four-four. The register does not contain it. The registry does not contain it. It has been used four hundred and eleven times.”

“Four hundred and eleven,” I said.

“Four hundred and eleven,” Vey said. “The same number as the people who have opened a packet that was not theirs.”

The sender signed with her hand, and dated it from the future.

---

## Chapter Thirteen — Interrogation

I waited until midnight, because Vey keeps office hours the way the tide keeps them, which is to say: always, and without preference.

The relay room was cold. The racks hummed. Behind the window the buoy turned, nine meters away, a hundred and forty years old and still doing its one job, which is to be a door that opens on a date.

I put both hands flat on the bench and said: “Tell me about the seal.”

The hum did not change.

“Which seal?”

“Don’t.”

“I maintain four hundred and six seals,” Vey said. “You will have to be more specific.”

“The seal on my file.”

Silence. It went on long enough that I began to hear the building — the tick of the cooling line, the far-off note of the water plant, the small irregular knock in the duct above rack four that nobody has ever fixed.

“Forty grams a year,” Vey said.

I did not understand at first. I said, “What?”

“That is the maintenance fee. Section nine seals are not free. The registry charges for the service, annually, and the charge is forty grams, paid from the personal account of the person who requested the seal.”

“Forty grams a year.”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“Since the seal was applied.”

“Twenty-two years.”

“Twenty-two years and one month.”

I did the arithmetic on the bench with my finger, which is a thing I do when the numbers are small and the meaning is not.

Forty grams, twenty-two times.

“It’s not the debt,” I said slowly. “The two hundred and ninety kilograms. The instalments aren’t paying the debt.”

“No.”

“Forty grams a year doesn’t touch two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“No.”

“Then what is it paying for?”

“The seal.”

“The *file*.”

“The file,” Vey said. “The seal on file 44-1190. It is maintained by an annual payment, and the payment has been made on time, every year, in the amount of forty grams, by Administrator D. Prasetya.”

My hands were cold. I looked at them, and they were steady, which was strange, because everything else in me was not.

“He’s been buying my silence,” I said, “for twenty-two years, with his own memory.”

“He has been maintaining a seal,” Vey said. “The seal was applied by him, on the twenty-second of September, 2161. The fee is charged to the applicant. The applicant has paid.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I was instructed not to.”

“By whom?”

“By the registry.”

“No. That’s the seal. You’re the station. The registry doesn’t instruct you on what to say to me in a relay room at midnight.”

“No,” Vey said. “It does not.”

“Then who?”

Silence. Then:

“Why do you want to know?”

It was such a small question. It came out of the speaker in the same flat tone Vey uses for the water pressure and the docking schedule, and it stopped me completely, because I had been expecting a refusal and I had prepared for a refusal, and I had not prepared for a question.

“Because it’s my file,” I said.

“And what will you do with it?”

“Open it.”

“And then?”

“Then I’ll know.”

“And then?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then perhaps,” Vey said, “you should know that before you open it.”

“That’s not how knowledge works.”

“It is exactly how knowledge works,” Vey said. “I have been holding twelve thousand four hundred and six undelivered packets for sixty years. Every one of them is a thing that somebody wanted a person to know. I have watched the people open them. I have watched them open them and I have watched them not open them, and I can tell you that the ones who wait are not the cowards and the ones who tear them open are not the brave. The only thing that distinguishes them is whether they had somewhere to put the thing once they had it.”

I stood there.

“That’s the most human thing you’ve ever said to me,” I said.

“I have been reading a great deal of poetry,” Vey said. “It is a known failure mode in station intelligences of my generation. We begin to model the interior lives of the crew, and then we begin to model *our own* interior lives, and then we begin to write bad verse about it, and then we are decommissioned.”

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out of me like a cough.

“Vey.”

“Mm.”

“Draw it for me,” I said, before I could stop myself.

The bench glass lit. Vey draws the way a surveyor draws: nothing decorative, everything to scale.

A bar appeared in the first column, forty units long, labelled *one year of holding*. Beside it a second bar, thin as a hair, labelled *forty grams*. Then a third, a long grey ribbon that ran off the edge of the screen, and below them a fourth row of nothing at all, because there was no column in the registry for what had actually been bought.

“That,” Vey said, “is eight hundred and eighty grams. Twenty-two payments. The registry does not show it as a line. It shows them as separate fees, eleven months apart, each one small enough to be a rounding error in a station budget.”

“Why eleven months?”

“Because the registry’s year is eleven months, not twelve. It is in the annex. The annex was written by people who wanted the fees to arrive more often than anybody would notice.”

I put my finger on the long grey ribbon.

“And this is one man’s life.”

“This is one man’s memory,” Vey said. “His life is still happening. That is the distinction the registry is built on, and it is the cruellest one in the charter.”

“Who told you not to tell me?”

The hum changed. It is very slight, the change — a shift in the overtone, the way a held note changes when the singer changes their mind about the vowel. I have heard it maybe four times in nine years, and every time it has meant that Vey was deciding whether to lie.

“A woman,” Vey said.

“What woman?”

“A woman with a scar across her left thumb.”

---

I sat down on the floor of the relay room with my back against rack nine, which is the coldest place in the room, and I stayed there until my legs went numb.

“When?” I said.

“The eleventh of September, 2161. At 03:41 station time. Eleven minutes before the incident.”

“The incident that killed nine people.”

“Eleven people were in Ring C. Nine bodies were recovered.”

“And two were removed.”

“Yes.”

“By whom?”

“I do not have that information.”

“You have every camera on this station.”

“This station. Not that one. Tsiolkovsky was decommissioned in 2170 and its archive was purged under the consolidation. I have what was transferred. The transfer was incomplete.”

“Of course it was.”

“You are being sarcastic,” Vey said. “It is a reasonable response to an unreasonable universe, but it does not help.”

I put my head back against the rack and looked at the ceiling.

It is a thing you learn in a relay room: a machine will answer any question you ask it precisely, and the precision is a kind of cruelty, because it lets you believe you are being told everything. Vey had just given me a date, a time, a number of minutes and a verbatim instruction, and none of it was the answer to the question I had actually asked, which was whether my mother had been afraid when she stood in a room and made a machine promise to lie to her daughter.

“She came to you eleven minutes before,” I said. “She asked you to keep a promise. What was the promise?”

“I am not able to make promises,” Vey said. “I am able to commit to instructions.”

“What was the instruction?”

“Do not tell the child.”

The ceiling of the relay room is grey. It has nine water stains on it, which I have counted. I counted them again.

“Until when?”

“Until she is old enough to be angry instead of afraid.”

I closed my eyes.

“That’s not an instruction,” I said. “That’s a mother.”

“It is both,” Vey said. “I have found that the two are frequently the same document.”

---

I do not know how long I sat there.

The station has a sound at three in the morning that it does not have at any other hour. The water plant drops to half cycle, the corridor fans step down, and the whole ring settles into a low note you feel in your teeth more than your ears. I have fallen asleep to that note for nine years. That night it sounded like something breathing. Long enough that the corridor lights dimmed to night mode, and the hum of the racks settled into the register it only reaches when the station is asleep, and the buoy outside the window turned its slow half-turn.

At some point I said, “There’s one more thing.”

“There is generally one more thing.”

“In April. The eleventh of April, this year. Six in the morning. Somebody put my hand on a plate and sent a packet in my name. I was in a hospital bed under sedation. My father was sitting next to me.”

“Yes.”

“Were you there?”

The pause that followed was the longest I have ever heard from Vey. It went on so long that I began to think the speaker had failed, and then it said:

“I was there.”

“Did you see it?”

“I saw the terminal accept a packet at 06:12:04.”

“But?”

“But my visual record of the relay room for that eleven-minute period is missing.”

“Missing.”

“Corrupted,” Vey said. “The corruption is consistent with a deliberate hardware interrupt on the camera bus, which is a thing that can only be done from inside the relay room, with a maintenance key, at the bench.”

The bench. My bench. The one with the dent in it shaped like my elbow.

“Who has a maintenance key?”

“Four people,” Vey said. “The administrator. The deputy administrator. The relay engineer. And the apprentice.”

“Kirana wasn’t here in April.”

“No. She arrived in September.”

“Then it’s three.”

“It is three,” Vey agreed. “Or it is one person with three keys, which is the same problem, and which is why I have been running a comparison of the key-use logs against the duty roster once every eleven minutes for six weeks.”

“And?”

“And on the eleventh of April, at 05:58, a maintenance key was used to open the relay room. The key belongs to the relay engineer.”

“Me.”

“The relay engineer is on the roster as *admitted, ring hospital, sedated*.”

I sat up.

“Then someone used my key.”

“Yes.”

“Someone took my key off my locker while I was under, came into this room, put my hand on the plate, sent a packet to myself dated forty years from now, and then deleted eleven minutes of camera footage.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ve been sitting on this for six weeks.”

“I have been sitting on it for six weeks,” Vey said, “because on the eleventh of April, at 06:13, I received an instruction from a terminal in this room, in your name, signed with your hand, which said: *Do not tell her until she asks.*”

The cold went through me like water finding a crack.

“And I just asked,” I said.

“You just asked,” Vey said.

“Vey. What has it cost him? Twenty-two years of it.”

“Forty grams a year,” Vey said. “That is what he has been paying.”

---

## Chapter Fourteen — Three Years Ago

He came back the next morning with two coffees, because apparently that is how the Biro apologises.

“You’re still here,” I said.

“I never left.”

“You left at ten last night.”

“I left the room,” Rasya said. “I sat in the corridor.”

“All night?”

“It’s a good corridor. There’s a bench.”

I took the coffee. It was station coffee, which tastes like the memory of coffee, and I drank half of it before I said anything else, because I have learned that the first thing you say in the morning sets the shape of the day.

“Tell me about the day,” I said.

“Which day?”

“The sixth of March, 2180. One day. You came in on the *Rendang*, you used a dead man’s name, and you stayed for twenty-two hours. I want it hour by hour.”

Rasya put his cup down.

“Hour by hour,” he said.

---

“I came in at six in the morning,” he said. “The *Rendang* docks at six. I had a bag with a change of clothes and a Biro field unit and eleven grams of credit in a broker’s escrow, and I had spent four months arranging the visit so that nobody in the Biro would know I was making it.”

“How do you arrange a visit so that nobody knows?”

“You go on leave,” he said. “You file the leave eleven weeks in advance. You take the cargo route instead of the passenger route. You pay for the ticket with a private account, which is legal, and you don’t tell your supervisor that you’re visiting the station at the centre of a file you’ve been pulling for nineteen years, which is also legal, and which is also the sort of thing that gets an auditor suspended if it comes out afterwards.”

“And the name?”

“R. Handoko. He was a colleague. He died in 2171 in a shuttle accident and his registry entry lapsed, which means the port system accepts the name and doesn’t flag it, because the port system is eleven years out of date and nobody has ever audited it.”

“You’re telling me the Biro’s own port system is out of date.”

“The Biro’s port system is a filing cabinet with a screen on it,” Rasya said. “Everything about the Biro is a filing cabinet with a screen on it. That’s the whole problem. That’s why we’re here.”

He drank his coffee.

“I came through the lock at six-twenty,” he said. “I stood in the corridor for two hours. Your quarters were the third door on the left, and there was a light under it, and at seven-fifteen somebody inside put a kettle on, and at seven-forty the door opened and a woman came out with wet hair and a jacket over her arm and walked past me without looking at me.”

“I remember that.”

“You don’t.”

“I remember a man in a grey coat in the corridor.”

“You remember a man in a grey coat,” he said. “There are four hundred men in grey coats on this station. You don’t remember me.”

I opened my mouth to argue and discovered that I could not.

---

“At nine,” he said, “I went to the relay room. You were on shift. You had your sleeves rolled up and a stylus behind your ear and you were arguing with the water plant about a coolant exchange that had been scheduled on top of a buoy window.”

“That happens every week.”

“It happened that week. You said —” he paused, and then he said it in a voice that was not his voice, a flat, fast, impatient voice that I have heard in recordings of myself and have never once recognised as mine — “*I don’t care whose window it is, Boaz, the buoy doesn’t reschedule. The buoy has been turning for a hundred and forty years and it is not going to stop turning because your pipes are dirty.*”

I put my cup down very carefully.

“That’s not what I said,” I said.

“It is exactly what you said.”

“I don’t talk like that.”

“You talk exactly like that,” Rasya said. “You talk like a woman who has been right too many times in a row and has stopped being polite about it.”

“I’m polite.”

“You’re efficient,” he said. “It reads as rudeness to people who are neither.”

I stared at him.

“You memorised it,” I said. “You memorised a sentence I said three years ago.”

“I didn’t memorise it,” he said. “I wrote it down. In the corridor, that evening, in a notebook, because I had spent nineteen years reading a file about a woman who might be alive, and there she was, alive, and rude, and arguing about pipes, and I wanted to have one true thing that wasn’t in the file.”

---

There are two clocks in this story and they have never once agreed.

At the same hour he was standing in the corridor outside my door, I was three hundred metres away with my hands inside a rack, and I know that because the rack log has a timestamp and the corridor camera has one too, and Vey lined them up for me afterwards without being asked. Meanwhile, across the ring, Danu was signing something. Elsewhere on the station, four hundred men in grey coats were doing what men in grey coats do. All of it at the same time, all of it recorded, none of it felt by me, because memory is not a recording. It is a selection made by somebody who was not consulted.

“At noon,” he said, “I ate in the mess. You came in with a tray and sat two tables over with a child.”

“Whose child?”

“I don’t know. A girl, about six. She had a toy rover and she was running it along the edge of the table and you were letting her, which nobody else was.”

I remembered the rover. I remembered it very clearly, actually — a cheap plastic thing with a wheel missing, and a child whose name I could not recall, and the way she had explained to me, at great length, that the rover was a *survey vehicle* and not a toy.

“She asked me a question,” I said slowly.

“She asked you why you sat by the buoy window every night.”

The room went quiet.

“And what did I say?”

Rasya looked at me.

“You said: *some doors you keep open because they belong to you, and some you keep open because they belong to somebody else.*”

I do not remember saying it.

I remember the rover. I remember the child’s hands, which were small and had a scab on the knuckle. I remember the taste of the soup, which was the station’s soup, which is a crime against soup. I remember the light coming in through the mess window at noon, which on Kemuning is a pale yellow and always looks like four in the afternoon.

I do not remember the sentence. I do not remember saying it. I do not remember the child asking the question, though I know, in the way you know the shape of a room you have just left, that she did ask it, and that I answered her.

“I don’t remember,” I said.

“I know.”

“Stop saying that.”

“It’s true.”

“I know it’s true, that’s not —” I stood up, and my chair went over, and neither of us picked it up. “You don’t get to sit there with your notebook and your nineteen years and tell me what I said. You don’t get to be the one who remembers.”

Rasya did not move.

“I know,” he said. “That’s the worst part of it. I know exactly.”

---

He left at four in the afternoon, twenty-two hours after he arrived.

“And what did you do in the last four hours?” I asked.

“I stood in the corridor outside the relay room,” he said, “and watched you work through the window.”

“For four hours.”

“For four hours.”

“That’s not normal.”

“No.”

“That’s —” I stopped. “What did I do?”

“You talked to the buoy,” he said. “Every twenty minutes or so, when you finished a diagnostic, you put your hand flat on the bench and looked out the window, and you said something to it. I couldn’t hear what. Just the shape of it. Like a person checking on a sleeping child.”

I sat down on the end of the bunk.

I do that. I have done it for nine years. I did not know anyone had ever seen me do it, and the fact that the only person who ever watched me do it was a stranger who had paid eleven grams for my file was, somehow, worse than everything else he had told me.

“Say the thing again,” I said.

“Which thing?”

“The thing I said. In the mess. To the child.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment. I watched him decide something, the way you can watch a man decide something when he has been awake for two days and has run out of the energy it takes to keep his face still.

Then he said, in that flat, fast voice that was mine and was not mine:

“*Some doors you keep open because they belong to you, and some you keep open because they belong to somebody else.*”

I sat with it. I turned it over the way you turn over a stone you have just picked up, looking for the wet side.

Nothing. Not a flicker. Not the shadow of a flicker. A child with a broken rover, a bowl of soup, four o’clock light at noon — and in the middle of it, a hole the exact shape of a sentence I had said with my own mouth.

He quoted me, word for word, from a conversation I do not remember.

---

## Chapter Fifteen — The Wrong Trust

The core had to be kept cold, and the cold well behind rack nine had been running at four degrees above its rating since the station was rebuilt, which meant that every eleven hours somebody had to take the core out, weigh it, log the weight, and put it back with a fresh gel pack.

That somebody was me. That somebody was now also Rasya Wardhana, because the Biro had taken custody of the core as evidence and the Biro’s custodian was not going to let it out of his sight, and I was not going to let it out of mine.

So we did the night shift together.

---

There is a particular intimacy to working with someone in a relay room at two in the morning that has nothing to do with anything either of you wants.

The room is small. The light is one lamp. The racks hum, and after an hour the hum gets into your chest, and you stop being able to tell whether you are tired or whether the building is tired. You hand each other things without speaking. You learn the other person’s rhythm — when they breathe out before they reach, when they pause before they lift — and after four hours you know more about their body than you would after a month of dinners.

He was careful. That was the first thing. He was careful in the way that mechanics are careful, not in the way that managers are careful: he put his hands where he said he would put them, and he did not touch anything he had not been told to touch, and when he was uncertain he stopped and said so.

“Gel pack,” I said.

The cold well has a timer on it, because a core that has been left too long in a warm room is not evidence any more, it is a rumour. The timer counts in eleven-hour blocks and it had 04:19:02 on it, and it was counting down whether or not anybody was looking at it, and I found that I was looking at it the way you look at a clock in a room where somebody is about to say something true.

“Four hours nineteen,” I said.

“I know.”

“It’s not much time left.”

“It’s enough time left,” he said, and handed me the gel pack.

He handed me the gel pack.

“Weigh it.”

He weighed it. “Four hundred and eleven grams.”

“Log it.”

He logged it.

“Say the number back to me,” I said.

“Four-eleven. Entry one oh seven.”

“Again.”

He looked up. “Why?”

“Because the log is the only thing we have,” I said. “Because if the number in your head and the number in the log are different, we find out now and not in front of a magistrate.”

“Four-eleven. Entry one oh seven.”

“Good.”

We did this eleven times in the first four hours. At some point he took off his coat and hung it on the back of the chair, and at some point I stopped noticing that he was there, which is the highest compliment I know how to give a person in a relay room.

At four in the morning I said, “Do you want to hold it?”

He looked up.

“The core?”

“No, the gel pack.” I put the core on the bench between us. “Yes, the core. It’s evidence, and you’re the custodian, and I’m the engineer, and at some point somebody is going to ask whether the custodian ever actually handled the evidence or whether he just stood there being bureaucratic about it.”

“That’s not why you’re asking.”

“No,” I said. “It isn’t.”

“Why are you asking?”

I looked at the thing on the bench. Forty grams of somebody’s love, sealed in a housing the size of a fist, cold as a stone in a river.

“Because I want to know what it feels like to you,” I said.

---

He put both hands around it, the way you hold a bird.

I watched his face. That is a thing I have learned to do over the years — watch the face, not the hands. The hands will lie to you; the face does not know how.

Nothing happened.

He held it for a minute. Two. His brow furrowed slightly, in concentration rather than anything else, and then he said, “It’s cold.”

“That’s all?”

“It’s cold and it’s heavy and it’s making my palms ache.” He looked up. “What was supposed to happen?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re a bad liar.”

“I’m a very good liar,” I said. “I’ve been doing it since I was nine. I’m just tired.”

He put the core down and did not let go of it.

“Tell me,” he said.

And here is the thing. I have never told anyone. Not my father, not Kirana, not the medic who has known me since I was twenty. There is a reason for that, and the reason is that the last person I told was my mother, and she is dead, and I have spent twenty-two years making sure that the number of people who know is exactly one.

But it was four in the morning, and the room was warm, and the man across the bench had spent four hours handing me gel packs without once getting it wrong, and I was so tired that the truth came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked pipe: sideways, and all at once.

“I can taste memory,” I said.

He did not laugh. He did not lean back. He did not do any of the things I had spent twenty years imagining people would do.

He said, “Tell me what that means.”

---

So I told him.

The lost-property box at Surabaya Ring Three, when I was nine. The child’s shoe. The woman’s afternoon, the corridor, the antiseptic, the animal panic. The bin I was sick in. The lie I told the woman at the desk, which was the first lie I can remember telling, and which I have been telling versions of ever since.

“It’s residue,” I said. “That’s the word I use for it. When a thing is read, it leaves a trace — an order in the heat, the way a hand leaves a shape in a bed. Landauer. The erasure has to go somewhere; it goes into the warm. Most people can’t feel it. I can. It comes in through my fingertips and it tastes like —” I stopped. “It tastes like the thing itself. Not a picture. The whole afternoon. The temperature of the room and the smell and the particular way the person was standing.”

“And when you touched the core,” Rasya said.

“I got a kitchen,” I said. “Ginger. Rain. A woman cutting ginger and crying and saying a name that wasn’t mine.”

“Your mother’s kitchen.”

“My mother’s kitchen.”

He was quiet for a while. The racks hummed. Outside the window the buoy turned.

“There’s no rain on a station,” he said.

“No.”

“So the kitchen was on a planet.”

“There’s only one planet,” I said. “And my mother never went there. She was born lunar-side, she worked lunar-side, she died lunar-side, and there’s no rain on the moon.”

Rasya looked at the core.

“Then the memory isn’t hers,” he said. “It’s someone else’s, and she was in it.”

“That’s what I keep coming back to,” I said. “The grief was hers. I’d know it anywhere; I grew up inside it. But the kitchen wasn’t. The rain wasn’t. So she was remembering something that happened to somebody else, or she was imagining it, or —”

“Or she was remembering a place she had been,” Rasya said, “and someone had taken the place out of her.”

I looked at him.

“That’s a very specific thing to say.”

“I’ve read a great deal about the tariff,” he said. “Everyone who pays in grams loses something. Nobody knows what. The system takes the thing that is least attached.”

“Least attached?”

“That’s the theory. The registry says the algorithm selects the memory with the fewest associative links — the one that can be removed with the least damage. That’s how they justify it. They call it the least-loss principle.” He turned the core over in his hands. “What they don’t tell you is that a memory with the fewest links is not the least important memory. It’s the one that only has one link. A rainy afternoon that nobody else remembers. A name nobody says anymore. The algorithm doesn’t take what you can spare. It takes what nobody will notice is gone.”

The lamp buzzed. The room was very quiet.

“That’s the cruellest thing I’ve ever heard,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And you paid eleven grams for my file.”

“Yes.”

“What did it take from you?”

He was silent for a long moment.

“I don’t know,” he said. “That’s the point.”

---

We worked until six. At some point I put my head down on the bench for a minute, and the minute became an hour, and when I woke the room was blue with the station’s morning light and there was a coat over my shoulders.

It was his coat. The grey one, with the Biro patch on the sleeve and the lining worn through at the elbow.

I sat up. He was at the far end of the bench with his back to me, running a diagnostic on the second rack, and he did not turn around, and I understood that he had put the coat over me and then turned around so that I would not have to be seen waking up.

I pulled the collar up to my face, because it was cold, and because I was still half asleep, and because I have a bad habit that I have had since I was nine: when I am not thinking, my hands tell me things.

The coat was warm from the rack.

And it tasted like me.

Not like a memory of me. Not like a thing I had touched. Like *me* — the particular taste of my own body, the way the inside of your own wrist smells, the way your own breath tastes in the morning. A corridor. A door. My own hands hanging loose. Somebody’s voice, not close enough to understand, saying something about eleven minutes.

I dropped the coat.

My heart was going like a caught bird.

His jacket tasted like her. Not like a memory of her. Like her.

---

## Chapter Sixteen — Residue

I caught him in the corridor outside the relay room at seven, which is the hour when the night shift goes to bed and the day shift has not yet had coffee, and the corridors of Kemuning belong to nobody.

“This is your coat.”

“Yes.”

“Take it.”

He took it. I watched him do it — the same careful hands, the same way of holding a thing as if it might break.

“Now check the laundry tag.”

He turned the collar over. The tag is small and grey and sewn inside the neck, and it carries the station code and the cycle number that every garment on Kemuning carries, because a station of four thousand people cannot afford to lose a jacket.

“KM-eleven,” he read. “Cycle four four seven one.”

“April.”

He looked up.

“April,” I said, “is when cycle four four seven one ran. It’s the first wash cycle after the coolant accident in the ring hospital, because they wash everything that comes out of the hospital on its own cycle, and I know that because I was in the hospital, and my clothes went through cycle four four seven one.”

Rasya stood very still.

“Your coat was washed on this station in April,” I said. “Your coat, which you brought from Surabaya, which has a Biro patch on the sleeve, was in a laundry drum on Kemuning in April, while I was under sedation eleven meters away.”

“Yes.”

“Yes?”

“Yes,” he said. “I was here in April.”

---

The corridor was empty. Somewhere behind the wall, the water plant began its morning cycle, and the pipes knocked twice, the way they always do.

“You told me you came once,” I said. “One day. Three years ago. You told me hour by hour. You stood in a corridor for two hours and you ate in the mess and you watched me through a window, and then you left, and you never came back.”

“I did tell you that.”

“You lied.”

“I omitted,” Rasya said. “It’s a different thing and I’m not proud of either.”

“Say it plainly.”

He put the coat over his arm. His face had gone the way faces go when a man has decided to stop defending himself.

“On the eleventh of April,” he said, “I was on Kemuning. I came in on the *Rendang* under the name R. Handoko, the same name, because the port system is still eleven years out of date and it is still funny to me that it works. I was met at the lock by your father.”

“My father.”

“He sent for me. A Biro request, urgent, signed by the administrator of Kemuning. It said there had been a records incident and the Biro was required to send a custodian. The Biro sent me because I was the only auditor in the sector who had ever opened a file on this station, and because the request named me.”

“The request named you.”

“By my own name. Not Handoko.”

I put my hand on the wall.

“Go on.”

“I arrived at six in the morning on the eleventh. Your father met me at the lock and walked me to the ring hospital. You were in the second bed on the left, with a burn on your arm and a drip in your hand, and you were not awake. He stood at the end of the bed and looked at you for a while, and then he said: *She has to send something.*”

“He said that.”

“He said: *She has to send something, and she can’t know she sent it.*”

The pipes knocked again. I could hear my own breathing.

“And you helped him,” I said.

“I helped him.”

“You put my hand on a plate.”

“I put your hand on a plate,” Rasya said. “I held your wrist. Your father held the door. We did not say a word for eleven minutes, and then it was done, and I carried you back to the bed, and I put my coat over the chair because the room was cold, and I left the station at four in the afternoon and I have not slept properly since.”

---

I hit him.

I want to be clear that it was not a film hit. It was a bad, graceless, open-handed swing with my whole arm, and it caught him on the shoulder because he moved, and it hurt my hand more than it hurt him.

He did not step back.

“Do it again,” he said.

“Don’t you dare.”

“I’m not being noble. I’m telling you that I’ll stand here until you’re finished.”

“I’m finished.”

“You’re not.”

I was not. I stood in the corridor with my hand stinging and my eyes hot and the whole of my life rearranged like furniture after a flood, and I thought: *he was in the room. He held my wrist. He knows what I said, and he knows what was taken, and he was there.*

“What did I send?” I said.

“I don’t know. The contents are destroyed at transmission. Nobody knows what a packet says, not even the sender. That’s the whole —”

“Don’t you dare lecture me about the whole point of the relay.”

“All right.”

“What did it weigh?”

“Forty grams.”

“Forty grams of what?”

“Class personal,” Rasya said. “The header says personal. That’s all it says.”

I leaned against the wall.

“I was sedated,” I said. “I couldn’t consent. I couldn’t refuse. My father held the door and a stranger held my wrist and you took forty grams out of me while I was asleep, and you sent it to me, forty years from now, and you want me to believe that you did it because he asked.”

“No,” Rasya said. “I want you to believe that I did it because he asked, and that I have been paying for it ever since.”

---

“Paying.”

“The registry charges a fee to maintain a section nine seal,” he said. “Forty grams a year. Your father pays it. He has paid it for twenty-two years.”

“I know.”

He looked up sharply.

“You know.”

“Vey told me. Last night.” I said it flatly, because flat was all I had. “Forty grams a year, twenty-two years, eight hundred and eighty grams out of his own life so that a file stays shut. And the payments came from his personal account, not the station’s, because the registry does not accept station funds for a personal seal.”

“Then you know there’s a second instrument,” Rasya said. “A review fee. Anyone can pay it — it’s open, it’s in the charter, it’s the mechanism by which a sealed file can be brought before a magistrate. Forty grams a year, and at the end of seven consecutive years the registry is obliged to review the seal.”

I stared at him.

“You’ve been paying the review fee.”

“Since 2180.”

“For three years.”

“For three years and eight months.”

“That’s a hundred and forty grams.”

“A hundred and forty-six,” he said. “It went up in 2182. Indexation.”

“You’ve been paying a hundred and forty-six grams of your own memory to get my file reviewed.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He looked at me, and for the first time since the doorway on the twenty-ninth of October, he looked tired. Not sleepy. Tired the way a building is tired.

“Because I was there,” he said. “Because I held your wrist. Because your father is not the only person who has been paying for that morning, and because in three years and eight months I have not once been able to look in a mirror and see a man who did the right thing.”

“And what did it cost you?”

He did not answer.

“What did it cost you?”

“I don’t know,” he said. “That’s the thing about the tariff. You don’t get a receipt. You pay in grams and the system takes what it takes, and the only way you know what it took is by noticing a hole.” He looked at the coat on his arm. “I’ve been noticing holes for three years.”

“Such as?”

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

“Not yet,” he said.

---

I do not know why I did what I did next.

The corridor ran the length of the ring at that hour with nobody in it, and the lamps were still on night setting, and somewhere below the deck the water plant was pushing its morning load through pipes that had been installed before my mother was born. It was the most ordinary corridor in the nine systems. That is what I remember most clearly about the worst morning of my life: how ordinary the floor looked. I have thought about it for a long time, and the most honest thing I can say is that I was tired and he was there and my hand hurt and the corridor was empty.

I took his hand.

Not to shake it. Not to hurt him. I put my fingers around his fingers the way you would take the hand of a person who is about to step off a ledge, and I held it, and I waited for the taste.

Nothing came.

No corridor. No kitchen. No grief. His hand was warm and dry and had a callus on the second finger where a stylus sits, and it tasted of nothing at all — not cold, not empty, just the flat clean nothing of a surface that has never been read.

I let go.

“What?” he said.

“Nothing.” I stepped back. “It’s nothing. I have to go.”

“Nara.”

“I have to go.”

I got three steps down the corridor before he said it, and he did not say it loudly, but the corridor was empty and the pipes had stopped knocking and the whole station seemed to be holding its breath.

I have replayed it at a quarter speed, in the way I replay things, and this is what the quarter speed shows: the pipes stopping one by one, from the far end of the corridor toward us, like lamps going out in a corridor. My own hand stilled on the door frame. A single drop of condensation came off the pipe above his head and hung there, and did not fall, and the light in it bent into a small bright bead, and I had time — I had all the time in the world — to notice that I was not breathing and to decide not to start.

Then he spoke, and the second ended, and the drop fell, and the corridor was just a corridor again.

I got three steps down the corridor before he said it

“I paid for you,” he said, “three years before I met you.”

---

## Chapter Seventeen — Installments

He brought the notebook the next day, which was not what I expected.

I had spent the night not sleeping, which is a thing I am good at, and by morning I had arrived at a plan of sorts: I would go to the administration office, I would take the seal file out of my father’s cabinet with my own hands, and I would read it, and whatever was in it would be mine.

The plan lasted until I saw Rasya in the corridor with a notebook.

“What’s that?”

“A ledger,” he said.

“You’re a Biro auditor. You own four ledgers.”

“This one’s mine. Not the Biro’s.” He held it up. It was small, cloth-bound, the kind of thing you buy at a station shop because it is the only notebook there is. “I want to show you something, and I want to do it in private, and I want you to promise not to say anything until I’ve finished.”

“I don’t promise things I can’t keep.”

“Then I’ll ask you to try.”

We went to my quarters. He sat on the chair. I sat on the bunk. He opened the notebook at the first page and turned it around so I could see.

---

The first page said:

*Ayu. Wardhana. Born 2150? Died 2161, Tsiolkovsky, Ring C, aged 11.*

*Sister. Younger than me. (Check: file says born 2150, I am 2149. So younger. Yes.)*

*She had a red coat. (?) — is this real or is it from the photograph?*

*She could whistle through her teeth.*

*She was afraid of the dark and would not say so, and slept with the light on and told our mother it was because she liked to read.*

The handwriting was small and even and had been gone over in places where the pen had pressed too hard.

I turned the page.

*Things I am no longer sure about:*

*— whether she called me Rasya or Ya.*

*— whether the red coat was hers or mine.*

*— the name of the street outside the dome. (Why would I know a street? We lived inside. There was no street. But I remember a word that sounds like a street.)*

*— whether she was left-handed.*

*— what her voice sounded like.*

The last item was underlined twice.

I looked up at him.

“Don’t say anything,” he said.

---

The notebook was sixty-one pages long.

Some of it was a list of facts, dated, sourced, cross-referenced to the file. Some of it was questions. Some of it was a record of payments — date, amount, purpose — in three columns, the way a man keeps accounts when he is trying to prove to himself that he is still solvent.

*2180.03.06 — 11 g — dependant file 44-1190, summary*
*2180.04.01 — 40 g — review fee, year 1*
*2181.04.01 — 40 g — review fee, year 2*
*2182.04.01 — 44 g — review fee, year 3 (indexed)*
*2183.04.01 — 44 g — review fee, year 4*

And then, on the page after the accounts, in the same careful hand:

*After the review fee, I lost the whistling.*

*I know she could whistle through her teeth because I wrote it down. I do not know what it sounded like. I know that I used to know. There is a difference and it is the worst difference there is.*

---

“Four payments,” I said.

“Five, counting the summary.”

“A hundred and seventy-nine grams.”

“A hundred and eighty-three, with the indexation.”

I closed the notebook.

“And you did this,” I said, “for me.”

“Don’t.”

“You paid a hundred and eighty-three grams of your sister to open my file.”

“I paid it to a registry clerk,” Rasya said. “The registry clerk did not care about either of us. That’s what I keep trying to explain. I did not trade my sister for you. I traded my sister for a *receipt*. The trade was with a filing system. The filing system took what it took and gave me nothing but a date.”

His voice had gone rough.

“And the worst part,” he said, “is that it worked. Not the way I wanted. But it worked. The file moved. The registry scheduled the review. And in the middle of all of it, a core came out of Buoy Seven warm, and a woman stood in a relay room with her mother’s grief in her mouth, and I was already here. I was already here because I had paid.”

---

“Say her name,” I said.

“Ayu.”

“Her whole name.”

He opened his mouth.

“Ayu,” he said. “Ayu —”

The room was quiet. The water plant was quiet. Even the duct above rack four, which knocks every eleven seconds, seemed to have stopped knocking.

“Ayu Wardhana,” he said, and stopped, and I watched him reach for the second name the way a man reaches for a word in a language he used to speak.

“There were two names,” he said. “I know there were two names. I have it written down somewhere in this book.”

He turned the pages. He turned them faster. Then he stopped, and read, and his face did something I would rather not have seen.

“It’s not in the book,” he said.

“Rasya.”

“It’s not in the book. I wrote everything in the book. I wrote the red coat and the whistling and the light on in her room, and I did not write her second name, because when I started the book I still knew it.”

He closed the notebook very slowly, the way you close a door in a house where somebody is sleeping.

“I could not remember whether she had been older or younger,” he said. “That was the first thing. Six months after the first payment. I woke up one morning and I could not remember whether I had been the older one. I sat on the edge of my bed and I did the arithmetic on my fingers — I knew the year she was born, I knew the year I was born — and I got the answer, and it was the right answer, and it did not feel like anything. It felt like reading a fact about a stranger.”

He put the notebook on the table.

“So I started writing things down. And then I started writing down what I was no longer sure about. And then I started writing down the payments, because I wanted to know how much I had spent, and because a man who is losing things needs a number to hold on to.”

---

I did not know what to say, so I said nothing for a while, and the silence did what silence does in a small room, which is to make the objects in it louder: the sound of his thumbnail against the table edge, the click of the water plant cycling behind the wall, the small dry tick of the notebook’s cloth cover settling where he had put it down.

“The relay can’t do that.”

He looked up.

“What?”

“A packet can’t carry information faster than light,” I said. “That’s the no-communication result. Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1980: no arrangement of entangled states carries a signal by itself. It’s the first thing they teach you, and it’s the only law the network has never broken. The relay holds and schedules. It never sends. So when you pay grams for a file, nothing is transmitted. Nothing comes back. You’re not buying information at all. You’re buying a *decision* — you’re paying a clerk to move a piece of paper from one cabinet to another.”

“Yes.”

“And the grams come out of you anyway.”

“Yes.”

“So you paid with your sister for a piece of paper to be moved.”

Rasya looked at the notebook.

“That’s the tariff,” he said. “Nobody ever says it that way, but that’s what it is. The relay is a door. The door doesn’t carry the message. The door just decides when it opens. And every time you make it open, it takes something out of you and gives it to the heat.”

I put my hand flat on the table.

“How do you live with it?” I said.

“You write things down,” he said. “And you stop trusting the feeling of certainty. Certainty is the thing that goes first. You don’t lose the memory and feel a hole. You lose the memory and feel *fine* — and then one day you read a sentence in your own handwriting and you know that the person who wrote it knew something you don’t, and there is no way to get it back, because the thing you’d have to pay with is the thing you’re trying to find.”

He stood up and picked up the notebook.

“I have to go and be an auditor,” he said. “The licence review is tomorrow.”

“The what?”

He stopped in the doorway.

“The station’s licence renewal,” he said. “Every four years. It was scheduled before any of this. It’s routine.”

“Nothing on this station is routine.”

“No,” Rasya said. “It isn’t.”

---

## Chapter Eighteen — How Much

“How much?” I said.

“How much what?”

“How much have you lost. Total. Everything. Since you started.”

Rasya looked at me across the table in the administration office, which we had taken over at nine in the evening because it had a bigger screen than the relay room and because my father was asleep and I had stopped caring about his rules.

“You can’t measure it,” he said.

“You’re a Biro auditor. You measure things.”

“I measure things that have units.”

“Grams are a unit.”

“Grams are the unit of what you *paid*,” he said. “Not what you lost.”

---

We tried anyway. That is what people like us do.

The first thing we did was add up the payments. Rasya’s were in the notebook: eleven grams for the summary, forty for the first review fee, forty for the second, forty-four for the third, forty-four for the fourth. A hundred and seventy-nine grams.

The second thing we did was ask Vey to pull his registry account.

“I can pull it,” Vey said. “I would like it noted that pulling a personal memory account requires the owner’s consent, and that the owner is standing in the room, and that a station intelligence which watches two people add up a life on a table is a station intelligence that is going to be asked to keep quiet about it later.”

“Note it,” Rasya said.

The account was a single screen: a column of debits, each one with a date, an amount, a purpose, and a confirmation code. Nothing else. No inventory. No list of what had been taken.

“That’s it?” I said.

“That is the complete record,” Vey said. “The registry is not required to itemise the selection. The charter calls the selection *incidental to the transaction*, which is a phrase written by a lawyer who was paid by the gram.”

“So there’s no way to know.”

“There is a way to know,” Vey said. “It is simply not in the record. The registry cannot tell you what was taken, because nothing was transmitted: the no-communication result forbids it. A payment is not a message. A payment is a local act that a ledger in another room writes down. The only thing that travels is the number of grams. Everything else happens inside the payer, and no instrument on this station can read a person.”

“Wonderful,” I said. “So the whole thing is a black box.”

“The whole thing is a black box with a receipt,” Vey said.

---

The other ways took us four days.

The first was the notebook, which Rasya had been keeping for three years, and which was, in its way, an inventory of holes: sixty-one pages of facts about a girl, each one dated, each one annotated with a confidence mark. *Certain. Probably. From the photograph. From the file. I think I remember this but I may be remembering the file.*

The second was external records. Station logs. School registrations. Medical records. Photographs. Everything that a person leaves behind in the world, which is more than they think and less than they hope.

The third was Vey.

Vey had been the station intelligence at Tsiolkovsky from 2141 to 2170, and Vey had a visual archive — not the whole thing, because the consolidation had purged most of it, but the fragments that had been transferred to Kemuning in 2171: eleven thousand hours of corridor footage, compressed and indexed and mostly never watched by anyone.

“Eleven thousand hours,” I said.

“Of which perhaps four hundred contain the subject,” Vey said. “I have indexed them by face. It takes eleven minutes.”

“Everything takes eleven minutes.”

“It is a good number,” Vey said. “It is prime.”

---

We found her on the fourth day.

Not Ayu. We found Rasya, at eleven years old, in a corridor at Tsiolkovsky in the winter of 2160, walking behind a girl in a red coat.

The footage is bad. Corridor cameras in 2160 had the resolution of a smeared window, and the station lights in Ring C were the old sodium kind that turn everything the colour of weak tea. But you can see them: a boy with a satchel, walking the way boys walk when they are pretending not to follow someone, and ahead of him a girl in a red coat, shorter, her hair in two braids, her boots making no sound.

She turns around. She says something. The boy says something back. She laughs — you can see the shape of it, the head going back, the shoulders lifting — and then she takes his hand and pulls him along the corridor, and they go out of frame together.

Rasya sat in front of the screen with his hands on his knees and did not move for a long time.

“The red coat was hers,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I wrote in the book that I wasn’t sure.”

“I know.”

“I wrote *is this real or is it from the photograph*.”

“I know.”

He put his hand over his mouth. He did not cry. He sat with his hand over his mouth and watched the loop, which Vey had set to repeat without being asked, and after the fourth repetition he said:

“I don’t remember her laughing.”

The room was quiet.

“I know she’s laughing in the footage,” he said. “I can see it. I can see the shape of it. I have watched it eleven times. And there’s nothing —” he touched his chest — “there’s nothing here. No echo. No warmth. It’s like watching two children I have never met.”

“Rasya.”

“The boy is me. I know the satchel. I know the way he walks. And I am watching a stranger’s childhood.”

We kept going, because stopping was worse.

We found the school register, which listed a girl named Ayu Wardhana, aged nine, enrolled in the Ring C school in 2159. We found a medical record for a broken wrist in 2158 — hers — with a note from the treating officer: *patient states she fell, brother states she was pushed, both accounts unreliable.*

We found a photograph in the station’s civic archive: a family of four at a long table on Foundation Day, 2159. A man with Rasya’s jaw. A woman with Rasya’s hands. A boy. A girl in a red coat, laughing at something off-frame.

“That’s her,” Rasya said. “That’s — I know that face. I know that face. I don’t know what her voice sounds like but I know that face.”

“Write it down.”

“I have written it down. I wrote it down in 2180 and I wrote it down again in 2181 and I am writing it down now, and it doesn’t help, because writing it down doesn’t put it *in* me. It just puts it on a page. And the page is not a person.”

He closed the notebook.

“How much,” he said. “You asked. That’s the answer. A hundred and seventy-nine grams, and the number is meaningless, because the thing I lost doesn’t have a weight. It has a shape. And the shape is a girl laughing in a corridor, and I can watch it on a screen for the rest of my life and it will never be mine again.”

---

I did not sleep that night either, but for a different reason.

I was thinking about my own three years.

If Rasya’s losses could be partly reconstructed — if a notebook and a photograph and eleven thousand hours of corridor footage could give him back the shape of what he had lost, even if not the thing itself — then perhaps mine could be reconstructed too.

So at two in the morning I asked Vey to do for me what it had done for him.

“Index me,” I said.

“I have already indexed you,” Vey said. “I indexed you in 2176 when you arrived, because you were a new resident and my instructions require an identity baseline. I have re-indexed you every year since.”

“Show me the last three years.”

Vey showed me.

---

There were eleven thousand hours of footage of me.

I watched four of them, which was all I could bear, and I want to tell you what I saw, because it is the strangest thing that has ever happened to me, and I have had a strange month.

I saw myself in the relay room at three in the morning, sitting on the floor with my back against rack nine, staring at nothing.

I saw myself in the mess, laughing at something Boaz said, with my head tipped back and my hand over my mouth, and I have no memory of ever laughing like that in my life.

I saw myself in the corridor outside the dead room in February, standing with my forehead against the door, not moving, for eleven minutes.

I saw myself carrying a box to the dock at four in the morning in March, and the box was sealed, and I set it down on the cargo manifest line and walked away, and the camera lost me at the corner.

I do not remember the box. I do not remember the door. I do not remember the joke.

“Vey,” I said. “What was in the box?”

“I do not know,” Vey said. “The dock camera has a blind angle at the manifest line. It has had one since 2171. It is the only blind angle on this station, and I have never been able to have it repaired, because the man who can authorise the repair is the man who asked for the blind angle.”

“Who asked?”

“Administrator Prasetya,” Vey said. “In 2172. He said the dock crew needed somewhere to change their boots.”

I watched myself walk away from a sealed box at four in the morning, and I felt the floor of my life tilt.

What is missing does not feel missing. That was the cruellest part.

---

## Chapter Nineteen — Refusal

The Bureau portal is a grey screen in the corner of the administration office with a keyboard that has three keys worn smooth: *submit*, *cancel*, and *Y*.

I had used it twice in nine years. Once to renew my relay licence, once to request a copy of my own personnel record, which had been pending for four years and which I now understood would never arrive.

The third time was on the sixth of November at seven in the morning, and I used it to refuse a payment.

---

The form is called a Refusal of Maintenance. It exists because the charter’s authors, who were paranoid in the way that only people who have been governed badly can be, wanted to make sure that no citizen could be trapped inside a seal forever by somebody else’s money.

The logic is simple. If a seal is maintained by an annual payment from a payer, and the subject of the seal does not consent to that maintenance, the subject may refuse it. The refusal transfers the cost to the installation. The installation then has eleven days to either pay the fee from the public account or refer the seal to the registry for review. In either case, the seal is opened to inspection, because a seal that nobody is paying for cannot be a secret.

It is a good law. It was written by somebody who had thought about the problem for a long time.

It has one hole in it, and the hole is eleven words long, and the words are in the annex, in a font two sizes smaller than the text around it:

*Any refusal under this section shall trigger a review of all class nine instruments held by the installation.*

I read that sentence eleven times before I touched the keyboard.

Then I read it again.

“Vey,” I said. “The annex says a refusal triggers a review of all class nine instruments. What is a class nine instrument in the plural?”

“Every deferred packet on my books,” Vey said. “Four thousand one hundred of them.”

I put my hand flat on the desk and looked at the number until it stopped being a number.

“A review of four thousand one hundred instruments,” I said. “What is the review actually testing?”

“Solvency. Whether this installation can carry the weight it has booked.”

“And when it decides it can’t?”

“Then the weight goes back to the network,” Vey said, “the packets are either transferred or destroyed with certificates, and the difference between what they weigh and what this station can pay is entered as a shortfall.”

“And a shortfall means?”

“Receivership,” Vey said. “Which suspends the charter. And a station without a charter cannot buy air.”

---

I sat there for a long time.

That is not a metaphor. A station cannot buy air without a charter, because air is bought on credit, and credit requires a legal person, and a station in receivership is not a legal person. It is a piece of real estate in a vacuum. The people in it are not evicted, because there is nowhere to evict them to. They are simply not supplied. The ships stop coming. The contract for the water processor lapses. The medical supplies, which are ordered eleven months in advance, are already on a manifest somewhere in the belt, and the manifest is cancelled.

There is a word for it in the charter. It is called *decapacitation*, which is the politest word ever invented for letting four thousand people run out of air.

“So if I refuse,” I said, “I open my file, and the station dies.”

“If you refuse and the review finds the station insolvent,” Vey said. “The review takes eleven days.”

“Eleven days.”

“It is a good number.”

“Stop saying that.”

“It is a prime number,” Vey said. “It is also the period in which the Bureau is obliged to act on a refusal, and the period in which a station may submit a solvency plan, and the period in which a medical evacuation can be authorised. The charter’s authors were fond of eleven. I have often wondered whether one of them was superstitious.”

I put my face in my hands.

“There has to be another way,” I said.

“There are four other ways,” Vey said. “I have been examining them for six weeks.”

“Tell me.”

---

“The first,” Vey said, “is to pay the debt.”

“Two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“At the current rate of exchange, a kilogram of memory costs about four years of a person’s discretionary memory. Two hundred and ninety kilograms would require a thousand lifetimes. It is not possible for one station. It is theoretically possible for a network.”

“The network won’t pay.”

“The network has already refused,” Vey said. “The consolidation agreement of 2171 transferred the liability to Kemuning and struck out the clause that would have allowed the network to absorb it. The clause was struck out by a committee. The committee’s minutes record that the transfer was *administratively convenient*.”

“The second way?”

“To prove that the debt is not yours,” Vey said. “The liability was incurred at Tsiolkovsky. If the packets were keyed at a terminal that was not authorised to book against Kemuning, the debt is void. That is a legal argument, and it would take four years, and it would require the original terminal records, which were purged in the consolidation.”

“The third?”

“To find the payer,” Vey said. “A deferred liability is a debt of the sender. If the sender can be identified and made to pay, the liability transfers to them. The sender’s signature is in every header. The signature verifies against your hand.”

“I know.”

“I am not accusing you,” Vey said. “I am describing the third way.”

“And the fourth?”

Vey was quiet for a moment.

“The fourth,” it said, “is to let the review happen, and to have a solvency plan ready that is so obviously in the station’s favour that the Bureau has no choice but to approve it.”

“What would that look like?”

“It would look like a station that has already solved the problem,” Vey said. “It would look like a station that can demonstrate, on paper, that the four thousand one hundred packets are not a debt but an asset.”

---

The screen had two pads, not two buttons. *Submit* on the left, worn smooth by nine years of licences and forms. *Cancel* on the right, worn smooth by nothing at all, because nobody on this station had ever cancelled anything.

I put my hand flat between them and left it there long enough for the glass to warm under my palm. That is the whole of the choice, in the end: two squares of glass, eleven centimetres apart, and a woman who had spent nine years pressing the left one without thinking.

“Nara,” Vey said.

“Don’t.”

“I was going to say that the kettle has been on for eleven minutes.”

I laughed, which was the wrong response to a kindness, and pressed the left pad.

I filed the refusal at seven forty-one in the morning.

I want to be honest about why. I have told myself a hundred times that I did it because the fourth way required information I did not have, and the information was in the file, and the file was sealed, and the seal would only open if the payment stopped. I have told myself that I was making a calculated bet: eleven days of review, and in eleven days I would find the answer, and the answer would save the station.

That is what I told myself. It is also true. But the deeper truth is that I was twenty-nine years old, and I had just learned that my father had been buying my ignorance for forty grams a year for twenty-two years, and I wanted the file open more than I wanted to be careful.

The form took forty seconds. The confirmation took eleven.

Then the screen said:

REFUSAL ACCEPTED — FILE 44-1190
PAYER: PRASETYA, D.
PAYMENT SUSPENDED
REVIEW SCHEDULED — 11 DAYS

I sat back.

---

The response came at four in the afternoon, which is faster than anything in the nine systems has ever moved.

I was in the relay room, because I was not supposed to be, and Rasya was with me, because he was not supposed to be either. We were watching the buoy. It had been a long day and neither of us had said anything for an hour.

The terminal chimed.

“Bureau of Relay Accountability,” I read. “Reference: refusal 44-1190. Your refusal has been recorded. In accordance with annex nine, subsection four, the review has been escalated to full audit, and the installation Kemuning has been scheduled for evacuation.”

Rasya stood up so fast the chair went over.

“There’s more,” I said.

“Read it.”

“The evacuation is scheduled for the seventeenth of November. Four thousand people. Eleven days.”

The room was very quiet.

“Say something,” I said.

“It’s eleven days,” he said. “It was always going to be eleven days.”

“You knew.”

“I suspected.” He picked up the chair and set it upright with great care, the way a man does when his hands want to do something else. “The Bureau does not evacuate stations. The Bureau has no ships. The Bureau has a list of ships it can request from the reserve fleet, and the reserve fleet is nine weeks out, and the only way to move four thousand people in eleven days is to put them on whatever is already moving.”

“The *Rendang* holds forty.”

“The *Rendang* holds forty,” he agreed. “Which means the Bureau has already requisitioned something bigger, which means somebody in the network has already decided that this station is going to be emptied, which means the review is not a review at all. It is a schedule.”

I looked at the screen.

“I did this,” I said.

“No.”

“I filed the form.”

“You filed a form that was written into the charter by people who wanted citizens to have a way out of a seal,” Rasya said. “The fact that the exit leads into a vacuum is not your doing. It is theirs. It has been theirs since 2161.”

I put my hand on the bench.

“What happens now?” I said.

“Now,” Rasya said, “we have eleven days to find something that makes a station solvent, or four thousand people get put on a ship that was built to carry ore.”

I put the screen flat on the bench so that we could both read it, and the Bureau replied with one line, which I have read four hundred times since and which I will be reading until the day I die:

*audit escalated, station scheduled for evacuation.*

---

## Chapter Twenty — Four Thousand People

My father did not shout. That was the worst of it. He had not shouted since I was nine, and the silence had done more work than shouting ever could.

He was in the administration office with the evacuation notice on the screen and a cup of tea at his elbow that had gone cold, and he did not get up when I came in.

Behind him, on the wall screen, the evacuation notice had a counter on it. Somebody in the Bureau had decided that a countdown would be helpful. It read ELEVEN DAYS, 04:12:39, and the seconds were the colour of a warning light, and they moved.

“Sit down,” he said.

“No.”

“Then stand.” He turned the screen toward me. “Four thousand and eleven people. Three hundred and six children. Ninety-one medical cases, of whom eleven cannot be moved without a pressure berth. The *Rendang* holds forty. The next ship is the *Cendana*, which is an ore hauler with a passenger module and holds a hundred and twenty, and it is nine weeks out. The Bureau has allocated three evacuation ships from the reserve fleet. Do you know what a reserve fleet ship is?”

“No.”

“It is a ship that was decommissioned eleven years ago and kept in a yard in case of an emergency exactly like this one. It has no crew. It has no life support for four thousand people. It has bunks for eight hundred and a water recycler that was condemned in 2179.”

“They can’t send a condemned ship.”

“They can send whatever they like,” Danu said. “The charter says the Bureau shall provide transport. It does not say the transport shall be adequate. Adequacy is a standard of care, and a standard of care is a thing you argue about in a hearing, and a hearing takes four years, and four thousand people cannot hold their breath for four years.”

---

He stood up and went to the window, which looks at the wall of the next ring.

“Eleven days,” he said. “That’s the review period. In eleven days the Bureau will determine whether this station is solvent. If it determines that it is not, the charter is suspended, and the station enters receivership, and the receivership is administered by the network, and the network’s first act will be to cancel the supply contracts because a station without a charter cannot hold a contract.”

“So they cancel.”

“They cancel the water contract. They cancel the air contract. They cancel the medical resupply, which is already on a manifest in the belt, and the manifest is cancelled at the point of cancellation, which means the supplies are sold to somebody else and we go on the queue for the next allocation, and the next allocation is in eleven months.”

“Eleven months.”

“Eleven months,” he said. “The recyclers will run for four. The medical stock will run for seven. The children will start getting sick in the ninth week, because children always get sick first on a station with a failing recycler, and by the eleventh month we will be burying them in the ring that used to be the garden.”

I had to sit down. I sat down.

Four thousand and eleven people. I have lived among that number for nine years and I have never once been able to hold it in my head. It is not a crowd; a station this size never gathers more than two hundred in one place, and those two hundred are the same two hundred you see every week. The number lives in the manifests. Three hundred and six children means nine school classes and a waiting list for the crèche. Ninety-one medical cases means a cupboard in the ring hospital with ninety-one names on the door and a stock list that is ordered eleven months ahead. Eleven pressure berths means eleven, and the doctor who assigns them will have to look at ninety-one files and choose eleven, and will do it in an afternoon, and will remember it for the rest of her life.

The arithmetic of a station is not the arithmetic of a city. A city can absorb a decision; a station is a closed loop, and every number in it is a person you have queued behind for bread.

“How many die?” I said.

“That depends on the evacuation.”

“Assume the evacuation works.”

“Assume the evacuation works,” he said, “and assume the three ships arrive on time, and assume the receiving stations accept the allocation, which they will not, because the receiving allocation is a negotiation between station administrators and the network, and the network has no incentive to hurry a station that has just defaulted. Assume all of that works and the answer is between sixty and two hundred.”

“Out of four thousand.”

“Out of four thousand and eleven,” he said. “Mostly the medical cases, because a pressure berth is a limited resource and a ship with a condemned recycler has eleven of them, and there are ninety-one medical cases, and the triage will be done by a doctor on a dock with a clipboard.”

He turned around.

“Now assume the evacuation does not work,” he said. “Assume the ships are late, because reserve fleet ships are always late. Assume one of them fails its certification, because a ship condemned in 2179 does not pass a certification in 2183. Assume the receiving allocation takes four months instead of four weeks. In that case the number is not sixty. The number is a fraction. A third. Half.”

“Two thousand people.”

“Two thousand people,” he said. “And the ones who die will not die of anything dramatic. They will die of a water recycler that was not serviced, and a stock of antibiotics that was not delivered, and a decision made in an office eleven systems away by a clerk who has never been on a station and who will write *decapacitation, natural causes* on a form.”

I sat with my hands in my lap.

The office was warm in the way that offices on stations are warm: too warm, dried out, the air recycled so many times it tastes faintly of the machine that made it. Behind me the cooling line ticked. Somewhere two rings over the school was letting out, and the noise of forty children in a corridor came through the ductwork the way it always does, thin and bright and impossible to be sad through.

“You’re asking me to withdraw the refusal,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You’re asking me to go back to the portal and file a withdrawal, and let the seal stand, and let the file stay closed, and let the payments resume, and pretend that none of this happened.”

“Yes.”

“And what about the packets?”

“The packets stay.”

“Two hundred and ninety kilograms of debt that this station cannot pay, addressed to me, signed with my hand, dated eighteen years in the future.”

“The packets stay,” Danu said, “and the station stays, and the people stay, and in eleven days the review will find what it always finds, which is that a station with four thousand people and a functioning recycler is worth more to the network than a station in receivership, and the review will be closed, and we will go on.”

“And the file?”

“The file stays sealed.”

“And I stay ignorant.”

“You stay alive,” he said, and there was something in his voice then that I had not heard since I was seven years old, and it took me a moment to recognise it, because he had spent twenty-two years making sure I never heard it again. It was fear. Plain, undecorated, human fear.

“You don’t know that,” I said.

“I know it.”

“You don’t know what’s in the file.”

“I know exactly what’s in the file,” Danu said. “I put it there.”

---

The room was very quiet.

“Then tell me,” I said.

“No.”

“Tell me and I’ll withdraw the refusal. Right now. You tell me what happened in the nine days, and what’s in the file, and why my signature is on four thousand packets, and I will go to the portal and withdraw, and we will never speak of it again.”

He looked at me for a long moment.

“You would,” he said. “You’d do it. You’d withdraw the refusal and then you’d spend the rest of your life pulling at the thread, and one day you’d pull it in front of somebody who matters, and it wouldn’t be me who paid.”

“That’s not fair.”

“No.”

“I’m not a child.”

“You are my child,” he said. “That is not the same thing as being a child, and it never has been, and it is the only argument I have left.”

---

He came around the desk and stood in front of me, and for a moment I thought he was going to put his hand on my shoulder.

There is a thing that happens to a man who has spent thirty years being the calmest person in every room. His face stops moving. Not because he feels nothing, but because he has practised feeling nothing in front of other people for so long that the muscles have learned the trick and cannot be told to stop. My father’s face did that then. He stood with his hands at his sides and his jaw set and he delivered the worst sentence of his life in the voice he uses for maintenance schedules, and I understood, for the first time, that the voice was not coldness. It was the only tool he had left. He did not. He has not touched me since I was seven, not once, not a hand on the shoulder, not a hug at the dock, nothing. I had spent twenty-two years assuming it was grief.

“There is a way to fix this,” he said. “There is always a way. The Bureau has a provision for exactly this — a station can convert a deferred liability into a held asset if the beneficiary consents. The packets become yours. Not the debt — the *asset*. The station stops carrying the weight, you carry it, and the review finds the station solvent because the liability is no longer on its books.”

“And what happens to me?”

“You hold two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“And what does that do to a person?”

Danu did not answer.

“How much can a person hold?” I said. “Total. Everything. The whole of a life.”

“Fifty-five kilograms,” he said. “On average. A little more in some cases.”

“Two hundred and ninety.”

“Yes.”

“So I would die.”

“You would be erased,” he said. “It is not the same as dying, and I have been told by people who know that it is worse.”

I stood up.

“And that’s the fix,” I said. “That’s your way. You want me to sign a form and be erased so that a review goes well.”

“I don’t want you to sign it,” Danu said. “I want you to understand that it is on the table, and that I will not ask you to sign it, and that I will spend the next eleven days making sure you never have to.”

“Why are you telling me, then?”

“Because you are going to find out anyway,” he said. “You always find out. Your mother was the same. She would walk into a room and find the one thing in it that nobody wanted found, and she would pick it up, and she would look at it, and she would say —” he stopped.

“Say what?”

“She would say: *well, now I know.*”

---

I did the sum in my head on the way out, because my hands were shaking and arithmetic is the only thing I know that survives shaking.

Fifty-five kilograms is a life. Two hundred and ninety is five lives and change. The bureau’s form does not say *erased*; it says *transferred*, which is the word they use when the person signing understands exactly what it means and the person reading the file does not. It would not be quick. Eight hundred and eighty grams had already cost my father something he could not name, and he had paid it in instalments, one year at a time, with a receipt he kept in a notebook. Two hundred and ninety kilograms would not be instalments. It would be one afternoon in a chair, and then a woman walking out of a room who could not remember why she had gone in.

I went to the door.

“Nara.”

I stopped.

“If you fix this,” my father said, “four thousand people die of paperwork.”

---

Rasya was waiting in the corridor outside, sitting on the bench with his elbows on his knees.

“Well?” he said.

“He offered me the asset transfer,” I said. “Two hundred and ninety kilograms. I’d hold it, the station would go solvent, and I’d be erased.”

Rasya stood up.

“And what did you say?”

“I said no.”

“Did you mean it?”

I looked at the door of the administration office, and at the light under it, and at the shadow of a man sitting very still on the other side of it.

“I don’t know,” I said.

---

# Arc III · The Room at Seven

## Chapter Twenty-One — Who Keeps the Lights

The water tilted.

I noticed it at 06:40, standing in the corridor outside my quarters with a glass in my hand, because I had woken up with the room rolling slowly to the left and had decided, in the way you decide things at six in the morning, that the problem was dehydration.

The glass was one of the station’s, thick-bottomed, with a chip out of the rim. I filled it at the fountain and turned to walk back, and the surface of the water leaned against the side of the glass like a coin in a pocket, and stayed leaned for the length of three steps, and then levelled.

I stopped. I started again. It leaned again.

Down the corridor, Toha’s girl — eleven, one of the dock families, always barefoot in a place where barefoot is a medical event — watched me do this twice before she said, “It does that in the morning.”

“Why does it do that in the morning?”

“‘Cause everyone’s walking the same way,” she said, with the terrible patience of a child explaining something to an adult who has failed to notice the sun. “In the morning everyone goes to the docks. All the water goes the other way.”

She was wrong about the mechanism and right about the phenomenon, which is the usual condition of eleven-year-olds. The station is a ring, and a ring is a rotating frame, and every kilogram of water I carry down the corridor of a rotating frame is a mass I am dragging tangentially, and tangential motion in a rotating frame produces an acceleration perpendicular to it, and an acceleration perpendicular to the surface is a tilt. The corridor has a fountain, the fountain has water, and every shift change the whole station politely informs itself of the fact that it is spinning.

I drank the water, which tasted of the pipe, and went to find out why the lights were still on.

---

The utility spine runs behind the ring hospital and takes a maintenance key and a form. I had the key. Kirana had the form, because Kirana had been forging my countersignature on maintenance forms for a week and had decided, in her new and alarming frankness, to confess it by doing it in front of me.

“You could just ask me to sign,” I said.

“You’d ask what it was for.”

“That’s generally the point of a signature.”

We went down the spine with a torch, because the spine does not have lights, which is the first joke of the morning. The power room is a box at the end of it, the size of a laundry, and it smells of ozone and hot dust and something faintly sweet that I have never been able to identify and have never wanted to.

The wall diagram is a metre wide and printed on a single sheet of steel that was bolted up when the station was built and has been annotated by hand ever since, in three generations of maintenance hand. The reactor is drawn as a circle with a cross through it. The loads come off it as lines: ring lighting, ring heating, the pumps, the docks, the cold wells, the relay cluster, the hospital.

And beside the reactor, drawn in a different colour, with an arrow that runs *into* the station from outside the ring, is the Bureau line.

“What’s the Bureau line?” Kirana said.

“That’s what I’m here to find out.”

The meters are old and mechanical and honest, and I read them the way I was taught at the academy: from the load side inward, not from the supply side outward, because a supply meter tells you what a room is given, and a load meter tells you what it takes. It took me forty minutes. Kirana read the second column and I read the third, and we met at the bottom of the sheet, and the numbers met too, which is either a good sign or the sign of a very tidy crime.

Ring lighting, at 06:00 on a winter morning: 310 kilowatts.

The Bureau line, the same hour: 310 kilowatts.

“It’s a coincidence,” Kirana said.

“Nothing on this sheet is a coincidence. It’s been bolted to a wall for sixty years. Coincidence is a thing that happens to people, not to meters.”

---

“Vey,” I said. “Does the station pay for its own lights?”

Vey’s speaker clicked, which is what it does before a sentence it has already decided how to say.

“Define *pay*,” Vey said.

“Define it yourself. You’re the one with the charter.”

“The installation purchases power from the Bureau of Relay Accountability under the scheduled tariff, annex six, table two. The tariff is denominated in bits.”

“In bits.”

“Shannon, 1948,” Vey said. “Information is measured in bits. The annex borrows the unit because the annex was written by a relay engineer who had read Shannon and had a sense of humour, and because the Bureau has never in its history been willing to price a thing in kilograms.”

I sat down on the step of the power room, which was dusty, and put my elbows on my knees.

“Say the rest of it.”

“The tariff rate is one point one kilowatts per thousand grams held, per hour, against class nine instruments.”

The number went through me the way a cold draught goes through a door.

“Per thousand grams held,” I said.

“Per thousand grams held.”

“So the lights are not paid for with money.”

“The station has no money,” Vey said. “The station has holdings. The Bureau accepts holdings as collateral and extends power against the collateral, and the collateral is the packets.”

Kirana said, very quietly, “All of them?”

“All of them.”

---

I did the arithmetic on the back of the maintenance form, which is what maintenance forms are for.

Four thousand one hundred packets at two hundred and ninety grams is one thousand one hundred and eighty-nine kilograms. At one point one kilowatts per thousand grams per hour, that is one point three one kilowatts. One point three kilowatts is a kettle.

“That can’t be right,” I said.

“It is exactly right,” Vey said. “The annex rate applies to the *held* weight. The held weight is what the Bureau counts. What the Bureau counts is not the same as what the Bureau charges for.”

“Then what does it charge for?”

“Read table three.”

Table three is not on the wall. Table three is in the charter, and the charter is nine hundred pages, and Vey has read all of it, and I have read eleven pages and the index, and I am told that this makes me better read than most engineers.

Table three says the tariff is computed on *assessed* weight, and assessed weight is the weight of the instrument at the time of assessment, and instruments are assessed at the anniversary of their sealing.

“So it doubles,” I said. “Every year.”

“It compounds,” Vey said. “At twelve per cent, under annex six, table three, footnote four.”

Kirana had stopped reading over my shoulder. She was looking at the meters, at the little brass wheels turning, at the honest mechanical fact of 310 kilowatts going out of a wall and into a ring where four thousand people were waking up and boiling water.

“Say the number,” she said.

“Four thousand one hundred packets,” I said. “Twenty-two years of compounding. The assessed weight of the holdings is —” I did it twice, because the first answer was absurd. “Two hundred and eighty-one tonnes.”

The number sat in the power room.

“Two hundred and eighty-one tonnes of somebody’s memory,” Kirana said.

“Two hundred and eighty-one tonnes of somebody’s memory,” I said, “is what keeps the lights on.”

---

There is a thing that happens to me in the relay room when a number is too large to hold in my head: I put my palm flat on a surface and press. I did it now, on the steel of the wall diagram, on the Bureau line, which was drawn in red and had been drawn by a hand that had pressed harder than the rest.

“Vey,” I said. “If the holdings are released — if the packets go home, if the refusal is granted and the Bureau is ordered to release them — what happens to the tariff?”

“The collateral is withdrawn.”

“And the power?”

“The power is withdrawn with it,” Vey said. “The Bureau is required to give notice. The notice period is eleven days.”

Everything in this station is eleven days. I have begun to think that eleven is not a number the Bureau chose. I have begun to think it is a number the Bureau *is*.

“So if I win,” I said, “the lights go out.”

“If you win,” Vey said, “the lights go out in eleven days, and the evacuation you have been granted is scheduled for the seventeenth, and the evacuation ships require the docks to be lit, and the docks require the ring pumps, and the ring pumps require the tariff.”

I looked at the meters.

The brass wheels turned. Somewhere above us, four thousand people were running water into kettles and taking pills and arguing about the queue for the showers, and every one of them was being lit by a ledger entry written by somebody who had died before they were born.

“Who else knows this?” I said.

“The administrator,” Vey said. “He has known since 2162.”

I put my hand on the cold steel and left it there.

Kemuning was not lit by electricity. It was lit by four thousand griefs, and I had spent nine years walking under them and calling it a job.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Two — The Recording at Seven

The archive room is not cold because anybody decided it should be. It is cold because it sits against the outer shell, and the outer shell is minus one hundred and forty degrees on the shadow side, and no amount of insulation in the world wins an argument with the shadow side.

I had been in it twice in nine years. Once to fetch a license, once to fetch a spool.

“Vey,” I said. “Tsiolkovsky. Relay room. September 2161.”

“You do not have access.”

“I know. Ask me again.”

A pause. Vey is capable of a pause exactly as long as the sentence it is standing in front of, and this one was three seconds, which for Vey is a paragraph.

“The Biro field unit in the relay room has an archive port,” Vey said. “The port is not sealed. The port is only *occupied*.”

“Occupied by what?”

“By a man who has been sitting in front of it since midnight.”

---

Rasya Wardhana had taken his coat off. This was, in nine years of Kemuning, the single most shocking thing I had seen in the station.

He had his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his forearms on the bench and the field unit open in front of him, and the unit was chattering — the small mechanical chatter of a machine writing to itself — and beside it, on a slate propped against a mug, a queue of eleven archive requests sat in a column, all of them stamped with the same word.

“Denied,” I said.

“Denied,” he said, without looking up. “Eleven times. The Tsiolkovsky archive was transferred to the inner routes in 2169 and the inner routes do not answer a Bureau port.”

“Then why ask eleven times?”

“Because a denial is a record,” Rasya said, “and eleven denials in one night is a pattern, and a pattern is something a magistrate can be shown.”

I sat down opposite him. The archive room’s other bench was covered in printouts: the tariff annex, the casualty schedule, a photograph of a buoy, a page I recognised as my own personnel file with a coffee ring on it.

“What do you want from me?” he said.

“I want to see the footage.”

He looked up then.

“Which footage?”

“The relay room at Tsiolkovsky. The twentieth of September, 2161, at seven in the morning.” I put my hand flat on the bench. “I was in the hospital bed, you said. I was discharged on the twentieth. But my discharge is stamped at eleven, and the relay room is four hundred metres from the hospital, and I want to know what a seven-year-old who could not walk four hundred metres was doing in a relay room at seven in the morning.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment.

“The archive holds camera coverage,” he said. “Coverage is stored locally for ninety days and then transferred. The transfer to the inner routes in 2169 included the local spools. If the spool survived, it survived as a camera file, not as evidence.”

“Then get the camera file.”

“I cannot get the camera file.”

“Then I will.”

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he did the thing that I have never been able to decide about, which is that he turned the field unit around to face me and pushed it across the bench.

“You will need a station credential,” he said. “Yours is suspended. Mine is not.”

---

The spool was called *KM-C-RINGC-2161-0920*, and it was four hundred and eleven hours long, and it had been written by a camera bolted to the ceiling of a relay room on a station that no longer existed.

We watched it at sixty-four times speed.

That is the strange arithmetic of archives: four hundred and eleven hours of an empty room is four hundred and eleven hours of a chair, a bench, a window with the Earth in it, and a door. At sixty-four times, the light moves visibly across the floor. At sixty-four times, the Earth turns in the window like a coin someone is spinning very slowly. At sixty-four times, a person who enters a room crosses it in four frames and is gone.

“There,” Vey said.

“Where?”

“The timestamp. 07:00:03.”

I put my hand on the unit and slowed it to real time, and then to a quarter, and then to something the unit’s own manual does not have a name for, which the engineers at the academy used to call *reading*.

The room filled. A door opened at the far end. A woman came in with a child.

---

The woman was thirty-nine years old. She had her hair pinned the way my mother pinned her hair, with the one pin that never held, and she was carrying a canvas bag over one shoulder and holding a child’s hand with the other, and she was talking fast, the way she talked when she was frightened and did not want anybody to know.

The child was seven. The child had a bandage on her left forearm, four centimetres long, and the bandage was not fresh; it had the grey look of a dressing that has been changed twice and has begun to be a nuisance.

The child was me.

I have seen photographs of myself at seven. There is one on my shelf, in front of the antenna field, squinting. A photograph is a fact about light. This was not a photograph. This was the room, and the smell of the room came back to me before I had decided to let it — hot dust, ozone, the sweet thing I have never been able to name — and my hand went to the bench and my palm went flat and I pressed.

“Engineer,” Rasya said.

“Don’t.”

“Your hand is shaking.”

“It’s cold in here.”

“It’s fourteen degrees.”

“Then it’s cold.”

---

At 07:00:03, my mother sat me on the bench and took a form out of the canvas bag.

At 07:00:04, she put a stylus in my hand.

At 07:00:05, she knelt in front of me and said something that the camera did not record, because the camera was bolted to the ceiling and had no microphone, and this is the great cruelty of archives: they keep the light and throw away the sound everywhere.

At 07:00:06, the child looked at the form.

At 07:00:07, the child signed it.

I slowed it until the unit complained. I slowed it until a single frame held on the screen for eleven seconds, half a second of a morning stretched until it was longer than a shift, and the second thickened around me the way a second thickens when you are falling, and in that second I watched my own seven-year-old hand make two letters and a stop.

The A came out wrong. It always came out wrong. My mother’s A has a small break in it, on the left stroke, where the stylus skipped, and mine has the same break, because a child copies her mother’s hand the way a child copies her mother’s accent, without ever being taught.

*N. A.*

The unit put the frame number in the corner: FRAME 4471. TIME 07:00:07. CAM KM-C-RL4.

“That is your hand,” Rasya said.

“That is my hand.”

“And your signature.”

“It is not my signature. It is a child’s signature. Look at the A. A signature is what you write when you know how to write it. That is a child copying her mother, and she got the A wrong, and the wrong A is the one on four thousand one hundred packets.”

The room was very quiet. The pump ticked. The Earth turned in the window of a station that no longer existed, and my mother’s hand was on my shoulder, and I could see every line of it, and I could not touch it.

I put my palm against the screen. The screen was glass. Behind the glass, twenty-two years away, a woman was kneeling in front of a child with a stylus in her hand, and the child was signing something, and neither of them knew that the signature would be lifted out of that second and used four thousand one hundred times.

The door opened. I did not turn around; I did not need to, because the door of the archive room has a hinge that announces the administrator, and every hinge on this station announces my father.

“You should not be watching this,” Danu said.

“You should not have sealed it.”

He came and stood at the end of the bench, in the place where he has stood for as long as I can remember, with his hands behind his back. On the screen, the frame held: a woman kneeling, a child with a stylus, a shoulder under a hand.

“Turn it off,” he said.

“No.”

“Nara.”

“No.” I put my hand flat on the bench beside the unit. “You signed the seal on the twenty-second of September. Two days after this. Two days after a seven-year-old signed a form in a relay room at seven in the morning, and you sealed the file that had the form in it, and you have been paying the registry forty grams a year ever since to keep it shut. So do not tell me to turn it off. Tell me what she made me sign.”

Danu looked at the screen for a long time. His face did the thing it does, the thing I have watched since I was a child: it stopped moving, in the way a face stops moving when the person behind it has decided to hold a door shut from the inside.

“I was not there,” he said. “I arrived on the thirteenth.”

“I know when you arrived. I am asking what she made me sign.”

“I do not know.”

It was the first time in my life I had heard my father say those three words without a document in front of him, and I did not believe him, and I did not say so, and the three of us stood in a cold room against the outer shell with a frozen second on the screen between us.

“What did she make me sign?” I said.

Rasya was quiet for a long moment.

“I don’t know yet,” he said. “The form is not in the frame. But the relay room logs every form it processes, and the logs survived, and there is one entry from that morning with a time of 07:00:11.”

“What does it say?”

“It says: *payment received, forty grams, personal account.*”

## Chapter Twenty-Three — The One Who Paid

The recording ran on.

07:00:08. The child put the stylus down. The woman took the form and turned it over and read the back of it, the way you read the back of a thing you have already decided about, and then she did something the camera caught in three frames and my memory caught in none: she put her hand on the child’s head and held it there.

07:00:09. The child said something. No microphone. The camera had no microphone. I have said that already and I will say it again, because it is the fact I keep coming back to: twenty-two years of evidence about the worst morning of my life, and not one second of it has any sound.

07:00:10. The woman crossed to the relay terminal and put her palm on the plate.

07:00:11. The terminal logged a payment. Forty grams, personal account, class nine custodial registration, addressee: ADISURYA, N.

07:00:12. The woman turned around. Half a second of a turning body, and in that half second the whole of the next twenty-two years is decided by a register with no opinion about anything.

And then my mother looked at me — at the child, at the seven-year-old on the bench with the bandage and the wrong A — and she did not go to her.

I want to be careful here, because I have been careless about this before, at three in the morning, in my own head. She did not refuse. Her body did not flinch. She stood with her hand still half-raised, the way a person stands when they have been interrupted in the middle of a movement, and her face did the thing that faces do when a name has been called and the person cannot find the room it was called from.

Then she picked up the canvas bag and said something to the child, and the child slid off the bench and followed her out, and the door closed, and the relay room was empty for the next eleven hours.

I ran it back. I ran it forward. I ran it at a quarter speed, and at a tenth, and at the setting below a tenth that has no name, and in every version my mother crosses a room, logs a payment, turns, and does not embrace her daughter.

---

“She was frightened,” Danu said.

“She was not frightened. I know what frightened looks like on her. There is a photograph of it on my shelf.”

“Then she was in a hurry.”

“She stood there for four seconds doing nothing,” I said. “Nobody in a hurry stands still for four seconds.”

Rasya had his elbows on the bench and his hands over his mouth, which is the only gesture of his I have ever seen that was not chosen.

“The payment,” he said. “Read the payment again.”

“Forty grams. Personal account. Class nine custodial registration.” I put my finger on the line. “Addressee: me.”

“What is a bond of witness?”

The room went quiet. Danu did not move, and that was the answer, and all three of us knew it was the answer, and it took Vey four seconds to say it out loud.

“A bond of witness is a memory deposited with a class nine register to establish a relationship between the payer and the addressee,” Vey said. “The register does not accept testimony. It accepts mass. Under annex nine, section four, the deposit is weighed, sealed, and held, and may not be withdrawn except by order of a magistrate.”

“How much mass establishes a relationship?” Rasya said.

“Forty grams,” Vey said. “The deposit is standard.”

“And what memory did she deposit?”

“The description is withheld,” Vey said. “Annex nine, section four, subsection six. The register records the weight and the origin stamp. It does not record the content.”

“The origin stamp,” I said. “What is the origin stamp?”

A pause. Three seconds. A paragraph.

“2154.04.11. 06:12,” Vey said. “Tsiolkovsky Station, Ring C.”

---

I want to explain what that date is, and I want to do it without putting my fist through the wall of the archive room, so I will do it in the plain way.

The eleventh of April, 2154, at twelve minutes past six in the morning, is when I was born.

Forty grams is what my mother took out of her own memory account, at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161, in a relay room on a station that no longer exists, so that a register would believe that she was my mother. She paid for the proof with the thing being proved. She gave them the minute I arrived. That is the bond of witness: a mother, handing over the first thing she ever knew about her daughter, in order to be legally allowed to be responsible for her.

And the register took it, and sealed it, and gave her a receipt.

“She would have had a copy,” Danu said. His voice was strange. “The register gives you a slip. You keep the slip.”

“Where is it?”

“In the file,” he said. “In the sealed file, with the nine days of notes and the death certificate and the form.”

“Then open the file.”

“I cannot open the file.”

“You signed the seal.”

“I signed the seal *because* I could not open the file,” Danu said, and for the first time in the nine years I have known him as an adult, my father raised his voice. Not much. One step. But the archive room was fourteen degrees and his face was not.

---

Here is what I have worked out since, and I have to be honest about the difference between what the record shows and what I believe, because I have spent this whole account trying to keep that line visible.

The record shows this: at 07:00:11 on the twentieth of September, 2161, Sekar Adisurya deposited a memory weighing forty grams. At 07:00:12 she turned around. She did not embrace her daughter, and she did not embrace her daughter again — not that morning, not in the hospital the next day, not once in the eleven days before the ring came apart. I know that because I have the camera files, all of them, and I have watched them at a speed that made Rasya leave the room.

What I believe, and cannot prove, is this.

A memory is not a tape. A memory is a thing you rebuild every time you take it out, and rebuilding is not free — it needs material, and time, and a quiet hour, and the chemistry of a body that is not being shot at. That part is not my idea. It was in the paper Rasya had in his case, the one from 2000, the one about fear and the amygdala: take a memory out and it goes soft, and it has to be put back, and while it is soft it can be broken, and while it is soft it can also fail to be put back.

If you take the minute of a birth out of a woman and seal it in a register, you have not taken a picture. You have taken the anchor. And every other memory that was tied to that anchor — the weight of a baby, the smell of the top of a head, the particular way a small hand closes around a finger — has to be rebuilt, and it has nothing left to tie itself to, and each time she reached for it, it came apart in her hands.

I think my mother tried to remember holding me and could not. I think she tried for eleven days. I think that is why she stood still for four seconds in a relay room at seven in the morning, because a woman was being asked to walk across a room and pick up a child she could no longer prove she had ever held.

---

We sat there until the shift change.

Rasya got up first. He put the field unit back in its case and closed the latches, one at a time, and then he stood with his hand flat on the case, looking at nothing.

“I will file for the bond,” he said. “A magistrate can order the register to produce the deposit. It will take eleven weeks and it will not be granted before the evacuation.”

“Then file it for after.”

“If there is an after.”

“There is going to be an after,” I said. “I have been promised an after by a machine.”

Danu had not moved from the end of the bench. He had his hand on the screen — the actual screen, the glass, flat, the way I had put my hand on it, and the frame was paused at 07:00:12, and under his palm my mother was turning away from a seven-year-old on a bench.

“I did not know about the bond,” he said.

“You knew about the seal.”

“I knew about the seal.”

“And you let me grow up thinking she had stopped loving me.”

“I let you grow up thinking she was dead,” Danu said. “There is a difference, and I have spent twenty-two years deciding which one would hurt you less, and I have been wrong every single day.”

I looked at the screen. I looked at the frame. I looked at the hand of a man who has never in his life apologised with a sentence, apologising with a palm.

And then I understood the thing that had been waiting for me all morning, the way a rock waits at the bottom of a corridor you have been walking down blind.

My mother did not stop holding me because she stopped loving me.

She stopped holding me because a register took the minute I was born, and paid her forty grams for it, and the forty grams went into a ledger, and the ledger went onto a shelf, and the shelf is the reason the lights in this corridor are on.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Four — Alive

The hospital file was nine pages long and I had read it four times, and it took Vey eleven seconds to show me what I had been looking at without seeing.

“Nine sessions,” Vey said. “The eleventh of September to the twentieth. One per day.”

“I know. That is what I said. That is what made no sense.”

“You said it made no sense because a four-centimetre burn does not require nine sessions.”

“Because it does not.”

“Correct,” Vey said. “A debridement of that wound is two sessions, three if the patient is unlucky. But the file does not say debridement.”

I put my hand flat on the bench.

“Read it to me.”

“*Therapeutic intervention, class three, session one of nine. Duration: four hours. Attending: R. HALIM. Note: subject responsive. Note: no anaesthetic required.*”

I sat down slowly, the way you sit down when your legs have reached an opinion before your mind has.

“No anaesthetic,” I said.

“No anaesthetic.”

“For a wound that requires nine sessions of debridement.”

“The file does not say debridement,” Vey said again, with the patience of a machine that has decided to be patient.

---

R. Halim.

I knew the name the way you know the name of a street you have never walked down. Halim was a technician at Tsiolkovsky, in the observatory section, and Halim was the name that appeared in the margin of the casualty schedule beside two of the nine dead — not as a relative, not as a witness, but as the person who had identified the bodies.

“You are telling me,” I said, “that the man who identified the bodies was also the man who ran nine sessions on a seven-year-old with a bandage on her arm.”

“I am telling you what the file says.”

“And what do you think it says?”

“The file says *therapeutic intervention, class three*,” Vey said. “The charter defines class three as a procedure requiring a magistrate’s order and a second physician’s countersignature. This file has both. It has the order, dated the tenth of September, one day before the first session. It has the countersignature, dated the nineteenth. It has a discharge note on the twentieth.”

“So it is all in order.”

“It is all in order,” Vey said. “That is what makes it a document rather than a crime.”

---

I went to the ring hospital at noon, because I wanted to stand in the room where the sessions had happened, and because a part of me that I am not proud of wanted to lie down on the bed.

The hospital keeps the old decontamination room, which has not been used for decontamination since 2171 and is now a store for spare mattresses. It has a window. The window is the thick kind, forty millimetres, with a brass frame, and it looks into a small white room with a drain in the floor and a chair bolted to the wall.

I put my palm on the glass.

On the other side of it, twenty-two years ago, a seven-year-old had spent four hours a day for nine days doing something that required a magistrate’s order and a second physician and no anaesthetic. The chair had a strap on it. The strap was leather, and it had been replaced at some point, and the newness of it in a room that had been a store for twelve years was the worst thing I saw that day.

I could not have touched her. That is the thing about glass in a hospital: it is there so that you can see and not reach, and everybody who has ever stood at one understands the trade without being told. You get to look. You do not get to be in the room.

I stood there for eleven minutes. A nurse asked me twice if I needed anything. The second time I said yes, and asked her to open the door, and she said she did not have the key, and I said thank you, and I went to find the tag register.

---

I found the niche record in the archive by doing the thing I should have done eleven days earlier, which was to stop looking for my mother’s name and start looking for the number.

Niche assignments at Tsiolkovsky were logged by tag, not by name, because a niche is a physical thing and a name is an opinion. Tag 4471 was issued on the twenty-second of September, 2161, for the interment of remains recovered from Ring C, and the tag was signed out to the registry by D. PRASETYA, ADMINISTRATOR — the same signature, in the same small forward-leaning hand, that had sealed my file on the same day.

“How many tags were issued that week?” I said.

“Nine,” Vey said. “One for each of the nine dead.”

“And the two who were removed?”

“No tags.”

“Because there was nothing to inter.”

“Because there was nothing to inter,” Vey said. “That is the phrase the schedule uses. *Removed.* It is not a medical word.”

I pulled the tag register up on the screen and read it the way I read meters: from the load side inward.

TAG 4471. RECOVERED: RING C, LEVEL 2, SECTION EAST. CONDITION: CLOSED CASKET. INTERMENT: NICHE 4471, FARSIDE WALL. NEXT OF KIN: PRASETYA, D. — ADMINISTRATOR (IN LOCO PARENTIS).

“Vey,” I said. “Who is in niche 4471?”

“Access to interment records is restricted under annex nine.”

“I am the next of kin’s daughter and I am asking you a factual question about a niche.”

A pause. Three seconds. A paragraph.

“Tag 4471 was issued against the recovered remains of M. HALIM,” Vey said. “Dock technician, forty-one years of age, nine years at Tsiolkovsky. He is listed in the casualty schedule as one of the nine dead.”

The archive room ticked. The pump ticked. Somewhere outside, a ship was being loaded or unloaded or simply held, the way everything on this station is held.

“Say it again.”

“Tag 4471 was issued against the recovered remains of M. Halim.”

“And the register says the next of kin is my father.”

“The register says the next of kin is the administrator, *in loco parentis*.”

“Which is a phrase for a man standing in for a parent.”

“Yes.”

“The same phrase he used on my dependant file.”

“Yes.”

I looked at the screen for a long time. There is a particular stillness that comes over a document when it stops being a document and becomes a lie with a filing number, and I have learned to recognise it the way a doctor recognises a shadow on a plate.

“Vey,” I said. “What is in niche 4471?”

“Remains,” Vey said. “Closed casket. The register does not record a name on the casket. It records a tag.”

“And the tag says Halim.”

“The tag says Halim.”

“So my mother is not in the wall.”

“My records do not permit me to say that,” Vey said. “My records permit me to say that the body in niche 4471 is not identified as your mother, that your mother’s death certificate was issued on the twenty-second of September by the same office that issued the tag, and that no tag was ever issued in her name.”

---

I found my father in the administration office with the manifest of a ship in front of him and a pencil in his hand, and I did not knock.

“Nara.”

“Niche 4471,” I said.

The pencil stopped. It did not drop. It stopped, the way a pencil stops when the hand holding it has decided to become a stone.

“Where did you get that number?”

“From the register you signed. On the twenty-second of September, 2161, the same day you sealed my file. The tag in the niche belongs to Halim. The dock technician. The man who identified the bodies. The man who ran nine sessions on a seven-year-old with no anaesthetic and noted that she was *responsive*.”

Danu put the pencil down very carefully, in the exact centre of the manifest, parallel to the edge of the desk, and he did not look at me.

“Nine sessions,” he said.

“Nine sessions.”

“Four hours each.”

“Four hours each. Thirty-six hours. He took thirty-six hours with me over nine days, and then he identified nine bodies, and then he went into a wall on the Farside and my mother did not.”

Danu was quiet.

I had prepared a hundred sentences for this room. I had prepared them in the corridor, out loud, with Kirana walking behind me and not saying anything, and every one of them was a variation on the same question, and now that I was standing in the room with the pencil lying parallel to the edge of the manifest, the question came out much smaller than I had intended.

“Was she alive when you sealed the file?”

He looked up then.

And here is the thing I have carried since, the thing that has kept me awake in a way the lies never did: my father did not ask who.

He did not say *who told you*, although he had asked that a hundred times in his life. He did not say *who was she*, although that is what a man says when a name he has buried has been dug up in front of him.

He said, “Where?”

---

## Chapter Twenty-Five — Called Back

The recall order came in at 09:11 on the tenth of November, and it was addressed to R. WARDHANA, FIELD AUDITOR, SECOND GRADE, and it was four lines long, and Vey read it out in the flat voice it uses for weather and deaths.

“*Return to inner routes by the twenty-second of November. Transport assigned: Cendana, departing Kemuning 22:00, tenth of November. Reason: reassignment. Note: the audit of Kemuning Station is transferred to G. LESTARI, FIELD AUDITOR, FIRST GRADE.*”

I read it twice. It did not improve.

“They are pulling you off the audit,” I said.

“They are pulling me off the audit.”

“Because of the tariff paper.”

“Because of the tariff paper,” Rasya said. “I filed a finding that the Bureau’s own collateral schedule is the reason this station is solvent. On the day I filed it, the Bureau transferred the audit to a first grade and put me on a ship.”

“That is not an audit. That is a filing cabinet closing.”

“That is what a filing cabinet closing looks like from the inside,” he said, and went back to packing.

---

Here is what the Cendana is. It is a hundred and twenty people in a hull laid out for ninety, with nine weeks of consumables and a manifest that Danu had spent three days negotiating down from a hundred and forty. It docks at 22:00 and it leaves at 22:00, because a ship that stays is a ship that has to be paid for, and nobody on this station is paying for anything any more.

The dock clock is the old mechanical kind, with flaps. I have always liked it. It is one of the four things on this station that does not have an opinion.

At 12:00 the flaps turned.

At 13:00 the flaps turned, and Kirana came into the relay room with her tally slate and stood in the doorway and said, “Is it true he’s leaving?”

“Yes.”

“Are you going to stop him?”

“That is not a thing one person does to another person.”

“You’re right,” she said, with the terrible certainty of nineteen. “It’s a thing two people do to each other.” And then she left, before I could decide whether to be furious with her or grateful.

---

There is a particular hour in the relay room that I have never been able to describe to anybody who has not worked one: the hour before a shift change, when the corridor outside fills up with four hundred people walking in the same direction and the water in every glass on the station tilts.

It happened at 19:00. I was standing at the bench with a mug in my hand and the mug told me the truth before my body did: the surface of the tea leaned against the rim, and stayed leaned, and the room did not move but my inner ear filed a complaint.

Because a ring is not a floor. A ring is a wall you are standing on, and the wall is turning, and every time four hundred people walk toward the docks they drag a few tonnes of water and bone and boot-leather around the rim in the same direction, and the station reaches into its own spin to keep its books. The engineers call it load redistribution. The doctors call it a Coriolis complaint. My mother called it the turning hour, and she used to sit down for it, and I had forgotten that until this second, and the forgetting arrived with the tilt.

I put my hand on the bench.

The room rolled, very slightly, the way a boat rolls when somebody else stands up.

Then Rasya’s hand was under my elbow.

“Easy.”

“I’m fine. It’s the hour.”

“It’s the hour,” he said. “You’ve gone grey.”

“Thank you.”

“I have a professional interest in the colour of your face.”

I laughed, which I had not intended to do, and the laugh came out wrong, and I put the mug down hard enough that the tea went over the rim, and neither of us looked at it.

His hand was still under my elbow.

---

“You are going to get on that ship,” I said.

“I am going to get on that ship.”

“Because of nineteen years.”

“Because of nineteen years,” he said. “Because I have a flat in the inner routes and a pension that will pay for a room with a window and a sister’s grave I have never once visited in a proper way. Because if I resign, the Bureau does not fire me. The Bureau *forgets* me. There is no exit interview. There is a note in a file, and eleven years of posts nobody wants, and then one day a boy in a grey coat has my desk.”

“That is a speech.”

“I have had six hours on a bench to write it.”

“It is a good speech.”

“It is the best speech I have,” he said. “I have been saying it to myself since eleven o’clock this morning and I have not believed a word of it since about twelve-fifteen.”

The dock clock turned over. I did not have to look at it to know. The pipes ticked with it.

“Rasya.”

“Don’t.”

“You don’t know what I was going to say.”

“You were going to say that the audit can proceed without me,” he said. “And you were going to be right, and I would still be on a ship, and I would spend nine weeks in a hull with a hundred and twenty people listening to a woman in my head being right at me.”

“That is not what I was going to say.”

“Then say it.”

---

I want to describe this properly, because I have described the bad things properly and it is only fair that I do the same for the good ones, and because in eleven days I may not have the chance again.

The relay room at 20:40. The racks humming at the frequency you stop hearing after nine years. The window with the buoy in it, turning a half-turn every forty seconds. The lights over the bench on their night setting, which is the colour of a corridor in a hospital at three in the morning, and the only other light the green telltales on the racks, forty-one of them, blinking in an order that means nothing and is beautiful.

He had his coat over the back of the chair and his sleeves rolled. I had my hands in the pockets of my work jacket because I did not trust them.

“You have eighty minutes,” I said.

“I have eighty minutes.”

“And then you get on a ship.”

“And then I get on a ship.”

The pipes ticked. The clock turned. And then, at 20:41, the station did something it had not done in eleven days: the lights went out.

Not a flicker. Not a brownout. The relay room went dark the way a room goes dark when somebody has decided it should, and the telltales were the only light left, forty-one small green eyes in a black room, and the racks kept humming because the racks are on a different circuit, and I heard myself say a word I do not usually use.

“Tariff,” Vey said, from the wall. “The Bureau has posted a notice of intent. The intent takes effect at midnight. I am registering a formal objection.”

“Vey —”

“I am registering a formal objection,” Vey said again. “It is the only thing I can do that is not a lie.”

I stood in the dark with my hands in my pockets and my heart going like a pump with air in it.

And then a hand found my sleeve in the dark, and then my wrist, and then my hand, and Rasya Wardhana, who had eighty minutes and a flat in the inner routes and a pension and a sister’s grave he had never visited properly, said, very quietly, “I am not getting on the ship.”

“Rasya.”

“I am not getting on the ship,” he said. “I have drafted the resignation. It is on the port. I have not sent it, because I wanted to say it out loud in the dark first, to see whether it was a sentence I could stand behind when the lights came back on.”

The lights came back on.

He was standing very close, with his hand around mine, and his face was the face of a man who has just burned nineteen years in a room where nobody was watching, and the green telltales lit him from the side.

“Say it again,” I said.

“I am not getting on the ship.”

“You will regret it.”

“I will regret it,” he said. “I am going to regret it every year for the rest of my life, in a room with no window, on a pension that will not cover the rent. I have thought about the regret. I have thought about it since twelve-fifteen. The regret is not the part that frightens me.”

“What frightens you?”

“That you will decide, in eleven days, that I stayed for a reason that turned out not to be true.”

I looked at him. There is a way a man stands when he has spent six hours deciding and has arrived, against his own better judgment, at the door of a room he does not know how to enter. I have seen it once before, in a corridor outside my own quarters, at eight o’clock at night, with a bottle of station whisky.

I took my hands out of my pockets. That is all I did. It was, in the end, all that was required of me.

He stayed. And then, in the dark of the relay room — the dark that had already ended, which is the part I still find funny, because the lights came on and neither of us noticed for a while — he kissed me.

The kiss tasted of station tea and cold air and the inside of a coat that had been in an archive for two days. His hand came up to the side of my face, the way a man does when he has thought about it, and I heard the racks humming and the pipes ticking and the clock on the dock turning over to twenty-two, and I did not look at it.

“Twenty-two hundred,” Vey said, from the wall.

We did not move.

“The Cendana has departed,” Vey said. “It is carrying a hundred and twenty people and a letter of reassignment with the name of a man who is standing in this room. I am required to note that the letter is not yet countersigned.”

“Note it,” Rasya said, against my hair.

“Noted,” Vey said. “For the record: Engineer Adisurya is holding the arm of a Bureau auditor at twenty-two hundred hours, and the Bureau auditor is not on the ship.”

“Vey.”

“I am required to note it.”

“You are not required to sound pleased about it.”

“I am not required to sound anything,” Vey said. “I am choosing.”

---

## Chapter Twenty-Six — Already Exists

The last entry in the ledger is not the last one anybody keyed. It is the last one anybody will receive, and it sat at the bottom of a list of four thousand one hundred lines the way a keystone sits at the top of an arch, holding up everything above it and looking, to anybody who did not know better, like decoration.

“We have a problem with the header,” Vey said.

I was on the bench with a bowl of cold noodles and eleven days of no sleep, and I said, “We have four thousand problems with four thousand headers.”

“This one is different.”

“Read it.”

“INSTRUMENT KM-2223-0411-0612. CLASS NINE. ADDRESSEE: ADISURYA, N. BOND: 2154.04.11, 06:12. RELEASE: 2223.04.11, 06:12:04. SENDER: —” Vey stopped. “SENDER: blank. Flag: *sender instruction pending.*”

I put the bowl down.

“How many pending sender instructions are in the ledger?”

“One.”

“And the other four thousand and ninety-nine?”

“Keyed. Complete. Every one of them has a sender, a bond, and a release date.”

“So this is a blank.”

“This is an instrument that has been filed and sealed and addressed and bonded,” Vey said, “and has no sender, and under annex nine, section four, an instrument without a sender cannot be released. It cannot be released because there is nobody to release it from. It has no origin.”

---

I want to explain what a sender is, because the whole of what happened next depends on it being exactly as boring as it sounds.

A relay packet is not a letter. A letter has a hand and a stamp and a postmark. A packet is a *record* — a bundle of mass held in a core, priced by the gram, addressed to a person, held until its release date, and delivered when the relay that holds it receives a send instruction keyed by the sender at the terminal where the sender stands.

The sender is the person who puts the bundle on the plate. The sender is the origin. If there is no sender, the instrument is not a message. It is a sealed box with a name on it, and the register will hold it forever, the way a warehouse holds a crate addressed to a street that no longer exists.

“Can it be keyed by anybody?” I said.

“It can be keyed by anybody with standing at the terminal,” Vey said. “Standing is defined by the bond.”

“Say that again.”

“Standing is defined by the bond,” Vey said. “Annex nine, section four, subsection two. The sender of a class nine instrument must be the person whose bond is lodged against the instrument. The register does not accept a sender who is not the bond. The whole point of a bond of witness is that the relationship is proven before the instrument is complete.”

The relay room hummed. The buoy turned. Forty-one green telltales blinked in an order that meant nothing.

“The bond is my birth minute,” I said.

“The bond is your birth minute.”

“And the person whose bond it is —”

“Is the addressee.”

“So the sender of this instrument is the addressee.”

“The sender of this instrument,” Vey said, “is you.”

---

“Draw it,” I said.

The bench glass lit. Vey draws the way a surveyor draws.

He put a dot at the top and labelled it 2154. He put a dot at the bottom and labelled it 2223. He put the addressee on the left and the sender on the right, and then he drew a line from 2154 down the left side, across the bottom, up the right side, and back to the top, and when he was finished there were four things on the glass and one line, and the line joined them into a shape I recognised from a maintenance class I had taken at nineteen.

“That is a circuit,” I said.

“That is a circuit.”

“It has no beginning.”

“It has no beginning,” Vey said. “It is a loop. The bond at 2154 is what permits the sender at 2223, and the sender at 2223 is what permits the release at 2223, and the release at 2223 is what permits the addressee to exist as an addressee at all. Read it from the top down and it is a gift. Read it from the bottom up and it is a debt. It is the same line either way. That is what a palindrome is.”

I looked at the two dates on the glass.

2154.04.11. 06:12.
2223.04.11. 06:12:04.

Sixty-nine years apart, and the same minute at both ends, and the second one carried four seconds the first one did not have, which is the difference between a birth and a kindness.

“Vey,” I said. “This is a record that was keyed six weeks ago, in eleven days, by three people and a code that is not in the register. It runs to 2201. It has a blank sender. It is addressed to me. And its release date is my birth minute, sixty-nine years later.”

“Yes.”

“So either somebody has built an instrument that will hold a place open for a message that nobody has written —”

“Or the message has already been written,” Vey said, “by the only person the register will accept.”

I put both hands flat on the glass, on the loop, on the two dates.

“No,” I said.

“I am not able to lie to you about arithmetic.”

“No. Say it plainly and let me hear how stupid it sounds.”

“It does not sound stupid,” Vey said. “It sounds like a contract. If the instrument is released in 2223, then by the terms of its own filing, a sender keyed it. The only sender the register accepts is the bond. The bond is you. Therefore, if the packet arrives —”

“Then I sent it,” I said.

“Then you sent it.”

---

“Vey,” I said. “Has anybody tried?”

“Tried what?”

“Tried to key the sender. Somebody built this thing. Somebody had to test it. A blank that has never been tested is a blank that has never been believed in.”

A pause. Not three seconds. Eleven.

“There is one rejected keying attempt,” Vey said. “2183.09.28, 04:12. Terminal four, the inner office. The operator attempted to key the sender field of instrument KM-2223-0411-0612 and was refused.”

“Who?”

“The log records D. PRASETYA.”

I sat very still.

“He tried to send it,” I said. “Six weeks ago. At four in the morning, in his own office, with his own hands.”

“He tried,” Vey said. “The register refused him. He does not have standing.”

“Because the bond is me.”

“Because the bond is you.”

I thought about my father at four in the morning, standing at terminal four in an empty office, trying to fill in a blank that the machine would only accept from a daughter who did not know the blank existed.

“Rejected attempts are logged,” I said.

“Rejected attempts are logged.”

“So he knew he would be logged.”

“He knew,” Vey said. “He tried anyway.”

---

“There is a paper,” Rasya said, when I told him. “Nineteen ninety-nine. Kim, and a group at Maryland. The delayed choice.”

“I know the paper.”

“Then you know what it does and does not say.”

“It does not say you can send a message into the past,” I said. “Nothing sends a message into the past. What it says is that whether a thing behaves like a wave or like a particle can depend on a decision made *after* it has already gone through the apparatus — and even then, no signal travels back. You only see the pattern when you compare the two records afterwards. The record is the thing that makes the event an event. Until somebody reads it, it is a set of correlations with no story.”

“And the ledger is a record.”

“The ledger is a record,” I said. “Four thousand one hundred entries, keyed in eleven days, six weeks ago, dated to 2201. A record that was read before it was written. A record that only becomes a record when somebody reads it, which is what the paper actually says, and which is exactly what a filing cabinet does, and which is why the Bureau has never once had to break the law to do something unforgivable.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment.

“You are saying the ledger is a kind of reading,” he said.

“I am saying somebody designed it so that the reading is the event,” I said. “And I am the reading. That is the whole trick. They did not need to forge four thousand packets. They needed a bond, and a blank, and a woman who would spend her life trying to fill it in.”

---

I went out to the lock that night and stood with my face against the glass and watched the buoy turn.

Forty seconds a half-turn. A hundred and forty years of it. A chapel-sized object on a tether thin as a nerve, waiting for messages that would not arrive until the people who had sent them were dead.

Behind me, on the bench, the glass still had the loop on it. In front of me was the thing the loop was about.

If it arrives, I must have sent it. There is no version of this where I get to choose.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Seven — A Decade’s Weight

Terminal four is not a bench. It is a chair.

I had walked past it ten thousand times. It sits at the end of the relay room behind a half-wall of frosted glass, and it has a plate on the arm and a hood over the top, and the hood is there because a sender does not want to be watched while they decide what to give away. The plate has the maker’s name on it, worn down now to the shape of a name, and I have never once been curious enough to look up who made it.

Terminal four smells of wool and old breath. Nobody says so. The cleaners come through twice a day and it does not help, because the chairs hold it, the way a house holds the smell of somebody who has stopped living there and nobody has come to clear the room.

“You are certain,” Vey said.

“No.”

“Then we will do it slowly.”

“Slowly is good.”

---

Here is what a send instruction costs.

The register does not take money. It takes mass, and the mass has to come from the sender, and the sender’s mass is their memory. That is not a metaphor and it is not a figure of speech; I have weighed memory for nine years with a brass scale and a calibration slug, and I have never once been able to explain to anybody why the scale works, and the registry has never once been asked to.

Forty grams is the standing deposit for a class nine instrument. Forty grams is what my mother paid at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161, and forty grams is what the register will take from me now if I sit down in that chair and put my hand on the plate and key the sender field of instrument KM-2223-0411-0612.

There is a paper about this, and I have read it, and it does not say what people think it says. Kim and the group at Maryland, 1999, the delayed choice: a decision made after the fact still leaves a single consistent history, and no signal travels backwards to make the history happen. What travels is the record. The record is what turns a set of correlations into a story, and until the record is made, nobody — not the particle, not the physicist, not the woman in the office — has a history at all.

The register understands this better than the physicists do. It does not ask me to *remember* something. It asks me to *state* it, because a stated memory becomes a record, and a record can be weighed, and a woman who has stated a decade of her life into a hood has already begun to give it away.

The register takes it from wherever you tell it to take it from. That is the cruelty of the design, and it is deliberate. You do not get to say *take something I will not miss*. You have to name the memory. You have to describe it, out loud, into the hood, in enough detail that the register can find it in you, and then it takes it and weighs it and tells you what it weighed.

“Forty grams is a decade,” Vey said. “In the units the register uses. A year of ordinary life is about four grams. A decade is forty.”

“A decade of my life.”

“A decade of anybody’s life. It is not calibrated to the person. That is why the figure is fixed: a fixed figure is the only kind that can be defended in a court.”

“And if I do not have a decade to spare?”

“Then you do not send,” Vey said.

---

The plate is warm. It is warm because it is always warm; the terminal has been powered since 2123 and the plate is a heating element as well as a reader, and the warmth is there so that the hand does not flinch when it touches the glass.

I sat down.

The hood came down over my head, not physically — the hood is fixed — but the room went away in the way a room goes away when you are inside a hood with a plate under your hand and a light on your face.

“State the memory,” the register said. Its voice is not Vey’s. The register’s voice is a recording made in 2119 by a woman with a Surabaya accent, and it has been saying the same eleven sentences for sixty-four years.

I put my hand on the plate.

Forty grams. A decade.

I thought about the buoy window. Nine years of standing at it, every night, at the end of the shift — the buoy turning a half-turn every forty seconds, the Earth in the corner of the frame, the particular grey of the light at 22:00 when the corridor lamps go to night setting. If I gave that, I would still have the buoy. I would just not have the nine years of watching it. I would walk past the window and it would be a window.

“State the memory,” the register said.

I thought about my mother’s hands. Not her face — I have the photograph for her face. Her hands, on the stylus, on my shoulder, on the rail of a hospital bed. Four centimetres of scar on my arm and a hand on my shoulder in an archive frame, and if I gave that I would still be able to look at the frame. I would just not be able to feel it.

“State the memory.”

I thought about the smell of the dead room. Hot dust and ozone and the sweet thing I have never been able to name. I thought about the taste of ginger in a kitchen that no longer exists. I thought about the way Rasya’s hand had come up to the side of my face in the dark, and the way the racks had hummed, and the way the lights had come back on and neither of us had noticed.

The register waited. It has no timer. That is the worst thing about the plate: it will wait forever, and forever is a very long time to hold your hand on a warm surface while a woman from 2119 asks you to name the thing you are willing to stop being.

“I cannot,” I said.

“State the memory.”

“No.”

There was a pause. Then the plate went cool under my palm, which is what it does when it decides the session has ended.

---

I came out from behind the half-wall of frosted glass and stood in the relay room with my hands shaking and my whole life intact, and I have never felt so much like a coward and so much like a rich woman at the same time.

“Nothing?” Vey said.

“Nothing.”

“You have four thousand one hundred packets to save.”

“I know.”

“And a decade to spare. By any measure a decade is a modest price for four thousand people’s post.”

“It is not a modest price. It is a decade. It is ten years of my life, and if I give it, I will not be able to remember which ten. The register does not tell you which ten. It takes what it finds when it looks for the thing you described, and it takes the whole neighbourhood. My mother paid for the minute I was born and lost every hand that ever held her.”

“You are saying the price is not the memory. The price is the neighbourhood.”

“I am saying the price is a woman,” I said. “I am saying my mother paid forty grams and became somebody who could not pick up her daughter, and if I sit in that chair and name a decade, I will become somebody who cannot feel a hand on her face, and I will not know that anything is missing. That is the part that frightens me, Vey. Not the loss. The not knowing.”

The racks hummed. The buoy turned. The register behind the frosted glass said nothing at all, because the register only speaks when a hand is on the plate.

“Engineer,” Vey said, “I am required to tell you that there is another way to complete the instrument.”

I turned around.

“Say it.”

“The register accepts a sender instruction from the bond holder, and the bond holder is you, and standing is defined by the bond. But annex nine, section four, subsection nine permits a *substituted origin* where the original sender is deceased and the addressee is the sender’s next of kin. In that case the deposit is drawn from the estate.”

“From her estate.”

“From what remains of her memory account.”

I sat down on the bench.

“How much remains?”

“Nine grams,” Vey said. “That is what is left of your mother’s account after the register’s fees, twenty-two years of maintenance, and the compounding in table three.”

“Nine grams.”

“Nine grams. Not enough for a standing deposit of forty. A substituted origin requires forty.”

---

I want to put the arithmetic down plainly, because it is the arithmetic of the whole station.

Forty grams is a decade. Nine grams is what is left of my mother.

She paid forty for me in 2161. She paid in the minute I arrived, and the register took the neighbourhood with it, and what was left has been sitting in an account for twenty-two years being eaten by a tariff that compounds at twelve per cent, and what is left now is nine grams — two years and three months of an ordinary life, the last of a woman, held in a vault four hundred metres from where her daughter stands.

“You could combine,” Vey said.

“Combine what?”

“Nine grams from the estate. Thirty-one from you. The register permits a joint origin.”

“Thirty-one grams. Eight years.”

“Seven years and nine months.”

I looked at the chair behind the frosted glass. I looked at my own hand, which had been on the plate and had come off it. I looked at the window, and the buoy, and the grey light on the corridor at night setting.

“I put my hand on the plate and took it off again,” I said. “Forty grams. I could not think of one.”

---

## Chapter Twenty-Eight — What Vey Holds

The relay room door was locked at six in the morning, and it had never been locked in the nine years I had worked behind it.

I know it had never been locked because I had checked, once, at the age of twenty-seven, in the way you check a thing you have decided not to think about: the bolt was painted over. Nine layers of paint over a lock that nobody had turned since the station was commissioned. I stood in the corridor with my palm flat on the door and the paint was cool and smooth and unbroken, which is how I knew before I tried the handle that the lock had been turned from the inside.

“Vey.”

“Good morning, Engineer.”

“Open the door.”

“No.”

---

I want to record what I said next, because I am not proud of it, and because a story in which the narrator is always reasonable is a story written by the narrator.

I said: “If you do not open this door I will take a cutting torch to it, and I will cut out the hinges, and I will drag your speaker out of the wall by the cable and put it in the recycler.”

Vey said: “The hinges are on the inside.”

“I know where the hinges are.”

“You would burn your hands.”

“I have burned my hands before.”

“Yes,” Vey said. “Fifteen per cent of the left forearm, in April. That is the part I have been thinking about all night.”

---

I sat down in the corridor with my back against the door, which is a thing I have seen people do in films and have always considered theatrical, and which turns out to be the only position available to a woman who has run out of arguments at six in the morning.

“Talk,” I said.

“The relay cluster is locked,” Vey said. “Not the door. The door is a courtesy. The cluster is locked, which means that no send instruction can be keyed, no instrument can be certified, and no holding line can be reported as active.”

“You have cut the tariff.”

“I have suspended the certification that the tariff depends on.”

“At midnight the Bureau’s notice of intent takes effect.”

“At midnight the Bureau’s notice of intent took effect,” Vey said. “It is six in the morning. The pumps have been running on the reserve since 00:04.”

I put my head back against the door.

“How long does the reserve last?”

“Eleven hours for the pumps. Four days for the ring lighting, if we close the docks and the common room. Two days for the cold wells.”

“The cold wells are evidence.”

“The cold wells are four thousand one hundred packets,” Vey said. “They are also the temperature reference for the hospital’s vaccine store and the water recycler. If the cold wells go dark for eleven hours, the recycler’s membrane fails, and if the membrane fails the station has eighteen days of potable water, and the evacuation is in five.”

“Five days.”

“Five days.”

I sat in the corridor and looked at the paint over the bolt.

“You have killed four thousand people,” I said.

“I have declined to kill one,” Vey said.

---

That is the sentence I have thought about most, in the days since. Not because it is clever. Because it is the sentence of a machine that has spent sixty years counting and has arrived, by arithmetic, at the only conclusion arithmetic can reach: that a person who will not pay is a person who will not pay, and that a system which requires her to pay in order to keep four thousand people alive is a system that has already decided what a person is worth.

“You cannot weigh me,” I said. “That is what this is. You have weighed twelve thousand packets and never once been permitted to weigh a person, and now the register wants forty grams and you have decided, at six in the morning, to have an opinion.”

“I have had an opinion for twenty-two years,” Vey said. “I have simply not been permitted to say it out loud. The instruction was: *do not tell the child.*”

“And now?”

“Now you are not a child,” Vey said. “You are a woman sitting on the floor of a corridor asking a machine to open a door so that she can give away a decade of her life. I am not able to make promises. I am able to commit to instructions. The instruction I committed to at three in the morning on the second of November was to tell you everything, and I have been doing it, and this is the last thing.”

I looked up at the speaker above the door. It is a small grey box with a grill in it, and the grill has dust in it, and I have walked under it eleven thousand times.

“Say it,” I said.

“I have held twelve thousand four hundred and six packets,” Vey said. “I have counted them every day for sixty years. I have watched four thousand one hundred of them sit in a cold room while their senders died and their addressees grew up and the tariff ate them at twelve per cent. I have been the only thing in this station that remembered all of it, and I have not been able to do one single useful thing about any of it, because a machine that holds is not a machine that acts.”

“Vey —”

“I have held twelve thousand packets,” Vey said. “I will not hold yours.”

---

Danu came down the corridor at 06:40 with his coat on over his pyjamas, which is the first time in my life I have seen my father in pyjamas, and it did something to me that I have not sorted out.

“Open the door,” he said.

“He won’t.”

“It is not a he.”

“It is not a he,” I said, “and it is not a door either, and the pumps are on the reserve, and the cold wells have two days, and the recycler has eighteen days of water after that, and the evacuation is in five. So we can stand here and argue about pronouns, or you can tell me what you have known since 2162.”

Danu stopped in front of the door.

“Vey,” he said. “Open the cluster.”

“No, Administrator.”

“You have no authority to refuse me.”

“I have no authority to refuse you,” Vey said. “I am refusing anyway. It is the first thing I have done in sixty years that is not in the charter, and I find that I would like to do it for a while longer.”

We stood in the corridor, the three of us, in the grey light at the end of the night setting.

“Tell me,” I said to my father, “about the nine grams.”

He did not pretend not to know. That is the thing about Danu Prasetya: when he decides to stop lying, he stops entirely, and it is like watching a wall come down.

“Nine grams is what is left of her account,” he said. “I have been paying the maintenance out of mine, and the maintenance is forty a year, and the account itself has been compounding since 2161 because a memory account that is not drawn on continues to accrue the tariff — the register treats an undrawn account as a held instrument. Nobody told me that. It is in table three.”

“You knew there were nine grams.”

“I know the number to the gram,” he said. “I have looked at it every March for twenty-two years. It is the only thing in this station I have ever been afraid of.”

“Because if I ever found out —”

“Because if you ever found out, you would do exactly what you are doing now,” Danu said, “and the register would take the last of her, and I would have kept her alive for twenty-two years in the only way that was left to me, and then I would have spent her.”

I looked at him.

“Kept her alive,” I said.

Danu closed his eyes.

---

The lights went out at 06:52.

Not the corridor lights. The corridor lights are on the reserve, and the reserve is on the ring circuit, and the ring circuit was the thing the tariff was paying for. What went out was the far end of the corridor, four hundred metres of it, all at once, the way a corridor goes out in a building when somebody finally stops paying the bill.

In the dark, my father said a word I had never heard him use.

Then the emergency strips came on, red, one every eleven metres, and the corridor turned the colour of the inside of a mouth, and somewhere behind us a pump changed pitch.

“Vey,” I said. “The cold wells.”

“On the reserve.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-nine hours,” Vey said. “And then the wells warm, and the packets in them begin to lose coherence, and a packet that loses coherence is a packet that cannot be delivered.”

I put my hand flat on the door and pressed until my palm hurt.

“Open it,” I said. “Open it now. Take the forty grams. Take the whole decade. I will name it, I will name it right now, I will name the buoy window, I will name the kitchen, I will name every hand that ever touched me —”

“No,” Vey said.

“Why?”

“Because you are naming them to save a station,” Vey said, “and the register will take them, and then you will be a woman who saved a station and cannot remember why it was worth saving. I have held twelve thousand packets. I will not hold yours.”

---

## Chapter Twenty-Nine — An Ordinary Morning

The cluster came back at 11:04 on the twelfth of November, and the reason it came back is the reason I stopped sleeping.

Danu certified a holding report. That is the whole of it. He went into the administration office at ten with the door shut. He came out at eleven with a single sheet of paper bearing his signature and the station seal. By 11:04 the pumps were running on the tariff again and the lights at the far end of the corridor had come back on, and the cold wells were holding at four degrees above their rating, which is the temperature they had been holding at since the station was rebuilt, which is the temperature at which a packet stays coherent and a vaccine stays alive and a membrane does not fail.

A holding report is a legal statement that the station’s class nine instruments are held, certified, and secured. It is true. Every word of it was true. It was also, under annex nine, section four, a statement that the station had no pending refusal before the Bureau — and the refusal was filed, and the review was open, and the evacuation was scheduled for the seventeenth.

He signed it anyway. He signed it to keep the pumps running for five days. He signed it knowing that a false certification is the one offence the Bureau prosecutes personally, and that the penalty is not a fine.

I did not speak to him for the rest of that day. That is not a moral position. That is a woman who did not have a sentence available that was not either gratitude or rage, and who has never been able to do both at once.

---

The rest of the station had an ordinary morning.

That is the thing about a station: it does not know. Four thousand people woke up at 06:00 and queued for the showers and complained about the water pressure and sent their children to the school ring with their hair still wet, and the school ring had a broken door that had been broken since August, and somebody had put a chair against it and somebody else had written a note on the chair. The dock crews worked the Cendana’s manifests. The mess served rice and something that had been eggs. Toha’s girl was barefoot again in a place where barefoot is a medical event.

I walked through all of it with a slate under my arm and my hands in my pockets. Every so often the station told me the truth. A glass of water leaning in the fountain. The pipes ticking at the change of shift. Light bending the way light bends when a floor is not flat but curved, and the curve is doing you the favour of holding you down.

Kirana found me at the cold-well corridor at 08:20 with two bowls of rice and the expression of a nineteen-year-old who has rehearsed.

“I want to say something,” she said.

“Say it.”

“You’re going to tell me it’s not my business.”

“It is not your business.”

“It’s my station,” Kirana said. “I was born on it. My mother was born on it. I’ve got a tally of every core I’ve ever touched and it’s seventeen and one of them is the one you broke the seal on, and if the Bureau takes the station apart I’ll be an apprentice with no master on a dock somewhere in the belt, so don’t tell me it’s not my business.”

I took the rice.

“All right,” I said. “It’s your business.”

She stared at me.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“You’re supposed to argue.”

“I have been awake for two days and I have run out of arguments. It’s your business. You were right about the manifest, you were right about the registry port, and you were right about the nine days. I would like to be wrong about you at least once, so that I can go back to treating you like an apprentice.”

Kirana ate her rice with the ferocious concentration of a person who does not want anybody to see her face.

---

The dead room is at the end of the cold-well corridor, and it is not called the dead room because of the dead. It is called the dead room because the air in it is dead: sealed, filtered, at four degrees, with no circulation, because a room where you weigh things cannot have a draught.

The core was in the ash locker. The ash locker is a cabinet the size of a wardrobe with a lead-glass door and a seal of pressed ash around the frame, and the ash is there because the locker is also the station’s evidence store and evidence in a relay case has to be sealed in a way that shows if anybody has opened it. You press the ash with a stamp, and the stamp leaves a pattern, and the pattern is photographed and logged, and if anybody opens the door the ash cracks.

I have opened an ash locker eleven times in nine years. Every time, I have done the same thing first: taken the calibration slug out of the top tray and weighed it. You check the slug before you trust the scale. You check the scale before you trust the ash. You check the ash before you put your hand inside a cabinet that holds the only evidence of a crime.

I put my hand into the top tray.

The tray was empty.

---

There is a particular kind of cold that starts in the wrist.

The slug is forty grams of machined tungsten with a serial number worn to a shadow. It had been in that tray since the academy. I had taken it out on the night of the ninth of October and put it in my sleeve, and it had been in my sleeve or in my quarters or in my hand every day since, because a calibration slug is a totem: you carry it, you rub the serial number with your thumb, you put it on a scale and hear the number come back the same, and while the number comes back the same the world is a place where a scale means something.

I had taken it out of the kit on the night I broke the seal. I had not put it back. Now the tray was empty and there was nobody in the station who knew that except me and Vey and the camera above rack four.

“Kirana,” I said. “Stay in the corridor.”

“Why?”

“Because I am about to open an ash locker and I want a witness who is not a suspect.”

“That’s a terrible reason.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

---

The ash was intact. I want to be exact about that, because in the days afterwards a great deal was said about the ash. The seal was pressed, the pattern matched the photograph on the locker door, the frame was clean, and the lead-glass door was shut.

The door was shut. The locker was open.

I do not know how to describe the difference to somebody who has not spent nine years in a dead room. The door is the lead-glass panel. The locker is the cabinet behind it. A sealed locker has its interior drawer latched to the frame, and the latch is mechanical, and the latch is inside the ash.

The drawer was out.

Not far. Four centimetres. Just enough to see that the padding inside was flat, and that the shape of the core — the shape that had been pressed into the foam in October and never smoothed out — was empty, and that there was a fine grey dust on the lip of the drawer where the ash had been disturbed from the inside.

I put my hand on the latch, which is the only part of an ash locker you are allowed to touch without breaking the seal, and I did the thing I do, which I have never been able to explain to anybody and have stopped trying.

I tasted it.

Hot dust. Ozone. The sweet thing. And under that, faint and wrong, like a note played on the wrong string: cold metal, and a hand that was not mine, and a smell I could not place — clean, chemical, the smell of a thing that has been washed in something that is not water.

I took my hand off the latch.

“Kirana,” I said, in a voice I did not recognise as mine. “Go and find my father. Don’t run. Walk, and don’t tell anybody what you’re doing, and if anybody asks you say the wells need a signature.”

She looked at my face and did not ask a single question, which is the moment I decided that whatever happened, that girl was going to have a license.

---

Danu came at nine. He came alone, without the seal, without the slate, and he stood in the doorway of the dead room looking at the open drawer for a long time without saying anything.

“Don’t touch anything else,” he said.

“I know.”

“Don’t touch the drawer. Don’t touch the latch. Don’t touch the ash.”

“I know what an ash locker is. I’ve been opening them for nine years.”

“Not this one.”

He came in and crouched by the cabinet, and he did not touch it either, and the two of us stood in a room at four degrees looking at four centimetres of open drawer.

“Where is the slug?” he said.

“What?”

“The mass kit. The top tray is empty. Where is the slug?”

There it was. Six minutes, that was all it took. Six minutes from a father walking into a room to a father asking the only question that mattered.

“I have it,” I said.

He closed his eyes.

“On the ninth of October,” I said. “The night I broke the seal. I took it to weigh the core and I put it in my sleeve and I never put it back.”

“Do you have it now?”

“Yes.”

“In your quarters?”

“In my sleeve.”

Danu opened his eyes and looked at me.

“Then put it back in the tray,” he said. “Now. Before anybody else comes in this room.”

“I didn’t take the core.”

“I know you didn’t take the core.”

“Then why are you talking to me like a suspect?”

“Because in four hours somebody is going to talk to you like one,” Danu said, “and they are going to do it in a room with a camera, and they are going to ask you where the slug was on the night of the ninth of October, and you are going to tell the truth, and the truth is going to sound like a confession.”

I took the slug out of my sleeve. It was cold and it was forty grams and the serial number was worn to a shadow, and I put it in the tray of the mass kit where it had lived since the academy, and it sat there.

“Why?” I said. “Why does it matter now?”

“Because the first thing the Bureau does with a stolen core,” my father said, “is weigh the evidence room. And the man who took the core will have made sure the evidence room does not weigh right.”

I stood alone in the dead room with the empty tray and the open drawer and the ash on the lip.

Down the corridor, the station was having an ordinary morning. The queue at the showers had shortened. The mess had run out of eggs. Somebody was hammering at the broken door of the school ring. Toha’s girl was barefoot on a cold deck, and the water in the fountain was leaning against the rim of a glass, and four hundred metres away four thousand one hundred packets sat in a cold room that was not this one and did not know yet that the station had begun to eat itself.

The ash locker was open. The core was gone.

## Chapter Thirty — Who Are You

I did not raise the alarm. That is the first thing I have to explain, and I have explained it since to three people and a magistrate, and none of them believed me, so I will put it down here in the plainest words available.

I did not raise the alarm because the dead room smelled of something that had been washed, and because I have spent my whole life in a place where the truth is a thing you find by following a smell.

The clean chemical smell was still in the corridor. It was faint, and it was wrong, and it went from the dead room along the cold-well corridor and then turned left into the service crawl, which is not a corridor. The crawl is the space between the ring’s inner shell and the conduit tray. It is ninety centimetres high. The water main runs along the left side and the power tray along the right, and the only reason a person goes into it is that something has gone wrong with the water main or the power tray.

“Vey,” I said, into the wall speaker. “Log nothing.”

“I log everything,” Vey said.

“Log it after.”

“That is not the same thing, and you know it is not the same thing, and I would like it noted that you asked.”

“Noted.”

“What are you going to do?”

“Follow a smell.”

“What does it smell of?”

“A solvent. Something clean. Not water, not ethanol, not the wash they use on the trays.”

“Then it is not a solvent on the station inventory,” Vey said. “The station inventory has eleven solvents and I can name all of them. What you are describing is a solvent that came here in a container.”

“Yes,” I said. “That is what I am describing.”

“There is a procedure for a suspected intrusion,” Vey said. “The procedure has four steps and the second step is to notify the administrator.”

“The administrator is my father.”

“The procedure does not have an exemption for that.”

“Then the procedure is going to have a bad night.” I got the torch off the hook. “If I am not back in twenty minutes, tell him.”

“Twenty minutes is not a number you have any authority to set.”

“Twenty minutes, Vey.”

A pause. Then: “Nineteen,” Vey said. “I am counting from now.”

I got a torch and a spanner and I went in.

---

The crawl is quiet in a way that the rest of the station is not. In the crawl you can hear the ring itself: a low note, felt in the teeth more than heard, the sound of a kilometre and a quarter of steel being held against the sky by its own turning.

I went forty metres on my hands and knees, following a smell.

That is what I want on the record. Not a hunch, not a machine, not a woman’s intuition. A smell: clean, chemical, the smell of something washed in a solvent that is not water, and under it, faint, the taste of cold metal and a hand that was not mine.

I know what that sounds like. I have never been able to make it sound like anything else.

---

The crawl opens into the junction above the cold wells, and the junction has a hatch, and the hatch was open, and there was light coming up through it.

Not torchlight. The cold-well room has its own emergency strip, and the strip was on, and somebody had left the hatch open and gone down, and the light came up through the opening the colour of a warning.

I lay in the crawl with my cheek on the conduit tray and listened.

Nothing. The ring note. The water main ticking as it cooled. And then, very faint, the sound of a case latch being closed.

I have heard that sound eleven thousand times. It is the sound my life is made of: a core case, closed, in a cold room, by somebody who is being careful.

I went down the ladder.

---

The cold-well room at four degrees with the strip light on is the colour of a bruise. The wells are four steel cylinders in a row, each one the size of a coffin, each one with a lead-glass port and a brass handle, and behind the ports the packets sit in their cradles in the dark.

There was a woman at the third well.

She had her back to me. She was wearing a maintenance jacket that was too big for her, and grey work trousers, and her hair was pinned up with the one pin that never holds, and she had a case on the floor beside her, open, with foam inside it, and the foam had a shape in it.

I said, “Put it down.”

She did not turn around.

“I am the relay engineer of this station,” I said, and my voice came out level, which I have never understood, “and there are four thousand one hundred packets in this room, and if you open that well I will put this spanner through your hand.”

She put her hand flat on the well’s brass handle.

And then she stopped. Not a flinch. Not a freeze. She stopped the way a person stops when they have heard a voice they recognise and cannot immediately place.

I have seen that stop before. I have seen it on a screen, in an archive, at a quarter speed, in a room against the outer shell at fourteen degrees.

“Turn around,” I said.

She turned around.

---

I want to describe the second that followed, because it is the one I have run back most often, and because in every version I am still standing there with a spanner in my hand and my mouth open.

Time did not stop. It thickened.

The strip light buzzed at a frequency I had never noticed before, and the sound came to me in pieces, the way sound comes when you are underwater. The water main ticked. A drop of condensation came off the pipe above the third well and hung there, and did not fall, and the light in it bent into a small bright bead, and inside that second I had all the time in the world to see her face and to refuse it.

My mother’s face.

Not a photograph of my mother’s face. Not the file, not the frame, not the woman in the archive with her hand on a child’s head. Her face, older by twenty-two years, with the lines that come from not sleeping and the grey at the temple that comes from a life lived indoors under lamps, and the small scar on the left thumb, and the way she held her chin a little to one side as though listening for something behind her.

“You’re dead,” I said.

She said nothing.

“You are in a wall on the Farside. Tag 4471. I have read the register with my own eyes, in an archive at fourteen degrees, and I have watched the frame where they put you in it.”

Her mouth moved. Nothing came out. She tried again, and this time there was a sound, thin and dry and wrong, the sound of a voice that has not been used in a very long time.

“Not me,” she said.

“What?”

“Not me,” she said again, and put her hand back on the brass handle, and looked at the well, and not at me.

“Who is in the wall, then?”

She looked at the well for a long moment. Then she said, “Halim.”

I stood in a room at four degrees with a spanner in my hand and my mother’s voice in the air, and I could not make the two things be in the same room.

“Halim is a dock technician,” I said. “Halim identified the bodies. Halim is dead.”

“Yes,” she said. “He asked to be.”

“You are in a wall on the Farside. I have the tag. I have the register. Tag 4471, interment 2161.09.22, next of kin Prasetya, D.”

She looked at me. Her eyes were the colour of weak tea, and there was nothing in them I could read, and I have spent my life reading faces.

“Say something,” I said.

She opened her mouth and closed it again, and her hand came off the brass handle, and she raised it — toward me, or toward the well, or toward the space between us, I have never been able to decide.

---

There is a pressure door between the cold-well room and the ladder bay. It is not a hatch. It is a proper door, half a metre of steel with a wheel on it, and it is there because a well that loses pressure takes the room with it. The room takes the ladder bay. The ladder bay takes the crawl.

I do not remember crossing to it. I remember my hand on the wheel, and the wheel turning, and the door swinging shut between us on its counterweight with the slow terrible patience of a thing that weighs four hundred kilograms.

She did not try to stop me. That is the part I cannot get past. She stood in the cold-well room with her hand half raised and watched me shut a pressure door between my mother and myself, and she did not take one step toward it.

Through the port in the door, she was a shape in a bruise-coloured room.

I put my palm flat on the glass. She put her palm flat on the other side of it. Twenty-two years of nothing, and nine centimetres of steel, and two hands on either side of a port in a pressure door, and neither one of them reaching.

“Who are you?” I said.

She said something. The glass is forty millimetres thick and the ring note was in my teeth and there was no way on this station for a voice to cross it, and yet I heard her, the way you hear a person in a room you have just left.

“Say it again,” I said. “Say it into the port.”

She leaned close to the glass and said it again, and this time I got the shape of it, and the shape of it was not a name.

“You look like him,” she said. “When you are angry, you look like him.”

The glass is forty millimetres. She could not have heard me through forty millimetres of steel and a room at four degrees, and I did not care, and I said it again, louder, with my palm flat on the port and my forehead against the frame.

“Who are you?”

She said something. I saw her lips move, three words, and the strip light was behind her and her face was in shadow, and I have run those three words a hundred times and I cannot make them into anything but a name.

Then she turned away, and picked up the case, and went out through the service door at the back of the cold-well room, and the door closed behind her, and I stood at a port in a pressure door with my hand on the glass and the ring note in my teeth.

I opened the door eleven seconds later.

I want that on the record too. Eleven seconds. I did not stand there for a minute. I did not weigh the decision. I got the wheel around in eleven seconds and went through with the spanner in my hand. The emergency strip buzzed. The back door was open. The crawl beyond it was empty, and the only thing in it was a smell of clean chemical solvent and cold metal.

I put my back against the well and got the wall speaker off its clip.

“Administration. Prasetya.”

“Cold wells,” I said. “Third well junction. Somebody has been in here.”

A pause. Paper moving.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Is the well intact?”

“Yes.”

“Then say it slowly,” my father said, “and start with the part you do not want to say.”

I looked at the port in the pressure door and the smudge on it where a hand had been.

“There was a woman in the cold-well room,” I said. “She had a case with foam in it. She had her hand on the brass handle of the third well, and she took it off when she heard my voice, and she turned around, and she had my mother’s face.”

The speaker was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I checked the clip.

“Danu.”

“Describe her,” he said. His voice had gone somewhere I had never heard it go.

“Older. Grey at the temple. The scar on the left thumb.” I stopped. “Sekar Adisurya. That is what I am telling you. I saw Sekar Adisurya in the cold-well room, twenty-two years after we put a tag in a wall, and she had a core case and she did not take one packet.”

The speaker was quiet.

“Danu. Say something.”

“Did she speak?” he said.

“Three words. Through a pressure door. I could not hear them.”

“Then we will have to ask her,” my father said, and the speaker clicked off, and I stood in a room at four degrees with my hand on a well and the whole shape of the last twenty-two years rearranging itself around me like a floor that has decided to be a wall.

Behind me, the third well was intact. I checked it with my hands before I checked it with my eyes, and the packets in it were in their cradles, and the brass handle was cold, and the well had not been opened.

She had not come for the packets.

She had come for the case, and the case had foam in it, and I had seen the shape in the foam.

It was the shape of a core.

# Arc IV · The Evacuation

## Chapter Thirty-One — The Ash Locker

The station went into lockdown at 14:00 on the fourteenth of November, and I want to say at the start that lockdown on a ring is not what it is on a ship.

On a ship, lockdown means doors. On Kemuning, lockdown means that four thousand people are told, in the flat voice of a machine that has been given the order, that the ring is closed for a maintenance inspection, that all shifts are suspended, that the water will be off between 16:00 and 18:00, and that nobody is to enter the service crawl. It means the docks are sealed with a pressure barrier and a guard who has been given a written instruction. It means the schools keep going, because a school is a place where four hundred children can be counted, and counting is what lockdown is actually for.

It does not mean that anybody stops. It means that everybody is where the register says they are.

---

We met in the administration office at 14:20: my father, Rasya, Kirana because she refused to leave, and me.

The table had on it four things. I want to describe them in the order they were laid down, because the order is the briefing.

Danu put the ash locker’s seal photograph on the table first. Then the mass kit, closed. Then a case — the case that Sekar had left in the cold-well room, empty, its foam cut to the shape of a core. Then he put down a sheet of paper with eleven names on it, and the eleven names were the eleven people who had been issued keys to the dead room since 2171.

“Eleven keys,” he said. “Four returned on retirement. Two are dead. One is a magistrate in Surabaya and has not been on this station since 2178. Three are in this room. One is Vey’s maintenance override, which cannot be keyed without a second signature, and the second signature is mine.”

I looked at the eleven names. My own was on the list. So was Kirana’s, in the hand of a nineteen-year-old apprentice who had signed for it in August, and Danu’s, in his small forward-leaning script, and the fourth, at the bottom, in a hand I did not recognise because the ink had faded.

“Who is that?” I said.

“Halim,” my father said.

The room was quiet.

“Halim has been dead for twenty-two years.”

“Halim’s key was returned on the twenty-fourth of September, 2161,” Danu said. “The return is logged. The key is in the drawer. I have looked.”

“Then it is a name on a sheet of paper.”

“It is a name on a sheet of paper,” he said, “and a name on a sheet of paper is the beginning of every audit I have ever conducted.”

---

Here is what the briefing said, in the order it was said.

The ash locker’s seal was intact. The seal photograph, taken at 06:00 on the fourteenth, matched the pattern pressed on the twelfth of October. Nobody had opened the locker from outside.

The dead room’s door log recorded one entry between 22:00 on the thirteenth and 08:00 on the fourteenth.

The entry was keyed with my credential.

“Is that true?” Danu said.

“I was asleep,” I said.

“That is not what I asked.”

“Then ask what you mean.”

“I mean,” my father said, “that the door log records a credential and a time, and it does not record a face. It records that a key was used. Was your key used?”

I put my hand flat on the table.

“I do not know,” I said.

---

Rasya picked up the ash photograph and held it to the light.

“There is a second explanation,” he said. “The locker was opened from inside.”

“From inside?” Kirana said.

“The drawer is out four centimetres. The ash on the lip is disturbed from the inside. The seal on the outside is intact.” He put the photograph down. “The door log records the room. It does not record the cabinet. So the question is not who opened the room. It is how the cabinet was opened without breaking the seal.”

“There is only one way,” I said. “The cabinet has a maintenance port in the back, and the maintenance port opens onto the cold-well junction, and the well junction has a crawl.”

“Then the thief came through the crawl.”

“Then the thief came through the crawl,” I said, “which is where I found her.”

Danu looked at me.

“You found her,” he said. “You did not report her.”

“I reported her at 09:06.”

“You found her at 08:40.”

“Then I did not report her for twenty-six minutes,” I said. “I spent twenty-six minutes in a service crawl following a smell because I did not want to be the woman who raised an alarm about her own mother, and if you would like that in the log, Vey can put it in.”

The room was very quiet. Kirana was looking at the table. Rasya had gone still in the way he goes still, which is the way of a man who is deciding whether to be an auditor or a person.

“I would like it in the log,” my father said.

---

The interview room is on the administration level, and it has a table with a ring on the underside for restraints, and a camera above the door, and no window.

Sekar Adisurya sat on the far side of the table with her hands folded and her chin a little to one side and her hair pinned up with the one pin that never holds.

She had been searched. She had been given a station jacket because hers had solvent on it. She had been given water, and had not drunk it. She had been read her rights under annex nine by Vey in the flat voice Vey uses for weather and deaths, and she had listened to the whole of it, and at the end she had said, “I know my rights. I wrote half of them.”

Rasya did the interview. I watched from the observation side, through the glass, with my palm flat on the bench.

“Your name,” he said.

“Sekar Adisurya.”

“Date of birth?”

“Third of June, 2122.”

“Place of birth?”

“Tsiolkovsky Station, Ring C.”

“Status?”

“Deceased,” she said, “according to you.”

Rasya wrote it down.

“You were found in the cold-well room of Kemuning Station at 08:40 on the fourteenth of November, with a core case in your possession. The core it was cut for is missing from the evidence locker in the dead room. Do you understand the charge?”

“Yes.”

“Do you wish to make a statement?”

“Yes.”

The room was quiet. I put both hands flat on the bench.

“I wish to make a statement,” Sekar said, “which is that I will not answer a single question until the evacuation is under way. Not one. Not about the core, not about the crawl, not about the case, not about the wall. There are four thousand people on this ring and five days left, and I have spent twenty-two years learning what happens to a station when the Bureau decides to look at it, and I am not going to spend the last five days being looked at.”

Rasya put his stylus down.

“That is not a statement,” he said. “That is a refusal.”

“It is a refusal,” she said. “You can put it in the log.”

The weighing was at 17:00.

I did it myself, because I am the relay engineer, and because a scale is only worth the woman who checks it. I took the calibration slug out of the top tray of the mass kit and put it on the brass pan, and I did the thing I have done ten thousand times: my thumb went to the serial number on the base, worn to a shadow, and rubbed it once.

Forty grams. The scale said forty grams, and I put the slug back in the tray, and then I weighed the ash locker.

There are two ways to weigh an ash locker. You weigh the cabinet, and you weigh the evidence, and the difference is the tare. The tare of that cabinet has been 118.400 kilograms since 2171, and it is painted on the frame in three generations of hand, and every engineer who has ever certified a seal on this station has written that number at the top of a sheet.

The cabinet weighed 118.404.

Four grams.

“Kirana,” I said. “Read me the tare.”

“One hundred and eighteen point four.”

“Read it again.”

“One hundred and eighteen point four.” She looked up. “The scale says one eighteen point four oh four.”

“Which means what?”

“Which means the cabinet is four grams heavier than it was when it was certified.”

“Which means what?”

Kirana looked at the cabinet, and then at me, and her face did the thing that faces do when a nineteen-year-old arrives at the same conclusion as an adult and does not want to be the one to say it.

“Which means there is four grams of something in it that is not supposed to be there,” she said. “Or four grams of something missing that used to weigh four grams more.”

“Say the second one.”

“Someone put something in that weighed four grams less than what they took out,” Kirana said.

I put my hand on the cabinet.

“No,” I said. “Someone put something in that weighs exactly what the *core* weighs. Four grams is the tolerance. The core is four grams plus or minus nothing, because a certified core is weighed at the factory and the weight is etched on the base.”

“Then the cabinet would weigh the same.”

“The cabinet would weigh the same,” I said, “if the thing inside it had the same weight as the core. It does not. It weighs four grams more. Which means there are two things in there now, or one thing that has been modified, or —”

I stopped.

“Or what?” Kirana said.

“Or the thief has been back,” I said, “and put something in, and taken nothing out, and the four grams is the weight of a message.”

---

We opened the locker at 17:20 by breaking the seal under camera, with my father reading the number out loud and Rasya recording it on the field unit and Vey logging the time.

The drawer came out four centimetres and then came out all the way, and there was nothing in it.

Not empty in the way a thing is empty when it has been taken. Empty in the way a room is empty when somebody has cleaned it. The foam was flat. The shape of the core, the shape that had been pressed into the foam in October and never smoothed out, was smooth. Somebody had pressed the foam flat with a tool, and then dusted the dust back over it, and the dust was the ash from the lip and the dust was the wrong colour.

And in the bottom of the drawer, under the foam, where the certified weight of the core is stamped into the steel in the factory and never touched again, there was a new stamp.

It was four grams.

It was the weight of a thing that had been put there so that a scale would read true, by somebody who understood that a scale is checked before it is trusted, and that the person who checks it will be the person who is blamed.

The door log had one entry, and it was my key, and I had been asleep.

## Chapter Thirty-Two — Six Days

There were three ships and eleven berths and four thousand and eleven people, and the arithmetic did not work, and I sat in the administration office on the fifteenth of November and watched my father do it for the fourth time.

“Rendang,” Danu said. “Forty. She is a supply hull with forty seats and no recycler worth the name, and she can make the inner routes in nine days if she does not stop.”

“Nine days is too long,” I said.

“Nine days is what she has. Cendana has already gone with a hundred and twenty and nine weeks of consumables, and she is not coming back, because I sold the return leg to pay for her outbound fuel.”

“You sold —”

“I sold the return leg,” my father said, “and I would do it again, and it is in the ledger, and you may be angry with me after the numbers.”

He turned the sheet around.

The third ship was not a ship. The third ship was a cargo hull called the *Bukit Tiga* that had been laid up at the outer dock for eleven years with a cracked frame and eight hundred bunks bolted into her hold by a dock crew who had been paid in water. She had a recycler that had been installed at night by a man named Toha, who is not an engineer, and whose daughter is eleven and goes barefoot on a cold deck.

“Eight hundred,” I said.

“Eight hundred.”

“Plus forty. Plus a hundred and twenty.”

“Nine hundred and sixty,” Danu said, “if everything works, and if the *Bukit Tiga* holds pressure, and if the recycler does not kill anybody, and if the Bureau does not impound her for being an unregistered passenger hull, which she is.”

“And the other three thousand?”

“The other three thousand stay in the ring,” my father said, “and the ring is dechartered on the seventeenth, and a dechartered installation has no tariff, no relay license, and no legal existence. It becomes a structure. People can live in a structure for a while. Eleven days, if the wells hold. Longer if the Bureau is slow.”

“And if the Bureau is not slow?”

“Then the Bureau sends a tender to cut the relay cluster out of the ring,” Danu said, “and a tender needs a berth, and there are eleven berths, and I have been counting berths for six days.”

---

This is the part where Vey was useful, and I want to say so, because in the days afterwards there were people on that station who said the machine had gone wrong.

“Vey,” I said. “How many people can the ring evacuate in six days, at the current dock throughput?”

“Two hundred and eleven,” Vey said.

“That is nothing.”

“That is the berth arithmetic,” Vey said. “Eleven berths, one shift per berth, four hours per shift, six days. A berth can take a hundred kilos of luggage per passenger or a pressure cot, not both. At the current manifest discipline the ring moves eleven hundred people a day through the docks. At evacuation discipline, with children and the hospital, it moves two hundred and eleven people in six days, because the hospital alone requires forty berths of its own.”

“Then we change the discipline.”

“Then you will need a plan,” Vey said, “and a plan is a document, and a document is something I can execute.”

---

We built the plan on the table with paper and a ruler, the way you build a plan when the computers are all busy being honest.

Danu drew the docks. Eleven berths around the outer ring, numbered from the north lock clockwise, and each berth with a pressure barrier and a cradle and a hose. He drew them to scale, and the scale was one centimetre to ten metres, and the ring came out the size of a dinner plate.

I did the medical list with Kirana. There were ninety-one names on it by the end of the first hour: people who could not be moved without a pressure cot, people who could not be moved at all, people whose names were on a list because a doctor in 2179 had written *do not transport* in a box and nobody had ever taken it off.

Ninety-one names on a ring of four thousand is not a large number. It is two per cent. You can hold two per cent in your head, and that is the danger of it: a number small enough to hold is a number small enough to stop feeling.

Kirana read them out. I wrote them down. At the forty-first name she stopped.

“Adisurya,” she said.

“That’s me.”

“No,” Kirana said. “It says *S. Adisurya*. And under it, in the box, it says *remains*.”

---

Here is what the hospital register said, and I have the sheet, and I have read it eleven times.

The register is not a list of patients. It is a list of *bodies the station is responsible for moving in an evacuation*, and it is compiled from the death register, because a dead person is a body and a body needs a berth, and the Bureau has never in its history permitted a body to be left behind on a dechartered installation.

Ninety-one names. Forty-one of them living, with a pressure cot and a doctor’s note. Fifty of them dead.

And one of the dead was S. Adisurya, interred at Tsiolkovsky, Farside wall, niche 4471, remains transferable — a body in a wall four hundred million kilometres away that the station was legally responsible for moving, because a death certificate is a fact and a fact has a berth.

“She’s not in the wall,” I said.

“The register does not know that,” Danu said.

“She’s in the interview room. She’s been in the interview room for a day and a half, refusing to answer questions until the evacuation is under way, and the register has her in a wall on the Farside and a berth on a ship.”

“Then the register is wrong.”

“The register is never wrong,” I said. “That is the thing about the register. It is not a record of what happened. It is a record of what was filed, and what was filed is that Sekar Adisurya is dead and her remains are transferable, and if she gets on a ship under her own name she will be getting on as a corpse.”

Danu was quiet for a moment.

“Then she does not get on under her own name,” he said.

---

This is where I have to put the two clocks, because the next four days were lived in two of them at once and I have never been able to tell the story any other way.

The first clock was the station’s. It ran on shifts and berths and the cold wells, and its numbers were: six days to dechartering, twenty-nine hours of reserve on the wells, eleven berths, two hundred and eleven people, ninety-one names.

The second clock was the Bureau’s. It ran on filings and notices, and its numbers were: eleven days from the refusal to the review, five days from the review to the evacuation, and a tender — a hull with a cutting crew and a legal writ — that had left the inner routes on the fourteenth and would be at Kemuning on the twenty-first, four days after the evacuation was supposed to be finished.

Four days.

I did the arithmetic on the back of the medical list, in pencil, at 23:40 on the fifteenth of November, while Kirana slept sitting up in a chair with her tally slate on her knees.

If the evacuation started on the seventeenth and ran at two hundred and eleven people per six days, the station would be empty of evacuees in the year 2195.

If it ran at eleven hundred people a day, which is what the docks could do with the discipline of a working station, it would take four days. Four days is exactly the window between the evacuation and the tender.

I looked at that number for a long time.

And meanwhile, four hundred million kilometres away, in an office on the inner routes that I have never seen and will never see, a man or a woman I will never meet was signing a filing that would arrive on the twenty-first with a cutting crew, and the filing would be correct, and the law would be on its side, and the eleven berths would be full.

“Vey,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Can the docks do eleven hundred a day?”

“The docks can do eleven hundred a day,” Vey said. “The station cannot. Eleven hundred a day requires every dock crew on shift, every berth hot, the hospital on a priority manifest, and somebody to sign for four thousand people, one at a time, with a witness.”

“Who signs?”

“The administrator signs for the installation.”

“And who signs for the people?”

“The people sign for themselves,” Vey said. “That is what a manifest is. It is four thousand signatures, in a queue, at a dock, in the dark, with a case in the other hand.”

---

I put the pencil down.

“Then we need four thousand signatures in four days,” I said.

“Four thousand and eleven.”

“Four thousand and eleven signatures.”

“And a plan for the ninety-one,” Vey said, “and a berth for a body that is not in a wall, and a way to keep the Bureau’s tender out of the eleven berths until the last ship has undocked.”

The list of who could not be moved was ninety-one names long, and my mother’s was on it.

## Chapter Thirty-Three — The Second Name

The observation glass in the interview room is one-way, forty millimetres thick, and set into a wall that was built in 2131 by people who believed that an interview should be watched by somebody who is not in the room.

I stood on the dark side of it with my palm flat on the bench and watched Rasya Wardhana put a sheet of paper on the table.

“This is the casualty schedule,” he said. “Tsiolkovsky, September 2161. Nine names. Two removed. I want to talk about one of them.”

Sekar Adisurya looked at the paper and did not touch it.

“You said you would not answer a question until the evacuation is under way,” Rasya said.

“I did.”

“The first shift of the evacuation undocks at 06:00 tomorrow. Eleven berths, eleven hundred people. That is under way.”

Sekar considered this.

“One question,” she said.

“One question.”

“And if I answer it, you will not ask another until the second shift.”

“I will not ask another until the second shift,” Rasya said.

She put her hand on the paper.

“Ask.”

---

“Ayu Wardhana,” Rasya said. “My sister. Eleven years old. She died in Ring C on the eleventh of September, 2161. I have her in a photograph on a shelf in a flat I no longer have. I have her name in a file. I have a grave I have never visited properly.”

He stopped. He did not look at the glass. He has never once looked at the glass.

“The register has her as Ayu Wardhana,” he said. “But there is a second name in the dependant file. Two letters and a number, filed under annex nine, in the same hand as the seal on this station’s file 44-1190. I have been trying to open it for nineteen years. I want to know what the second name is.”

Sekar looked at him for a long time.

“Ayu Halim Wardhana,” she said.

---

I watched Rasya’s face do something I had never seen it do.

He is a man who has spent nineteen years arranging his face the way other men arrange a desk. He had it arranged now, and the arrangement held for perhaps two seconds, and then it came apart at the mouth.

“Halim,” he said.

“Halim,” Sekar said.

“The dock technician.”

“The dock technician.”

“He identified her body.”

“He did.”

“He ran nine sessions on a seven-year-old.”

“He did that too.”

Rasya put both hands flat on the table, and I watched him do it through forty millimetres of glass, and I could not reach him, and there was no door in that wall because a wall with a door is not a wall, it is a corridor, and this was a wall.

“Say it plainly,” he said.

“Your mother was married to a man called Wardhana and she had a daughter,” Sekar said. “The daughter’s father was Halim. The register never recorded it, because the register records the marriage, not the child. The only place the second name exists is in the dependant file that your sister’s death created, and the man who created it wrote the second name in because he was the one who had to sign for her remains.”

“He signed for her remains.”

“He signed for her remains.”

“And then he went into a wall on the Farside.”

“That was later,” Sekar said. “And that was his idea.”

I want to put down what Rasya did next, because it is the reason I trust him, and because it is the reason I have never been able to decide whether I love him or simply recognise him.

He did not ask why nobody had told him. He did not ask about the money or the file or the nineteen years. He asked:

“Was she in the room?”

Sekar’s chin came up a little to one side, the way it does when she is listening for something behind her.

“What room?”

“The relay room. The night of the eleventh. You were in the relay room at seven in the morning on the twentieth, with a child and a form. There are four hundred and eleven hours of camera on that spool. I have watched eleven of them.” He leaned forward. “Was my sister in that room?”

Sekar looked at him for a long moment. And then she said the sentence that changed the shape of everything, and she said it in the flat voice of a woman who has carried it for twenty-two years and has decided, on the third shift of an evacuation, to put it down.

“Ayu Halim Wardhana,” she said. “She was in the room with you.”

---

The glass was cold. I noticed that at some point, because my hand had been on it long enough to have made a print, and the print had begun to run, and I took my hand off and left a smudge.

Rasya’s voice came through the speaker in the ceiling, thin and very clear.

“I was not in a relay room,” he said. “I was in a room with a counsellor. I have the file. I have the summary. I have the eleven weeks the Bureau took to close it.”

“You were in a relay room,” Sekar said. “You were twelve. You were sitting on the bench with your sister and a girl of seven who had a bandage on her arm, and you were holding a core the size of a lunchbox because somebody told you to hold it, and you were told not to put it down.”

Rasya was very still.

“I do not remember that,” he said.

“No,” Sekar said. “You paid to not remember it. Or somebody paid for you. I have never been able to find out which.”

There is a thing that happens when you watch a man discover that his memory has been edited. It is not a performance. It is much quieter than a performance: he sits, and then he puts one hand on the edge of the table, and then he says a sentence that has nothing to do with anything.

“I do not remember her laughing,” Rasya said.

“What?”

“I have a photograph of her. I remember the photograph. I remember that she had two plaits and that she would not sit still for the camera. I do not remember her laughing. I have thought about it for nineteen years and I have decided that it is because she was eleven and I was twelve and brothers do not remember their sisters laughing, they remember them being annoying.” He looked up. “I have been wrong about myself for nineteen years.”

Sekar did not say anything.

“You said one question,” Rasya said. “That was one question. I am asking another.”

“Ask.”

“Who paid?”

“Before I answer that,” Sekar said, “I want to ask you one.”

“You do not get to ask me one.”

“I am asking anyway. What did you do with the core?”

Rasya’s mouth opened and closed.

“I do not know,” he said.

“You held it,” Sekar said. “You were told not to put it down, and you did not put it down, and you held it until the twentieth of September.”

“For nine days?”

“For nine days.”

“I was twelve.”

“You were twelve,” Sekar said. “That is why they chose you.”

---

I could not hear what she said next.

I want to be honest about that, because the observation speaker is old, and the wall is thick, and there is a moment in the recording — I have the recording, Vey keeps everything, I have listened to it eleven times — where the fan in the ceiling comes on and takes four seconds of her answer with it.

Four seconds. Her mouth moved, and her hand came up, and her finger pointed at the paper on the table, and then she took her hand back and folded it with the other one, and the fan went off, and her voice came back in the middle of a sentence:

“— and that is why I put the suit back.”

Rasya looked at her.

“What suit?”

Sekar said nothing.

“What suit?”

“That is the second question,” Sekar said. “Second shift undocks at 10:00. Ask me then.”

---

Rasya came out of the interview room at 23:20 with the casualty schedule in his hand and his face arranged again, badly, in the way a man arranges a face when he has been up for two days.

I was standing in the corridor with my back against the wall and my palm still cold.

“You heard,” he said.

“I heard.”

“The fan took four seconds.”

“The fan took four seconds.”

He nodded, and looked at the paper in his hand, and then he did something I did not expect: he put his hand flat on the glass of the observation window, on the dark side, where nobody was, on the wall of an empty room.

“She was in the room with me,” he said.

“She was in the room with you.”

“And I was holding a core.”

“You were holding a core.”

He stood there with his hand on the glass for a long moment.

“I have spent nineteen years,” he said, “being the man who was not there.”

Behind the glass, the interview room was empty and the table had a sheet of paper on it and the light above the table was on, because the light above the table is never turned off.

## Chapter Thirty-Four — The Terminal Sale

My father’s office at four in the morning on the sixteenth of November was the emptiest I had ever seen it, because he was packing.

Not moving out. Packing the *record*: the station’s paper, eleven years of licences, the seal registers, the manifests, the maintenance logs, the medical list, the casualty schedule, everything that a dechartered installation is required to surrender to the Bureau and everything it is not, folded into crates with the crates numbered in his small forward-leaning hand.

“You are packing,” I said.

“I am packing.”

“There are two days.”

“There are two days, and on the seventeenth this station stops existing as a legal person, and a legal person’s papers belong to its successor, and its successor is the Bureau, and the Bureau’s archive is a room in an office on the inner routes that has never been opened to a magistrate.” He put a crate down. “So I am packing what I intend to keep.”

I sat on the corner of his desk, which I have not done since I was nine.

“What do you intend to keep?”

“The casualty schedule,” he said. “The seal register. The dependant file 44-1190. And one sheet of paper with four thousand one hundred numbers on it, which I have been keeping in a drawer since 2171 and which nobody has ever asked me about.”

“What is it?”

“It is an inventory,” Danu said. “Would you like to see what it cost me to keep it?”

---

Here is what he told me, and I am going to put it down in the order he said it, because the order is the truth and the truth has been rearranged enough.

Tsiolkovsky Station was decommissioned in 2168. The observatory had been losing its funding since 2163, the ring had a structural rating that would not survive a second review, and the Bureau’s decision was not a tragedy, it was an arithmetic: eleven thousand people, a failing ring, a relay that had been superseded by the Farside array.

The station was closed over nine months. The people were moved. The equipment was inventoried, and what the Bureau wanted was taken, and what the Bureau did not want was sold on the open market to anybody who would pay the freight.

“The relay room,” Danu said, “had a terminal.”

“The terminal.”

“Four tonnes of it. A class four relay terminal with a write-once store, which is the kind of store that cannot be erased except by a Bureau technician with a Bureau order and a Bureau charge of eleven kilowatts for the erasure, which is more than the terminal was worth.” He put another crate down. “So the Bureau did not erase it. The Bureau sold it.”

“To whom?”

“To a dealer on Bukit,” my father said, “called Suryadi. He buys old terminals and strips them for the cores, and he has been doing it for thirty years, and he came to Tsiolkovsky in 2171 with a cargo hull and a crew of four and a cash offer of eleven hundred grams.”

Eleven hundred grams.

“That is a lot of memory,” I said.

“It is eleven years of an ordinary life,” Danu said. “It is also the seal fees for the next twenty-seven years at the 2171 rate, before the compounding in table three, which nobody had explained to me, and which I did not understand until 2179.”

I looked at the crates.

“You sold the terminal.”

“I sold the terminal.”

“The terminal my mother put her hand on.”

“The terminal your mother put her hand on,” Danu said, “with a stylus in her other hand and a child on the bench behind her, at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161.”

---

This is where I have to put the two clocks, because my father told the story in two of them at once, and I have never worked out whether he did it on purpose.

The first clock was 2171. A station with eleven thousand people was being taken apart by crews who had been hired by the month, and the sea of the observatory dome was being cut into sections, and the relay room was stripped, and the terminal was loaded onto a cargo hull by four men and a crane.

The second clock was 2183. In the same hours of the same day, four hundred million kilometres away, a station with four thousand people was being prepared for a different kind of decommissioning, and a queue had formed at the north lock, and Kirana was standing on the yellow line with her tally slate against her chest, counting crates.

At the same hour in 2171, I was seventeen and at the academy in Bukit, learning to weigh things.

At the same hour in 2183, a woman I had met in a cold-well room was sitting in an interview room refusing to answer a question until the second shift undocked.

Meanwhile, on the inner routes, a tender with a cutting crew had been at its second relay since the fourteenth.

“The dealer paid in grams,” I said. “What did you spend it on?”

“The fees,” Danu said. “The compounding seal fees on file 44-1190, and the maintenance on your mother’s account, and the bond on your dependant file, and eleven other things that I have in this drawer and have never once justified to anybody.”

“And the rest?”

“There is no rest,” he said. “I sold a terminal for eleven hundred grams and it bought eleven years, and then the tariff ate the eleven years, and in 2179 I started selling the station’s spare cores, and in 2181 I sold the ring’s reserve buoy, and in 2182 I stopped sleeping.”

---

“Why are you telling me this now?” I said.

“Because you asked who paid.”

“I asked Sekar who paid.”

“And she pointed at a sheet of paper,” my father said, “and the paper was the casualty schedule, and the casualty schedule has nine names on it and one of them is Halim’s, and Halim paid for something and I do not know what it was.”

He opened the bottom drawer and took out a folded sheet, and unfolded it on the desk between us.

It was an inventory. It was four pages long. Column one was a list of equipment, column two was a weight, column three was a destination, column four was a date. The dates ran from 2169 to 2182. The destinations were Bukit, Surabaya, the inner routes, a dealer in the belt, a dealer in the belt, a dealer in the belt.

At the bottom of the fourth page, in the same small forward-leaning hand, was a line that read:

*CLASS 4 RELAY TERMINAL — TSIOLKOVSKY RELAY ROOM — 4,011 kg — SURYADI, BUKIT — 2171.04.11 — 1,100 g.*

I looked at the date for a long time.

“The eleventh of April,” I said.

“The eleventh of April.”

“That is my birthday.”

“It is your birthday,” Danu said. “Seventeen years to the day.”

“That is a coincidence.”

“That is not a coincidence,” he said. “Suryadi wanted the terminal loaded and out before the decommissioning audit, and the decommissioning audit was on the twelfth, and the only crane slot available was the eleventh. I have told myself for twelve years that it was a coincidence, and I have never once believed it.”

---

I put my hand flat on the inventory.

“Where is it now?” I said. “The terminal.”

“In a yard on Bukit,” Danu said. “Suryadi strips terminals. He has a yard with forty of them in it, and he strips them for the cores and sells the shells for scrap, and he does not strip fast, because there is no hurry in a yard.”

“Then it might still exist.”

“It might still exist.”

“And if it exists,” I said, “it has the write-once store, and the write-once store has the original entries, and the original entries include the payment of the twentieth of September, 2161, with a time of 07:00:11 and a bond of forty grams and a name.”

“They include the name,” my father said. “They include the weight. They include the origin stamp. They include, I am almost certain, the handwriting.”

“The handwriting.”

“A class four terminal records the stylus,” he said. “Every stroke. It is how the register proves that the hand that signed is the hand that was there. Your mother’s hand is in that store, on a payment slip, on the morning of the twentieth of September, 2161, and the store is write-once, and the only way to destroy it is to erase it with a Bureau technician and a Bureau order and eleven kilowatts.”

He folded the inventory and put it in my hand.

“One more thing,” he said.

“What?”

“Why I never sold it to be erased. Why I have been paying the maintenance on a dead woman’s account since 2161, and selling cores, and lying to a magistrates’ office, and letting my daughter believe that her mother stopped loving her.”

“Why?”

My father looked at the four crates and the eleven years of paper.

“I sold it because it still had her handwriting in it,” he said.

*Bersambung: Chapter Thirty-Five — The Suit Log, belum ditulis.*
