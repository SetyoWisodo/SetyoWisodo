He came to my quarters at eight that night with a bottle of something amber and two glasses, which on Kemuning means either a celebration or a confession, and I had done nothing worth celebrating.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” I said.

“The Biro has its own key,” he said. “It’s in the charter.”

“You keep saying that.”

“It keeps being true.”

I let him in. My quarters are one room and a wet cell, with a bunk, a table, a chair, and a window that looks at a wall. There is a photograph on the shelf above the bunk of a woman with brown hands and a scar across her left thumb, standing in front of a dish antenna the size of a house. I did not turn it face down. I thought about it. I decided that hiding it would be worse.

Rasya saw it, of course. He saw it the moment he came in, and he did not look away, and he did not say anything, and then he poured two glasses and sat down in the chair without being asked.

“I want to tell you something,” he said, “and I want to tell it in the right order, and I have been rehearsing the order for three years, so I would be grateful if you did not interrupt.”

“I interrupt.”

“I know.”

He drank. So did I. The amber was station whisky, which is to whisky what the tea is to tea, and it burned all the way down in a way I have always found companionable.

“My sister,” he said. “Her name was Ayu. She was eleven when she died. She died on the lunar side, at Tsiolkovsky Station, in the spring of 2161.”

The glass stopped halfway to my mouth.

“That’s the same spring,” I said.

“I asked you not to interrupt.”

“That’s the same spring my mother died.”

“I know,” he said. “I’m getting there.”

***
He told it in the right order, and the right order was this.

While he talked, I did the thing I do, which is to put two clocks side by side.

He was twelve in 2161. His sister Ayu was eleven. Their parents were relay technicians on the Farside network, which in those years was the most isolated posting in the nine systems, eleven thousand people under a dome that had been built by a company that no longer existed, doing astronomy that nobody on the near side cared about because the near side had weather in its politics and the far side did not.

In the spring of 2161 there was an incident at Tsiolkovsky. The official record calls it a depressurisation in Ring C. Nine dead. His sister was one of them. His parents survived because they were on the night shift in the antenna field and the incident happened at four in the morning in a ring they were not in.

Meanwhile, on the same evening in that same spring, a woman I would not meet for another eleven years was standing in a corridor at Tsiolkovsky with a child on her hip, and the clock above the door was reading the same hour as the clock in my quarters, because both clocks were set by the same relay. That is what a network is. It is not a place where things happen together. It is a place where things are recorded as happening together, and the difference between those two sentences is eleven days long.

“I was twelve,” he said. “They put me in a room with a counsellor and the counsellor gave me a picture of Ayu and told me to say goodbye to it. That’s the procedure. You say goodbye to the picture. Then they take the picture away and you go back to school.”

“Rasya.”

“I’m not finished.”

He poured another glass.

“The Biro closed the incident in eleven weeks. Nine dead, cause: seal failure, no finding of negligence. The file is three hundred pages long and I have read all of it, twice, once at twenty and once at thirty, and there is a thing in it that has never made sense to me.” He looked at me. “The manifest. The nine who died. There were eleven people in Ring C that morning at four o’clock according to the duty roster. Nine bodies were recovered. Two were never found.”

“A depressurisation doesn’t leave bodies,” I said. “It takes them.”

“Yes. Except that the two who were never found were listed in the casualty schedule as removed, not unrecovered. Different code. Different form. Removed is what they write when a body has been taken away by somebody, and there was nobody at Tsiolkovsky to take a body away, because the morgue was in Ring C.”

I put my glass down.

“Who were the two?”

“A woman called Sekar Adisurya,” Rasya said. “And a child.”

***
I want to say that the room spun. It did not. The room was very ordinary. There was a bottle on the table and a photograph on the shelf and a wet towel over the back of the chair, and the light was the yellow light of every station corridor in the nine systems, and outside the wall there was a wall.

“A child,” I said.

“A girl. Seven years old. No name in the casualty schedule. Just minor, dependant, female, seven.”

“There were nine dead.”

“Nine dead and two removed,” he said. “And when I asked the registry in 2179 for the file, they told me the file had been sealed at the request of the next of kin, and when I asked who the next of kin was, they told me the name was also sealed, and when I asked how a name could be sealed, they told me it was sealed under section nine.”

“Section nine again.”

“Section nine again.”

I picked up my glass and found it empty and did not remember drinking it.

“You came here,” I said.

“I came here.”

“Three years ago.”

“The sixth of March, 2180,” Rasya said. “I came in on the Rendang, which was called the Rendang then too, and I stayed one day, and I did not use my own name.”

“What name did you use?”

“R. Handoko. There’s a Handoko in the Biro registry, he’s dead, he doesn’t mind.”

“And what did you do on your one day?”

He looked at the photograph on the shelf.

“I stood in the corridor outside this room for two hours,” he said, “and I did not knock. And then I went to the relay room and asked the engineer on duty a question about the buoy maintenance cycle, and she answered it, and she had brown hands with a scar across the left thumb, and she looked at me as if I were a piece of equipment that had arrived without a manifest.”

“I remember that,” I said, and I did — a thin man in a grey coat asking about the maintenance cycle, and me answering, and then not thinking about him again for three years. “You asked about the cycle.”

“I asked about the cycle because I wanted to see your hands.”

“Why?”

“Because there was a photograph in the file,” he said. “Photograph eight, page sixty-one. A woman in front of a dish antenna, with her hand on the rim of it, and a scar across her thumb.”

“And you thought —”

“I thought,” Rasya said, “that the woman in the photograph had a daughter, and that the daughter had the same hands, and that if the daughter was alive then the casualty schedule was a lie, and if the casualty schedule was a lie then my sister’s death was a lie, and I wanted to know which lie I had been living in for nineteen years.”

The bottle was three-quarters empty. I had not noticed.

“You paid for my file,” I said.

“I paid a broker in Surabaya eleven grams.”

“Eleven grams.”

“It’s the rate for a dependant file,” he said. “Eleven grams of my own memory. I don’t know which eleven. That’s the thing nobody tells you when you’re young. You pay in grams and you never find out what they took. You just wake up one morning and there’s a hollow where something used to be, and you can’t tell whether it was a good thing or a bad thing, only that it’s gone and it was yours.”

I looked at him, and for the first time since the doorway, I did not see a Biro auditor.

I saw a man who had been carrying a file for nineteen years and had never once put it down.

***
“Show me the file,” I said.

“I can’t.”

“You just told me you paid for it.”

“I paid for the summary,” Rasya said. “Eleven grams buys the summary: date of birth, date of death, dependant file number, and a list of the sealed annexes. It does not buy the annexes.”

“Then show me the summary.”

He took a folded sheet out of his coat — an actual sheet, printed on actual paper, which on Kemuning is a kind of pornography — and put it on the table.

DEPENDANT FILE 44-1190 — SUMMARY
Subject: ADISURYA, N. (minor, 7)
Incident: TSIOLKOVSKY RING C — 2161.09.11
Injury: thermal, left forearm
Treatment: 9 sessions, 9 days
Outcome: [SEALED]
Annexes: 4 [SEALED]
Authority: [SEALED]

I read it four times.

“Nine sessions,” I said. “Nine days.”

“Yes.”

“That’s not a debridement. A debridement is two sessions. Three, if it goes bad.”

“I know.”

“The burn wasn’t bad enough for nine sessions. I’ve seen the scar. It’s four centimetres.”

“I know,” Rasya said. “That’s what I’ve been trying to tell you since the twenty-ninth of October. Whatever happened to you in that hospital, it was not treatment for a burn.”

I put my hand flat on the paper.

“You said you were going to leave.”

“I should.”

“You haven’t.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He looked at me for a long moment.

“Because I have been in this system for nineteen years,” he said, “and I have never once found a file that wanted to be read as badly as yours does.”
