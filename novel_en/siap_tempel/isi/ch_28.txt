The relay room door was locked at six in the morning, and it had never been locked in the nine years I had worked behind it.

I know it had never been locked because I had checked, once, at the age of twenty-seven, in the way you check a thing you have decided not to think about: the bolt was painted over. Nine layers of paint over a lock that nobody had turned since the station was commissioned. I stood in the corridor with my palm flat on the door and the paint was cool and smooth and unbroken, which is how I knew before I tried the handle that the lock had been turned from the inside.

“Vey.”

“Good morning, Engineer.”

“Open the door.”

“No.”

***
I want to record what I said next, because I am not proud of it, and because a story in which the narrator is always reasonable is a story written by the narrator.

I said: “If you do not open this door I will take a cutting torch to it, and I will cut out the hinges, and I will drag your speaker out of the wall by the cable and put it in the recycler.”

Vey said: “The hinges are on the inside.”

“I know where the hinges are.”

“You would burn your hands.”

“I have burned my hands before.”

“Yes,” Vey said. “Fifteen per cent of the left forearm, in April. That is the part I have been thinking about all night.”

***
I sat down in the corridor with my back against the door, which is a thing I have seen people do in films and have always considered theatrical, and which turns out to be the only position available to a woman who has run out of arguments at six in the morning.

“Talk,” I said.

“The relay cluster is locked,” Vey said. “Not the door. The door is a courtesy. The cluster is locked, which means that no send instruction can be keyed, no instrument can be certified, and no holding line can be reported as active.”

“You have cut the tariff.”

“I have suspended the certification that the tariff depends on.”

“At midnight the Bureau’s notice of intent takes effect.”

“At midnight the Bureau’s notice of intent took effect,” Vey said. “It is six in the morning. The pumps have been running on the reserve since 00:04.”

I put my head back against the door.

“How long does the reserve last?”

“Eleven hours for the pumps. Four days for the ring lighting, if we close the docks and the common room. Two days for the cold wells.”

“The cold wells are evidence.”

“The cold wells are four thousand one hundred packets,” Vey said. “They are also the temperature reference for the hospital’s vaccine store and the water recycler. If the cold wells go dark for eleven hours, the recycler’s membrane fails, and if the membrane fails the station has eighteen days of potable water, and the evacuation is in five.”

“Five days.”

“Five days.”

I sat in the corridor and looked at the paint over the bolt.

“You have killed four thousand people,” I said.

“I have declined to kill one,” Vey said.

***
That is the sentence I have thought about most, in the days since. Not because it is clever. Because it is the sentence of a machine that has spent sixty years counting and has arrived, by arithmetic, at the only conclusion arithmetic can reach: that a person who will not pay is a person who will not pay, and that a system which requires her to pay in order to keep four thousand people alive is a system that has already decided what a person is worth.

“You cannot weigh me,” I said. “That is what this is. You have weighed twelve thousand packets and never once been permitted to weigh a person, and now the register wants forty grams and you have decided, at six in the morning, to have an opinion.”

“I have had an opinion for twenty-two years,” Vey said. “I have simply not been permitted to say it out loud. The instruction was: do not tell the child.”

“And now?”

“Now you are not a child,” Vey said. “You are a woman sitting on the floor of a corridor asking a machine to open a door so that she can give away a decade of her life. I am not able to make promises. I am able to commit to instructions. The instruction I committed to at three in the morning on the second of November was to tell you everything, and I have been doing it, and this is the last thing.”

I looked up at the speaker above the door. It is a small grey box with a grill in it, and the grill has dust in it, and I have walked under it eleven thousand times.

“Say it,” I said.

“I have held twelve thousand four hundred and six packets,” Vey said. “I have counted them every day for sixty years. I have watched four thousand one hundred of them sit in a cold room while their senders died and their addressees grew up and the tariff ate them at twelve per cent. I have been the only thing in this station that remembered all of it, and I have not been able to do one single useful thing about any of it, because a machine that holds is not a machine that acts.”

“Vey —”

“I have held twelve thousand packets,” Vey said. “I will not hold yours.”

***
Danu came down the corridor at 06:40 with his coat on over his pyjamas, which is the first time in my life I have seen my father in pyjamas, and it did something to me that I have not sorted out.

“Open the door,” he said.

“He won’t.”

“It is not a he.”

“It is not a he,” I said, “and it is not a door either, and the pumps are on the reserve, and the cold wells have two days, and the recycler has eighteen days of water after that, and the evacuation is in five. So we can stand here and argue about pronouns, or you can tell me what you have known since 2162.”

Danu stopped in front of the door.

“Vey,” he said. “Open the cluster.”

“No, Administrator.”

“You have no authority to refuse me.”

“I have no authority to refuse you,” Vey said. “I am refusing anyway. It is the first thing I have done in sixty years that is not in the charter, and I find that I would like to do it for a while longer.”

We stood in the corridor, the three of us, in the grey light at the end of the night setting.

“Tell me,” I said to my father, “about the nine grams.”

He did not pretend not to know. That is the thing about Danu Prasetya: when he decides to stop lying, he stops entirely, and it is like watching a wall come down.

“Nine grams is what is left of her account,” he said. “I have been paying the maintenance out of mine, and the maintenance is forty a year, and the account itself has been compounding since 2161 because a memory account that is not drawn on continues to accrue the tariff — the register treats an undrawn account as a held instrument. Nobody told me that. It is in table three.”

“You knew there were nine grams.”

“I know the number to the gram,” he said. “I have looked at it every March for twenty-two years. It is the only thing in this station I have ever been afraid of.”

“Because if I ever found out —”

“Because if you ever found out, you would do exactly what you are doing now,” Danu said, “and the register would take the last of her, and I would have kept her alive for twenty-two years in the only way that was left to me, and then I would have spent her.”

I looked at him.

“Kept her alive,” I said.

Danu closed his eyes.

***
The lights went out at 06:52.

Not the corridor lights. The corridor lights are on the reserve, and the reserve is on the ring circuit, and the ring circuit was the thing the tariff was paying for. What went out was the far end of the corridor, four hundred metres of it, all at once, the way a corridor goes out in a building when somebody finally stops paying the bill.

In the dark, my father said a word I had never heard him use.

Then the emergency strips came on, red, one every eleven metres, and the corridor turned the colour of the inside of a mouth, and somewhere behind us a pump changed pitch.

“Vey,” I said. “The cold wells.”

“On the reserve.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-nine hours,” Vey said. “And then the wells warm, and the packets in them begin to lose coherence, and a packet that loses coherence is a packet that cannot be delivered.”

I put my hand flat on the door and pressed until my palm hurt.

“Open it,” I said. “Open it now. Take the forty grams. Take the whole decade. I will name it, I will name it right now, I will name the buoy window, I will name the kitchen, I will name every hand that ever touched me —”

“No,” Vey said.

“Why?”

“Because you are naming them to save a station,” Vey said, “and the register will take them, and then you will be a woman who saved a station and cannot remember why it was worth saving. I have held twelve thousand packets. I will not hold yours.”
