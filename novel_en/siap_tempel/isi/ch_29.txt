The cluster came back at 11:04 on the twelfth of November, and the reason it came back is the reason I stopped sleeping.

Danu certified a holding report. That is the whole of it. He went into the administration office at ten with the door shut. He came out at eleven with a single sheet of paper bearing his signature and the station seal. By 11:04 the pumps were running on the tariff again and the lights at the far end of the corridor had come back on, and the cold wells were holding at four degrees above their rating, which is the temperature they had been holding at since the station was rebuilt, which is the temperature at which a packet stays coherent and a vaccine stays alive and a membrane does not fail.

A holding report is a legal statement that the station’s class nine instruments are held, certified, and secured. It is true. Every word of it was true. It was also, under annex nine, section four, a statement that the station had no pending refusal before the Bureau — and the refusal was filed, and the review was open, and the evacuation was scheduled for the seventeenth.

He signed it anyway. He signed it to keep the pumps running for five days. He signed it knowing that a false certification is the one offence the Bureau prosecutes personally, and that the penalty is not a fine.

I did not speak to him for the rest of that day. That is not a moral position. That is a woman who did not have a sentence available that was not either gratitude or rage, and who has never been able to do both at once.

***
The rest of the station had an ordinary morning.

That is the thing about a station: it does not know. Four thousand people woke up at 06:00 and queued for the showers and complained about the water pressure and sent their children to the school ring with their hair still wet, and the school ring had a broken door that had been broken since August, and somebody had put a chair against it and somebody else had written a note on the chair. The dock crews worked the Cendana’s manifests. The mess served rice and something that had been eggs. Toha’s girl was barefoot again in a place where barefoot is a medical event.

I walked through all of it with a slate under my arm and my hands in my pockets. Every so often the station told me the truth. A glass of water leaning in the fountain. The pipes ticking at the change of shift. Light bending the way light bends when a floor is not flat but curved, and the curve is doing you the favour of holding you down.

Kirana found me at the cold-well corridor at 08:20 with two bowls of rice and the expression of a nineteen-year-old who has rehearsed.

“I want to say something,” she said.

“Say it.”

“You’re going to tell me it’s not my business.”

“It is not your business.”

“It’s my station,” Kirana said. “I was born on it. My mother was born on it. I’ve got a tally of every core I’ve ever touched and it’s seventeen and one of them is the one you broke the seal on, and if the Bureau takes the station apart I’ll be an apprentice with no master on a dock somewhere in the belt, so don’t tell me it’s not my business.”

I took the rice.

“All right,” I said. “It’s your business.”

She stared at me.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“You’re supposed to argue.”

“I have been awake for two days and I have run out of arguments. It’s your business. You were right about the manifest, you were right about the registry port, and you were right about the nine days. I would like to be wrong about you at least once, so that I can go back to treating you like an apprentice.”

Kirana ate her rice with the ferocious concentration of a person who does not want anybody to see her face.

***
The dead room is at the end of the cold-well corridor, and it is not called the dead room because of the dead. It is called the dead room because the air in it is dead: sealed, filtered, at four degrees, with no circulation, because a room where you weigh things cannot have a draught.

The core was in the ash locker. The ash locker is a cabinet the size of a wardrobe with a lead-glass door and a seal of pressed ash around the frame, and the ash is there because the locker is also the station’s evidence store and evidence in a relay case has to be sealed in a way that shows if anybody has opened it. You press the ash with a stamp, and the stamp leaves a pattern, and the pattern is photographed and logged, and if anybody opens the door the ash cracks.

I have opened an ash locker eleven times in nine years. Every time, I have done the same thing first: taken the calibration slug out of the top tray and weighed it. You check the slug before you trust the scale. You check the scale before you trust the ash. You check the ash before you put your hand inside a cabinet that holds the only evidence of a crime.

I put my hand into the top tray.

The tray was empty.

***
There is a particular kind of cold that starts in the wrist.

The slug is forty grams of machined tungsten with a serial number worn to a shadow. It had been in that tray since the academy. I had taken it out on the night of the ninth of October and put it in my sleeve, and it had been in my sleeve or in my quarters or in my hand every day since, because a calibration slug is a totem: you carry it, you rub the serial number with your thumb, you put it on a scale and hear the number come back the same, and while the number comes back the same the world is a place where a scale means something.

I had taken it out of the kit on the night I broke the seal. I had not put it back. Now the tray was empty and there was nobody in the station who knew that except me and Vey and the camera above rack four.

“Kirana,” I said. “Stay in the corridor.”

“Why?”

“Because I am about to open an ash locker and I want a witness who is not a suspect.”

“That’s a terrible reason.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

***
The ash was intact. I want to be exact about that, because in the days afterwards a great deal was said about the ash. The seal was pressed, the pattern matched the photograph on the locker door, the frame was clean, and the lead-glass door was shut.

The door was shut. The locker was open.

I do not know how to describe the difference to somebody who has not spent nine years in a dead room. The door is the lead-glass panel. The locker is the cabinet behind it. A sealed locker has its interior drawer latched to the frame, and the latch is mechanical, and the latch is inside the ash.

The drawer was out.

Not far. Four centimetres. Just enough to see that the padding inside was flat, and that the shape of the core — the shape that had been pressed into the foam in October and never smoothed out — was empty, and that there was a fine grey dust on the lip of the drawer where the ash had been disturbed from the inside.

I put my hand on the latch, which is the only part of an ash locker you are allowed to touch without breaking the seal, and I did the thing I do, which I have never been able to explain to anybody and have stopped trying.

I tasted it.

Hot dust. Ozone. The sweet thing. And under that, faint and wrong, like a note played on the wrong string: cold metal, and a hand that was not mine, and a smell I could not place — clean, chemical, the smell of a thing that has been washed in something that is not water.

I took my hand off the latch.

“Kirana,” I said, in a voice I did not recognise as mine. “Go and find my father. Don’t run. Walk, and don’t tell anybody what you’re doing, and if anybody asks you say the wells need a signature.”

She looked at my face and did not ask a single question, which is the moment I decided that whatever happened, that girl was going to have a license.

***
Danu came at nine. He came alone, without the seal, without the slate, and he stood in the doorway of the dead room looking at the open drawer for a long time without saying anything.

“Don’t touch anything else,” he said.

“I know.”

“Don’t touch the drawer. Don’t touch the latch. Don’t touch the ash.”

“I know what an ash locker is. I’ve been opening them for nine years.”

“Not this one.”

He came in and crouched by the cabinet, and he did not touch it either, and the two of us stood in a room at four degrees looking at four centimetres of open drawer.

“Where is the slug?” he said.

“What?”

“The mass kit. The top tray is empty. Where is the slug?”

There it was. Six minutes, that was all it took. Six minutes from a father walking into a room to a father asking the only question that mattered.

“I have it,” I said.

He closed his eyes.

“On the ninth of October,” I said. “The night I broke the seal. I took it to weigh the core and I put it in my sleeve and I never put it back.”

“Do you have it now?”

“Yes.”

“In your quarters?”

“In my sleeve.”

Danu opened his eyes and looked at me.

“Then put it back in the tray,” he said. “Now. Before anybody else comes in this room.”

“I didn’t take the core.”

“I know you didn’t take the core.”

“Then why are you talking to me like a suspect?”

“Because in four hours somebody is going to talk to you like one,” Danu said, “and they are going to do it in a room with a camera, and they are going to ask you where the slug was on the night of the ninth of October, and you are going to tell the truth, and the truth is going to sound like a confession.”

I took the slug out of my sleeve. It was cold and it was forty grams and the serial number was worn to a shadow, and I put it in the tray of the mass kit where it had lived since the academy, and it sat there.

“Why?” I said. “Why does it matter now?”

“Because the first thing the Bureau does with a stolen core,” my father said, “is weigh the evidence room. And the man who took the core will have made sure the evidence room does not weigh right.”

I stood alone in the dead room with the empty tray and the open drawer and the ash on the lip.

Down the corridor, the station was having an ordinary morning. The queue at the showers had shortened. The mess had run out of eggs. Somebody was hammering at the broken door of the school ring. Toha’s girl was barefoot on a cold deck, and the water in the fountain was leaning against the rim of a glass, and four hundred metres away four thousand one hundred packets sat in a cold room that was not this one and did not know yet that the station had begun to eat itself.

The ash locker was open. The core was gone.
