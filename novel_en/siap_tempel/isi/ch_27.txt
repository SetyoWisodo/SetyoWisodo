Terminal four is not a bench. It is a chair.

I had walked past it ten thousand times. It sits at the end of the relay room behind a half-wall of frosted glass, and it has a plate on the arm and a hood over the top, and the hood is there because a sender does not want to be watched while they decide what to give away. The plate has the maker’s name on it, worn down now to the shape of a name, and I have never once been curious enough to look up who made it.

Terminal four smells of wool and old breath. Nobody says so. The cleaners come through twice a day and it does not help, because the chairs hold it, the way a house holds the smell of somebody who has stopped living there and nobody has come to clear the room.

“You are certain,” Vey said.

“No.”

“Then we will do it slowly.”

“Slowly is good.”

***
Here is what a send instruction costs.

The register does not take money. It takes mass, and the mass has to come from the sender, and the sender’s mass is their memory. That is not a metaphor and it is not a figure of speech; I have weighed memory for nine years with a brass scale and a calibration slug, and I have never once been able to explain to anybody why the scale works, and the registry has never once been asked to.

Forty grams is the standing deposit for a class nine instrument. Forty grams is what my mother paid at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161, and forty grams is what the register will take from me now if I sit down in that chair and put my hand on the plate and key the sender field of instrument KM-2223-0411-0612.

There is a paper about this, and I have read it, and it does not say what people think it says. Kim and the group at Maryland, 1999, the delayed choice: a decision made after the fact still leaves a single consistent history, and no signal travels backwards to make the history happen. What travels is the record. The record is what turns a set of correlations into a story, and until the record is made, nobody — not the particle, not the physicist, not the woman in the office — has a history at all.

The register understands this better than the physicists do. It does not ask me to remember something. It asks me to state it, because a stated memory becomes a record, and a record can be weighed, and a woman who has stated a decade of her life into a hood has already begun to give it away.

The register takes it from wherever you tell it to take it from. That is the cruelty of the design, and it is deliberate. You do not get to say take something I will not miss. You have to name the memory. You have to describe it, out loud, into the hood, in enough detail that the register can find it in you, and then it takes it and weighs it and tells you what it weighed.

“Forty grams is a decade,” Vey said. “In the units the register uses. A year of ordinary life is about four grams. A decade is forty.”

“A decade of my life.”

“A decade of anybody’s life. It is not calibrated to the person. That is why the figure is fixed: a fixed figure is the only kind that can be defended in a court.”

“And if I do not have a decade to spare?”

“Then you do not send,” Vey said.

***
The plate is warm. It is warm because it is always warm; the terminal has been powered since 2123 and the plate is a heating element as well as a reader, and the warmth is there so that the hand does not flinch when it touches the glass.

I sat down.

The hood came down over my head, not physically — the hood is fixed — but the room went away in the way a room goes away when you are inside a hood with a plate under your hand and a light on your face.

“State the memory,” the register said. Its voice is not Vey’s. The register’s voice is a recording made in 2119 by a woman with a Surabaya accent, and it has been saying the same eleven sentences for sixty-four years.

I put my hand on the plate.

Forty grams. A decade.

I thought about the buoy window. Nine years of standing at it, every night, at the end of the shift — the buoy turning a half-turn every forty seconds, the Earth in the corner of the frame, the particular grey of the light at 22:00 when the corridor lamps go to night setting. If I gave that, I would still have the buoy. I would just not have the nine years of watching it. I would walk past the window and it would be a window.

“State the memory,” the register said.

I thought about my mother’s hands. Not her face — I have the photograph for her face. Her hands, on the stylus, on my shoulder, on the rail of a hospital bed. Four centimetres of scar on my arm and a hand on my shoulder in an archive frame, and if I gave that I would still be able to look at the frame. I would just not be able to feel it.

“State the memory.”

I thought about the smell of the dead room. Hot dust and ozone and the sweet thing I have never been able to name. I thought about the taste of ginger in a kitchen that no longer exists. I thought about the way Rasya’s hand had come up to the side of my face in the dark, and the way the racks had hummed, and the way the lights had come back on and neither of us had noticed.

The register waited. It has no timer. That is the worst thing about the plate: it will wait forever, and forever is a very long time to hold your hand on a warm surface while a woman from 2119 asks you to name the thing you are willing to stop being.

“I cannot,” I said.

“State the memory.”

“No.”

There was a pause. Then the plate went cool under my palm, which is what it does when it decides the session has ended.

***
I came out from behind the half-wall of frosted glass and stood in the relay room with my hands shaking and my whole life intact, and I have never felt so much like a coward and so much like a rich woman at the same time.

“Nothing?” Vey said.

“Nothing.”

“You have four thousand one hundred packets to save.”

“I know.”

“And a decade to spare. By any measure a decade is a modest price for four thousand people’s post.”

“It is not a modest price. It is a decade. It is ten years of my life, and if I give it, I will not be able to remember which ten. The register does not tell you which ten. It takes what it finds when it looks for the thing you described, and it takes the whole neighbourhood. My mother paid for the minute I was born and lost every hand that ever held her.”

“You are saying the price is not the memory. The price is the neighbourhood.”

“I am saying the price is a woman,” I said. “I am saying my mother paid forty grams and became somebody who could not pick up her daughter, and if I sit in that chair and name a decade, I will become somebody who cannot feel a hand on her face, and I will not know that anything is missing. That is the part that frightens me, Vey. Not the loss. The not knowing.”

The racks hummed. The buoy turned. The register behind the frosted glass said nothing at all, because the register only speaks when a hand is on the plate.

“Engineer,” Vey said, “I am required to tell you that there is another way to complete the instrument.”

I turned around.

“Say it.”

“The register accepts a sender instruction from the bond holder, and the bond holder is you, and standing is defined by the bond. But annex nine, section four, subsection nine permits a substituted origin where the original sender is deceased and the addressee is the sender’s next of kin. In that case the deposit is drawn from the estate.”

“From her estate.”

“From what remains of her memory account.”

I sat down on the bench.

“How much remains?”

“Nine grams,” Vey said. “That is what is left of your mother’s account after the register’s fees, twenty-two years of maintenance, and the compounding in table three.”

“Nine grams.”

“Nine grams. Not enough for a standing deposit of forty. A substituted origin requires forty.”

***
I want to put the arithmetic down plainly, because it is the arithmetic of the whole station.

Forty grams is a decade. Nine grams is what is left of my mother.

She paid forty for me in 2161. She paid in the minute I arrived, and the register took the neighbourhood with it, and what was left has been sitting in an account for twenty-two years being eaten by a tariff that compounds at twelve per cent, and what is left now is nine grams — two years and three months of an ordinary life, the last of a woman, held in a vault four hundred metres from where her daughter stands.

“You could combine,” Vey said.

“Combine what?”

“Nine grams from the estate. Thirty-one from you. The register permits a joint origin.”

“Thirty-one grams. Eight years.”

“Seven years and nine months.”

I looked at the chair behind the frosted glass. I looked at my own hand, which had been on the plate and had come off it. I looked at the window, and the buoy, and the grey light on the corridor at night setting.

“I put my hand on the plate and took it off again,” I said. “Forty grams. I could not think of one.”
