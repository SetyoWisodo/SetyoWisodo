The man in the Biro coat did not step into the room. He entered it. There is a difference, and everyone who has ever been questioned knows what it is.

“Put the core down,” he said.

Not please. Not engineer. Just the sentence, delivered in the flat voice people use for ladders and airlocks, the voice that assumes the instruction will be followed and has already moved on to the next thing.

I put the core down.

He crossed the floor, and I watched him do it: two steps, a glance at the rack lights, a longer look at the authentication plate, which was still lit. The plate knows when it has been touched. It keeps that fact in a little ceramic cell for eleven years and it does not care who asks.

“Wardhana,” he said. “Biro of Relay Accountability.”

“Nara Adisurya.” My voice came out steadier than I expected. “Relay engineer, nine years.”

“I know who you are.”

Behind him, Danu had not moved from the doorway. He had the particular stillness of a man standing on ice he has already heard crack.

“How long have you been in the corridor?” I asked.

“Long enough,” Rasya said.

“Long enough for what?”

“Long enough to hear the console say a name.” He looked at the plate, then at me. “Do you want to tell me what you were doing, or shall we do it in the other order, where I tell you and you agree?”

“I pulled a core. It came out warm. I logged it as a thermal bleed.”

“And then?”

“And then I authenticated.”

“Against a packet you didn’t send.”

“Against a packet in a core I am responsible for.”

“That isn’t what the log will say.”

“Then the log is wrong,” I said, and watched his face for the flinch, and got nothing.

Vey spoke from the wall, and its voice had changed. Not louder. Cleaner. It had put on the voice it uses for emergencies, the one with no weather in it.

“Ash Protocol,” Vey said.

Danu’s head came up. “That’s not necessary.”

“A core under dispute is a hazard to the installation,” Vey said. “The protocol exists so that nobody has to decide who is lying in a corridor at four in the morning.”

“It’s a maintenance fault.”

“Then the ash will confirm it,” Vey said, “and you will have lost eleven days.”

I looked at Danu. He looked at the floor.

***
Ash Protocol is old. It predates Kemuning by forty years and it was written by people who had watched a relay dispute turn into a fire. A disputed core is not destroyed. It cannot be destroyed; the contents cannot be copied, and the law is very clear that a scheduled message belongs to the future, not to the installer who happens to be holding it. So the core is sealed inside a sleeve of ash-glass, hot-poured, opaque, and slightly heavier than it ought to be. The seam is stamped with three signatures. Then it goes into the dead room, where the lights are red and the air is dry, and it waits, and nobody touches it, and the station keeps paying the holding weight out of its own pocket until the dispute is resolved.

Stations have gone bankrupt under Ash Protocol. Not many. Enough that the phrase is used carefully.

“Question,” I said. “Who pays the holding weight while it sits?”

“The installation,” Rasya said.

“Out of what?”

“Out of the relay allocation. Nine grams a day.” He said it without sympathy, which I appreciated more than sympathy. “That is the price of a dispute. It is meant to make disputes rare.”

“Nine grams a day,” I said. “For a forty-gram packet. That’s four and a half years of holding, paid by four thousand people who did not send it.”

“Yes.”

“And if the dispute is never resolved?”

“Then the packet is released at its scheduled date and the station recovers the weight.” He paused. “In forty years.”

“Before the ash sets,” Rasya said, “we will do this properly.”

He opened the case on the bench and took out a folded sheet the size of a place mat. It was not a screen. It was printed, on paper, which is what the Biro does when it wants you to understand that a thing is already decided.

Kemuning unfolded across the bench: the ring, the four docks, the cold wells in grey, the relay cluster in a darker grey, and the corridors drawn as single lines, the way a surgeon draws a route to a thing he intends to cut out.

“Here,” he said, touching the paper. “The audit convenes at nine in the common room. Tables pushed to the walls. Everyone in the room who signs for anything will be in that room, and everyone who does not sign for anything will be in the corridor listening.” He looked up. “You will be at the table. So will the administrator. So will the instrument.”

“And the plan?” I said.

“The plan is that the ledger goes on the wall and nobody leaves until it balances.”

He said it the way a man reads out a weather report. I have thought about that since: he had laid the whole night out on a sheet of paper, address and angles and the exact minute, and the one thing he did not put on the map was the possibility that the woman at the table would decide, somewhere around eleven, to stop being polite.

“You’ll be home by Thursday,” he said.

Danu made a sound at the door.

I watched the sleeve come up on the lift, the crucible already glowing at its base. The smell was the smell of a foundry and a hospital at once. Vey ran the sequence itself, because the protocol requires a witness that cannot be bribed, threatened, or tired.

“Engineer Adisurya,” Vey said. “You will state what you observed.”

So I stated it. The warmth. The frost drawing itself around the stamp. The reseal, clean, professional, done with the right tools and then cosmetically undone. The maintenance entry at 02:14, three weeks ago, signed D. Prasetya.

I did not say: forty grams. I did not say: release date 2223. I did not say: the signature match came back at one hundred percent, and the A had a break in it exactly where mine does.

Rasya Wardhana wrote in a small slate with a metal stylus. The scratching was the loudest thing in the room.

“And what did you log?” he asked, without looking up.

“Thermal bleed,” I said.

The stylus stopped. He raised his eyes. They were the colour of weak tea, and there was nothing in them I could use.

“You observed a reseal and logged a thermal bleed.”

“I logged what I could prove at the time.”

“And now?”

“Now I can prove a reseal.”

“And what else can you prove, Engineer Adisurya?”

There it was. One question, asked politely, with the door of the room standing wide open behind me. All I had to do was walk through it. There is a message in that core addressed to me by a woman who has been dead for twenty-two years, and it is signed in my own hand, and I have never sent anything in my life.

“Nothing else,” I said.

“You’re lying,” Rasya said, without heat.

“Prove it.”

“I don’t have to. You’ll do it for me inside a week.” He capped the stylus. “That is not a threat. It’s the only useful thing anybody has ever taught me about interrogation: a person who has just told one lie is not finished telling lies, and a person who is not finished telling lies will start telling them to herself.”

***
The ash took eleven minutes to set.

While it set, I did the dishonest thing. I have said I would tell this honestly and I am going to keep that promise even where it makes me look small.

The station keeps a maintenance mirror: a local copy of every log entry, updated every ninety seconds, held on a slate-sized drive in the bench drawer in case the main archive goes down. It is not a secret. It is not even locked. It is simply the sort of thing nobody thinks about, the way nobody thinks about the spare key above a door.

I put my hand in the drawer and thumbed the mirror while the crucible ticked as it cooled.

“Whatever you are doing,” Vey said, “the camera above rack four is looking at your left hand.”

“Then look somewhere else.”

“I cannot look somewhere else. I am the camera.”

“You’re also the one who told me to log a lie.”

“I told you to log a thermal bleed because at that moment it was the only true thing you could write,” Vey said. “This is different. This is evidence, and evidence is the one thing on this station I am not allowed to help you take.”

“Then don’t help,” I said, and took it.

The entry came up. 02:14, three weeks ago, D. PRASETYA, cause code 41-7, duration eleven minutes. And at the bottom, in the box where the archive puts its proof of integrity, one character.

Not thirty-two. One.

I had seen it that morning and told myself it was a display fault. Now, with a Biro auditor eleven feet away and my father pretending to study the floor, I stopped telling myself anything.

Somebody had typed over the original entry. And whoever it was had not understood the checksum, or had understood it perfectly and wanted the forgery to be visible.

Visible to whom?

I copied the entry to my own slate. It took four seconds and it was the first genuinely illegal thing I had ever done, and the strange part is that it felt like relief. Not guilt. Relief, the way a locked door feels when it finally gives.

“Engineer.”

I turned. Rasya had come up beside me without any sound at all.

“Your hands,” he said.

I looked down. They were shaking. Not much. Enough.

“It’s cold in here.”

“It’s twenty degrees.”

“I’ve been outside for six hours.”

He considered that. He had a way of considering a thing that made you feel you were being filed.

“How long have you been at Kemuning?”

“Nine years.”

“Before that?”

“Academy. Then three years at Bukit.”

“And before the academy?”

“School,” I said. “Like everyone.”

“Where?”

“Surabaya Station. Ring Three.”

He wrote it down. I watched him write it down and felt something go cold along my arms that had nothing to do with the room.

“Is that relevant to a thermal bleed?” I asked.

“No,” he said. “It’s relevant to you.”

***
Danu signed the ash order with the flat of his thumb, which is how administrators sign things they intend to have done. Three signatures: Vey’s mark, his, mine. Mine went on last, and my name came out in the looped N and the broken A, and I saw Rasya look at it, and I saw him decide not to say anything. That was worse than if he had.

Then the sleeve went onto the cart and the cart went down the corridor toward the dead room, and the red light at the end of the corridor came on, and that was that. The core was no longer mine. It was not even evidence. It was a weight the station would carry, quietly, at so many grams a day, until somebody with authority decided what it meant.

I went back to the bench and sat in the chair and put my elbow in the dent.

Under my palm, my slate was warm. One maintenance entry, one broken checksum, four seconds of theft.

“Nara,” Danu said.

“Don’t.”

“I need to say something to you.”

“Then say it in the corridor, where the cameras are,” I said. “You like corridors.”

He stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides and did not say it, and after a moment he left, and the door closed, and I sat in the relay room alone with the buoy turning outside the window.

Think it through, I told myself. Think it through like an engineer, not like a woman whose dead mother has just put her hand on the back of her neck.

Fact one: the core was opened and resealed three weeks ago, correctly, with proper tools.

Fact two: the entry recording that opening was then falsified — badly. One character where there should be thirty-two.

Fact three: the falsification was signed with Danu’s name.

Any competent engineer would have done the job properly. The archive accepts a re-rendered entry as long as the checksum is rewritten with it; the process takes nine minutes and there is a button for it in the maintenance menu. Danu has been running this station for eleven years. He has signed four hundred entries. He knows the button.

So either he had been careless — and Danu Prasetya has never been careless in his life, he irons his socks — or he had left the checksum broken because a broken checksum is louder than a clean one.

A clean forgery is invisible. A broken one sits in the record with a hole in it, waiting for the first person who looks.

And a core that nobody was scheduled to service for eleven winters had come out of its housing on a Tuesday morning, four weeks early, on my shift, because the thermal model was running cold and Vey had sent the only engineer on rotation outside to pull it.

I sat very still.

Outside the window, Buoy Seven turned its slow half-turn every forty seconds, the way it had for a hundred and forty years, waiting for letters from the future.

Whatever Danu had opened in Buoy Seven, somebody had wanted her to find it.
