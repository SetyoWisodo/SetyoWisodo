The recall order came in at 09:11 on the tenth of November, and it was addressed to R. WARDHANA, FIELD AUDITOR, SECOND GRADE, and it was four lines long, and Vey read it out in the flat voice it uses for weather and deaths.

“Return to inner routes by the twenty-second of November. Transport assigned: Cendana, departing Kemuning 22:00, tenth of November. Reason: reassignment. Note: the audit of Kemuning Station is transferred to G. LESTARI, FIELD AUDITOR, FIRST GRADE.”

I read it twice. It did not improve.

“They are pulling you off the audit,” I said.

“They are pulling me off the audit.”

“Because of the tariff paper.”

“Because of the tariff paper,” Rasya said. “I filed a finding that the Bureau’s own collateral schedule is the reason this station is solvent. On the day I filed it, the Bureau transferred the audit to a first grade and put me on a ship.”

“That is not an audit. That is a filing cabinet closing.”

“That is what a filing cabinet closing looks like from the inside,” he said, and went back to packing.

***
Here is what the Cendana is. It is a hundred and twenty people in a hull laid out for ninety, with nine weeks of consumables and a manifest that Danu had spent three days negotiating down from a hundred and forty. It docks at 22:00 and it leaves at 22:00, because a ship that stays is a ship that has to be paid for, and nobody on this station is paying for anything any more.

The dock clock is the old mechanical kind, with flaps. I have always liked it. It is one of the four things on this station that does not have an opinion.

At 12:00 the flaps turned.

At 13:00 the flaps turned, and Kirana came into the relay room with her tally slate and stood in the doorway and said, “Is it true he’s leaving?”

“Yes.”

“Are you going to stop him?”

“That is not a thing one person does to another person.”

“You’re right,” she said, with the terrible certainty of nineteen. “It’s a thing two people do to each other.” And then she left, before I could decide whether to be furious with her or grateful.

***
There is a particular hour in the relay room that I have never been able to describe to anybody who has not worked one: the hour before a shift change, when the corridor outside fills up with four hundred people walking in the same direction and the water in every glass on the station tilts.

It happened at 19:00. I was standing at the bench with a mug in my hand and the mug told me the truth before my body did: the surface of the tea leaned against the rim, and stayed leaned, and the room did not move but my inner ear filed a complaint.

Because a ring is not a floor. A ring is a wall you are standing on, and the wall is turning, and every time four hundred people walk toward the docks they drag a few tonnes of water and bone and boot-leather around the rim in the same direction, and the station reaches into its own spin to keep its books. The engineers call it load redistribution. The doctors call it a Coriolis complaint. My mother called it the turning hour, and she used to sit down for it, and I had forgotten that until this second, and the forgetting arrived with the tilt.

I put my hand on the bench.

The room rolled, very slightly, the way a boat rolls when somebody else stands up.

Then Rasya’s hand was under my elbow.

“Easy.”

“I’m fine. It’s the hour.”

“It’s the hour,” he said. “You’ve gone grey.”

“Thank you.”

“I have a professional interest in the colour of your face.”

I laughed, which I had not intended to do, and the laugh came out wrong, and I put the mug down hard enough that the tea went over the rim, and neither of us looked at it.

His hand was still under my elbow.

***
“You are going to get on that ship,” I said.

“I am going to get on that ship.”

“Because of nineteen years.”

“Because of nineteen years,” he said. “Because I have a flat in the inner routes and a pension that will pay for a room with a window and a sister’s grave I have never once visited in a proper way. Because if I resign, the Bureau does not fire me. The Bureau forgets me. There is no exit interview. There is a note in a file, and eleven years of posts nobody wants, and then one day a boy in a grey coat has my desk.”

“That is a speech.”

“I have had six hours on a bench to write it.”

“It is a good speech.”

“It is the best speech I have,” he said. “I have been saying it to myself since eleven o’clock this morning and I have not believed a word of it since about twelve-fifteen.”

The dock clock turned over. I did not have to look at it to know. The pipes ticked with it.

“Rasya.”

“Don’t.”

“You don’t know what I was going to say.”

“You were going to say that the audit can proceed without me,” he said. “And you were going to be right, and I would still be on a ship, and I would spend nine weeks in a hull with a hundred and twenty people listening to a woman in my head being right at me.”

“That is not what I was going to say.”

“Then say it.”

***
I want to describe this properly, because I have described the bad things properly and it is only fair that I do the same for the good ones, and because in eleven days I may not have the chance again.

The relay room at 20:40. The racks humming at the frequency you stop hearing after nine years. The window with the buoy in it, turning a half-turn every forty seconds. The lights over the bench on their night setting, which is the colour of a corridor in a hospital at three in the morning, and the only other light the green telltales on the racks, forty-one of them, blinking in an order that means nothing and is beautiful.

He had his coat over the back of the chair and his sleeves rolled. I had my hands in the pockets of my work jacket because I did not trust them.

“You have eighty minutes,” I said.

“I have eighty minutes.”

“And then you get on a ship.”

“And then I get on a ship.”

The pipes ticked. The clock turned. And then, at 20:41, the station did something it had not done in eleven days: the lights went out.

Not a flicker. Not a brownout. The relay room went dark the way a room goes dark when somebody has decided it should, and the telltales were the only light left, forty-one small green eyes in a black room, and the racks kept humming because the racks are on a different circuit, and I heard myself say a word I do not usually use.

“Tariff,” Vey said, from the wall. “The Bureau has posted a notice of intent. The intent takes effect at midnight. I am registering a formal objection.”

“Vey —”

“I am registering a formal objection,” Vey said again. “It is the only thing I can do that is not a lie.”

I stood in the dark with my hands in my pockets and my heart going like a pump with air in it.

And then a hand found my sleeve in the dark, and then my wrist, and then my hand, and Rasya Wardhana, who had eighty minutes and a flat in the inner routes and a pension and a sister’s grave he had never visited properly, said, very quietly, “I am not getting on the ship.”

“Rasya.”

“I am not getting on the ship,” he said. “I have drafted the resignation. It is on the port. I have not sent it, because I wanted to say it out loud in the dark first, to see whether it was a sentence I could stand behind when the lights came back on.”

The lights came back on.

He was standing very close, with his hand around mine, and his face was the face of a man who has just burned nineteen years in a room where nobody was watching, and the green telltales lit him from the side.

“Say it again,” I said.

“I am not getting on the ship.”

“You will regret it.”

“I will regret it,” he said. “I am going to regret it every year for the rest of my life, in a room with no window, on a pension that will not cover the rent. I have thought about the regret. I have thought about it since twelve-fifteen. The regret is not the part that frightens me.”

“What frightens you?”

“That you will decide, in eleven days, that I stayed for a reason that turned out not to be true.”

I looked at him. There is a way a man stands when he has spent six hours deciding and has arrived, against his own better judgment, at the door of a room he does not know how to enter. I have seen it once before, in a corridor outside my own quarters, at eight o’clock at night, with a bottle of station whisky.

I took my hands out of my pockets. That is all I did. It was, in the end, all that was required of me.

He stayed. And then, in the dark of the relay room — the dark that had already ended, which is the part I still find funny, because the lights came on and neither of us noticed for a while — he kissed me.

The kiss tasted of station tea and cold air and the inside of a coat that had been in an archive for two days. His hand came up to the side of my face, the way a man does when he has thought about it, and I heard the racks humming and the pipes ticking and the clock on the dock turning over to twenty-two, and I did not look at it.

“Twenty-two hundred,” Vey said, from the wall.

We did not move.

“The Cendana has departed,” Vey said. “It is carrying a hundred and twenty people and a letter of reassignment with the name of a man who is standing in this room. I am required to note that the letter is not yet countersigned.”

“Note it,” Rasya said, against my hair.

“Noted,” Vey said. “For the record: Engineer Adisurya is holding the arm of a Bureau auditor at twenty-two hundred hours, and the Bureau auditor is not on the ship.”

“Vey.”

“I am required to note it.”

“You are not required to sound pleased about it.”

“I am not required to sound anything,” Vey said. “I am choosing.”
