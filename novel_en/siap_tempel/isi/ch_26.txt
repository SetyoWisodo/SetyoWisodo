The last entry in the ledger is not the last one anybody keyed. It is the last one anybody will receive, and it sat at the bottom of a list of four thousand one hundred lines the way a keystone sits at the top of an arch, holding up everything above it and looking, to anybody who did not know better, like decoration.

“We have a problem with the header,” Vey said.

I was on the bench with a bowl of cold noodles and eleven days of no sleep, and I said, “We have four thousand problems with four thousand headers.”

“This one is different.”

“Read it.”

“INSTRUMENT KM-2223-0411-0612. CLASS NINE. ADDRESSEE: ADISURYA, N. BOND: 2154.04.11, 06:12. RELEASE: 2223.04.11, 06:12:04. SENDER: —” Vey stopped. “SENDER: blank. Flag: sender instruction pending.”

I put the bowl down.

“How many pending sender instructions are in the ledger?”

“One.”

“And the other four thousand and ninety-nine?”

“Keyed. Complete. Every one of them has a sender, a bond, and a release date.”

“So this is a blank.”

“This is an instrument that has been filed and sealed and addressed and bonded,” Vey said, “and has no sender, and under annex nine, section four, an instrument without a sender cannot be released. It cannot be released because there is nobody to release it from. It has no origin.”

***
I want to explain what a sender is, because the whole of what happened next depends on it being exactly as boring as it sounds.

A relay packet is not a letter. A letter has a hand and a stamp and a postmark. A packet is a record — a bundle of mass held in a core, priced by the gram, addressed to a person, held until its release date, and delivered when the relay that holds it receives a send instruction keyed by the sender at the terminal where the sender stands.

The sender is the person who puts the bundle on the plate. The sender is the origin. If there is no sender, the instrument is not a message. It is a sealed box with a name on it, and the register will hold it forever, the way a warehouse holds a crate addressed to a street that no longer exists.

“Can it be keyed by anybody?” I said.

“It can be keyed by anybody with standing at the terminal,” Vey said. “Standing is defined by the bond.”

“Say that again.”

“Standing is defined by the bond,” Vey said. “Annex nine, section four, subsection two. The sender of a class nine instrument must be the person whose bond is lodged against the instrument. The register does not accept a sender who is not the bond. The whole point of a bond of witness is that the relationship is proven before the instrument is complete.”

The relay room hummed. The buoy turned. Forty-one green telltales blinked in an order that meant nothing.

“The bond is my birth minute,” I said.

“The bond is your birth minute.”

“And the person whose bond it is —”

“Is the addressee.”

“So the sender of this instrument is the addressee.”

“The sender of this instrument,” Vey said, “is you.”

***
“Draw it,” I said.

The bench glass lit. Vey draws the way a surveyor draws.

He put a dot at the top and labelled it 2154. He put a dot at the bottom and labelled it 2223. He put the addressee on the left and the sender on the right, and then he drew a line from 2154 down the left side, across the bottom, up the right side, and back to the top, and when he was finished there were four things on the glass and one line, and the line joined them into a shape I recognised from a maintenance class I had taken at nineteen.

“That is a circuit,” I said.

“That is a circuit.”

“It has no beginning.”

“It has no beginning,” Vey said. “It is a loop. The bond at 2154 is what permits the sender at 2223, and the sender at 2223 is what permits the release at 2223, and the release at 2223 is what permits the addressee to exist as an addressee at all. Read it from the top down and it is a gift. Read it from the bottom up and it is a debt. It is the same line either way. That is what a palindrome is.”

I looked at the two dates on the glass.

2154.04.11. 06:12.
2223.04.11. 06:12:04.

Sixty-nine years apart, and the same minute at both ends, and the second one carried four seconds the first one did not have, which is the difference between a birth and a kindness.

“Vey,” I said. “This is a record that was keyed six weeks ago, in eleven days, by three people and a code that is not in the register. It runs to 2201. It has a blank sender. It is addressed to me. And its release date is my birth minute, sixty-nine years later.”

“Yes.”

“So either somebody has built an instrument that will hold a place open for a message that nobody has written —”

“Or the message has already been written,” Vey said, “by the only person the register will accept.”

I put both hands flat on the glass, on the loop, on the two dates.

“No,” I said.

“I am not able to lie to you about arithmetic.”

“No. Say it plainly and let me hear how stupid it sounds.”

“It does not sound stupid,” Vey said. “It sounds like a contract. If the instrument is released in 2223, then by the terms of its own filing, a sender keyed it. The only sender the register accepts is the bond. The bond is you. Therefore, if the packet arrives —”

“Then I sent it,” I said.

“Then you sent it.”

***
“Vey,” I said. “Has anybody tried?”

“Tried what?”

“Tried to key the sender. Somebody built this thing. Somebody had to test it. A blank that has never been tested is a blank that has never been believed in.”

A pause. Not three seconds. Eleven.

“There is one rejected keying attempt,” Vey said. “2183.09.28, 04:12. Terminal four, the inner office. The operator attempted to key the sender field of instrument KM-2223-0411-0612 and was refused.”

“Who?”

“The log records D. PRASETYA.”

I sat very still.

“He tried to send it,” I said. “Six weeks ago. At four in the morning, in his own office, with his own hands.”

“He tried,” Vey said. “The register refused him. He does not have standing.”

“Because the bond is me.”

“Because the bond is you.”

I thought about my father at four in the morning, standing at terminal four in an empty office, trying to fill in a blank that the machine would only accept from a daughter who did not know the blank existed.

“Rejected attempts are logged,” I said.

“Rejected attempts are logged.”

“So he knew he would be logged.”

“He knew,” Vey said. “He tried anyway.”

***
“There is a paper,” Rasya said, when I told him. “Nineteen ninety-nine. Kim, and a group at Maryland. The delayed choice.”

“I know the paper.”

“Then you know what it does and does not say.”

“It does not say you can send a message into the past,” I said. “Nothing sends a message into the past. What it says is that whether a thing behaves like a wave or like a particle can depend on a decision made after it has already gone through the apparatus — and even then, no signal travels back. You only see the pattern when you compare the two records afterwards. The record is the thing that makes the event an event. Until somebody reads it, it is a set of correlations with no story.”

“And the ledger is a record.”

“The ledger is a record,” I said. “Four thousand one hundred entries, keyed in eleven days, six weeks ago, dated to 2201. A record that was read before it was written. A record that only becomes a record when somebody reads it, which is what the paper actually says, and which is exactly what a filing cabinet does, and which is why the Bureau has never once had to break the law to do something unforgivable.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment.

“You are saying the ledger is a kind of reading,” he said.

“I am saying somebody designed it so that the reading is the event,” I said. “And I am the reading. That is the whole trick. They did not need to forge four thousand packets. They needed a bond, and a blank, and a woman who would spend her life trying to fill it in.”

***
I went out to the lock that night and stood with my face against the glass and watched the buoy turn.

Forty seconds a half-turn. A hundred and forty years of it. A chapel-sized object on a tether thin as a nerve, waiting for messages that would not arrive until the people who had sent them were dead.

Behind me, on the bench, the glass still had the loop on it. In front of me was the thing the loop was about.

If it arrives, I must have sent it. There is no version of this where I get to choose.
