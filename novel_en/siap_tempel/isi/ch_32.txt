There were three ships and eleven berths and four thousand and eleven people, and the arithmetic did not work, and I sat in the administration office on the fifteenth of November and watched my father do it for the fourth time.

“Rendang,” Danu said. “Forty. She is a supply hull with forty seats and no recycler worth the name, and she can make the inner routes in nine days if she does not stop.”

“Nine days is too long,” I said.

“Nine days is what she has. Cendana has already gone with a hundred and twenty and nine weeks of consumables, and she is not coming back, because I sold the return leg to pay for her outbound fuel.”

“You sold —”

“I sold the return leg,” my father said, “and I would do it again, and it is in the ledger, and you may be angry with me after the numbers.”

He turned the sheet around.

The third ship was not a ship. The third ship was a cargo hull called the Bukit Tiga that had been laid up at the outer dock for eleven years with a cracked frame and eight hundred bunks bolted into her hold by a dock crew who had been paid in water. She had a recycler that had been installed at night by a man named Toha, who is not an engineer, and whose daughter is eleven and goes barefoot on a cold deck.

“Eight hundred,” I said.

“Eight hundred.”

“Plus forty. Plus a hundred and twenty.”

“Nine hundred and sixty,” Danu said, “if everything works, and if the Bukit Tiga holds pressure, and if the recycler does not kill anybody, and if the Bureau does not impound her for being an unregistered passenger hull, which she is.”

“And the other three thousand?”

“The other three thousand stay in the ring,” my father said, “and the ring is dechartered on the seventeenth, and a dechartered installation has no tariff, no relay license, and no legal existence. It becomes a structure. People can live in a structure for a while. Eleven days, if the wells hold. Longer if the Bureau is slow.”

“And if the Bureau is not slow?”

“Then the Bureau sends a tender to cut the relay cluster out of the ring,” Danu said, “and a tender needs a berth, and there are eleven berths, and I have been counting berths for six days.”

***
This is the part where Vey was useful, and I want to say so, because in the days afterwards there were people on that station who said the machine had gone wrong.

“Vey,” I said. “How many people can the ring evacuate in six days, at the current dock throughput?”

“Two hundred and eleven,” Vey said.

“That is nothing.”

“That is the berth arithmetic,” Vey said. “Eleven berths, one shift per berth, four hours per shift, six days. A berth can take a hundred kilos of luggage per passenger or a pressure cot, not both. At the current manifest discipline the ring moves eleven hundred people a day through the docks. At evacuation discipline, with children and the hospital, it moves two hundred and eleven people in six days, because the hospital alone requires forty berths of its own.”

“Then we change the discipline.”

“Then you will need a plan,” Vey said, “and a plan is a document, and a document is something I can execute.”

***
We built the plan on the table with paper and a ruler, the way you build a plan when the computers are all busy being honest.

Danu drew the docks. Eleven berths around the outer ring, numbered from the north lock clockwise, and each berth with a pressure barrier and a cradle and a hose. He drew them to scale, and the scale was one centimetre to ten metres, and the ring came out the size of a dinner plate.

I did the medical list with Kirana. There were ninety-one names on it by the end of the first hour: people who could not be moved without a pressure cot, people who could not be moved at all, people whose names were on a list because a doctor in 2179 had written do not transport in a box and nobody had ever taken it off.

Ninety-one names on a ring of four thousand is not a large number. It is two per cent. You can hold two per cent in your head, and that is the danger of it: a number small enough to hold is a number small enough to stop feeling.

Kirana read them out. I wrote them down. At the forty-first name she stopped.

“Adisurya,” she said.

“That’s me.”

“No,” Kirana said. “It says S. Adisurya. And under it, in the box, it says remains.”

***
Here is what the hospital register said, and I have the sheet, and I have read it eleven times.

The register is not a list of patients. It is a list of bodies the station is responsible for moving in an evacuation, and it is compiled from the death register, because a dead person is a body and a body needs a berth, and the Bureau has never in its history permitted a body to be left behind on a dechartered installation.

Ninety-one names. Forty-one of them living, with a pressure cot and a doctor’s note. Fifty of them dead.

And one of the dead was S. Adisurya, interred at Tsiolkovsky, Farside wall, niche 4471, remains transferable — a body in a wall four hundred million kilometres away that the station was legally responsible for moving, because a death certificate is a fact and a fact has a berth.

“She’s not in the wall,” I said.

“The register does not know that,” Danu said.

“She’s in the interview room. She’s been in the interview room for a day and a half, refusing to answer questions until the evacuation is under way, and the register has her in a wall on the Farside and a berth on a ship.”

“Then the register is wrong.”

“The register is never wrong,” I said. “That is the thing about the register. It is not a record of what happened. It is a record of what was filed, and what was filed is that Sekar Adisurya is dead and her remains are transferable, and if she gets on a ship under her own name she will be getting on as a corpse.”

Danu was quiet for a moment.

“Then she does not get on under her own name,” he said.

***
This is where I have to put the two clocks, because the next four days were lived in two of them at once and I have never been able to tell the story any other way.

The first clock was the station’s. It ran on shifts and berths and the cold wells, and its numbers were: six days to dechartering, twenty-nine hours of reserve on the wells, eleven berths, two hundred and eleven people, ninety-one names.

The second clock was the Bureau’s. It ran on filings and notices, and its numbers were: eleven days from the refusal to the review, five days from the review to the evacuation, and a tender — a hull with a cutting crew and a legal writ — that had left the inner routes on the fourteenth and would be at Kemuning on the twenty-first, four days after the evacuation was supposed to be finished.

Four days.

I did the arithmetic on the back of the medical list, in pencil, at 23:40 on the fifteenth of November, while Kirana slept sitting up in a chair with her tally slate on her knees.

If the evacuation started on the seventeenth and ran at two hundred and eleven people per six days, the station would be empty of evacuees in the year 2195.

If it ran at eleven hundred people a day, which is what the docks could do with the discipline of a working station, it would take four days. Four days is exactly the window between the evacuation and the tender.

I looked at that number for a long time.

And meanwhile, four hundred million kilometres away, in an office on the inner routes that I have never seen and will never see, a man or a woman I will never meet was signing a filing that would arrive on the twenty-first with a cutting crew, and the filing would be correct, and the law would be on its side, and the eleven berths would be full.

“Vey,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Can the docks do eleven hundred a day?”

“The docks can do eleven hundred a day,” Vey said. “The station cannot. Eleven hundred a day requires every dock crew on shift, every berth hot, the hospital on a priority manifest, and somebody to sign for four thousand people, one at a time, with a witness.”

“Who signs?”

“The administrator signs for the installation.”

“And who signs for the people?”

“The people sign for themselves,” Vey said. “That is what a manifest is. It is four thousand signatures, in a queue, at a dock, in the dark, with a case in the other hand.”

***
I put the pencil down.

“Then we need four thousand signatures in four days,” I said.

“Four thousand and eleven.”

“Four thousand and eleven signatures.”

“And a plan for the ninety-one,” Vey said, “and a berth for a body that is not in a wall, and a way to keep the Bureau’s tender out of the eleven berths until the last ship has undocked.”

The list of who could not be moved was ninety-one names long, and my mother’s was on it.
