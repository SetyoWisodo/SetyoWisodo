The recording ran on.

07:00:08. The child put the stylus down. The woman took the form and turned it over and read the back of it, the way you read the back of a thing you have already decided about, and then she did something the camera caught in three frames and my memory caught in none: she put her hand on the child’s head and held it there.

07:00:09. The child said something. No microphone. The camera had no microphone. I have said that already and I will say it again, because it is the fact I keep coming back to: twenty-two years of evidence about the worst morning of my life, and not one second of it has any sound.

07:00:10. The woman crossed to the relay terminal and put her palm on the plate.

07:00:11. The terminal logged a payment. Forty grams, personal account, class nine custodial registration, addressee: ADISURYA, N.

07:00:12. The woman turned around. Half a second of a turning body, and in that half second the whole of the next twenty-two years is decided by a register with no opinion about anything.

And then my mother looked at me — at the child, at the seven-year-old on the bench with the bandage and the wrong A — and she did not go to her.

I want to be careful here, because I have been careless about this before, at three in the morning, in my own head. She did not refuse. Her body did not flinch. She stood with her hand still half-raised, the way a person stands when they have been interrupted in the middle of a movement, and her face did the thing that faces do when a name has been called and the person cannot find the room it was called from.

Then she picked up the canvas bag and said something to the child, and the child slid off the bench and followed her out, and the door closed, and the relay room was empty for the next eleven hours.

I ran it back. I ran it forward. I ran it at a quarter speed, and at a tenth, and at the setting below a tenth that has no name, and in every version my mother crosses a room, logs a payment, turns, and does not embrace her daughter.

***
“She was frightened,” Danu said.

“She was not frightened. I know what frightened looks like on her. There is a photograph of it on my shelf.”

“Then she was in a hurry.”

“She stood there for four seconds doing nothing,” I said. “Nobody in a hurry stands still for four seconds.”

Rasya had his elbows on the bench and his hands over his mouth, which is the only gesture of his I have ever seen that was not chosen.

“The payment,” he said. “Read the payment again.”

“Forty grams. Personal account. Class nine custodial registration.” I put my finger on the line. “Addressee: me.”

“What is a bond of witness?”

The room went quiet. Danu did not move, and that was the answer, and all three of us knew it was the answer, and it took Vey four seconds to say it out loud.

“A bond of witness is a memory deposited with a class nine register to establish a relationship between the payer and the addressee,” Vey said. “The register does not accept testimony. It accepts mass. Under annex nine, section four, the deposit is weighed, sealed, and held, and may not be withdrawn except by order of a magistrate.”

“How much mass establishes a relationship?” Rasya said.

“Forty grams,” Vey said. “The deposit is standard.”

“And what memory did she deposit?”

“The description is withheld,” Vey said. “Annex nine, section four, subsection six. The register records the weight and the origin stamp. It does not record the content.”

“The origin stamp,” I said. “What is the origin stamp?”

A pause. Three seconds. A paragraph.

“2154.04.11. 06:12,” Vey said. “Tsiolkovsky Station, Ring C.”

***
I want to explain what that date is, and I want to do it without putting my fist through the wall of the archive room, so I will do it in the plain way.

The eleventh of April, 2154, at twelve minutes past six in the morning, is when I was born.

Forty grams is what my mother took out of her own memory account, at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161, in a relay room on a station that no longer exists, so that a register would believe that she was my mother. She paid for the proof with the thing being proved. She gave them the minute I arrived. That is the bond of witness: a mother, handing over the first thing she ever knew about her daughter, in order to be legally allowed to be responsible for her.

And the register took it, and sealed it, and gave her a receipt.

“She would have had a copy,” Danu said. His voice was strange. “The register gives you a slip. You keep the slip.”

“Where is it?”

“In the file,” he said. “In the sealed file, with the nine days of notes and the death certificate and the form.”

“Then open the file.”

“I cannot open the file.”

“You signed the seal.”

“I signed the seal because I could not open the file,” Danu said, and for the first time in the nine years I have known him as an adult, my father raised his voice. Not much. One step. But the archive room was fourteen degrees and his face was not.

***
Here is what I have worked out since, and I have to be honest about the difference between what the record shows and what I believe, because I have spent this whole account trying to keep that line visible.

The record shows this: at 07:00:11 on the twentieth of September, 2161, Sekar Adisurya deposited a memory weighing forty grams. At 07:00:12 she turned around. She did not embrace her daughter, and she did not embrace her daughter again — not that morning, not in the hospital the next day, not once in the eleven days before the ring came apart. I know that because I have the camera files, all of them, and I have watched them at a speed that made Rasya leave the room.

What I believe, and cannot prove, is this.

A memory is not a tape. A memory is a thing you rebuild every time you take it out, and rebuilding is not free — it needs material, and time, and a quiet hour, and the chemistry of a body that is not being shot at. That part is not my idea. It was in the paper Rasya had in his case, the one from 2000, the one about fear and the amygdala: take a memory out and it goes soft, and it has to be put back, and while it is soft it can be broken, and while it is soft it can also fail to be put back.

If you take the minute of a birth out of a woman and seal it in a register, you have not taken a picture. You have taken the anchor. And every other memory that was tied to that anchor — the weight of a baby, the smell of the top of a head, the particular way a small hand closes around a finger — has to be rebuilt, and it has nothing left to tie itself to, and each time she reached for it, it came apart in her hands.

I think my mother tried to remember holding me and could not. I think she tried for eleven days. I think that is why she stood still for four seconds in a relay room at seven in the morning, because a woman was being asked to walk across a room and pick up a child she could no longer prove she had ever held.

***
We sat there until the shift change.

Rasya got up first. He put the field unit back in its case and closed the latches, one at a time, and then he stood with his hand flat on the case, looking at nothing.

“I will file for the bond,” he said. “A magistrate can order the register to produce the deposit. It will take eleven weeks and it will not be granted before the evacuation.”

“Then file it for after.”

“If there is an after.”

“There is going to be an after,” I said. “I have been promised an after by a machine.”

Danu had not moved from the end of the bench. He had his hand on the screen — the actual screen, the glass, flat, the way I had put my hand on it, and the frame was paused at 07:00:12, and under his palm my mother was turning away from a seven-year-old on a bench.

“I did not know about the bond,” he said.

“You knew about the seal.”

“I knew about the seal.”

“And you let me grow up thinking she had stopped loving me.”

“I let you grow up thinking she was dead,” Danu said. “There is a difference, and I have spent twenty-two years deciding which one would hurt you less, and I have been wrong every single day.”

I looked at the screen. I looked at the frame. I looked at the hand of a man who has never in his life apologised with a sentence, apologising with a palm.

And then I understood the thing that had been waiting for me all morning, the way a rock waits at the bottom of a corridor you have been walking down blind.

My mother did not stop holding me because she stopped loving me.

She stopped holding me because a register took the minute I was born, and paid her forty grams for it, and the forty grams went into a ledger, and the ledger went onto a shelf, and the shelf is the reason the lights in this corridor are on.
