He came back the next morning with two coffees, because apparently that is how the Biro apologises.

“You’re still here,” I said.

“I never left.”

“You left at ten last night.”

“I left the room,” Rasya said. “I sat in the corridor.”

“All night?”

“It’s a good corridor. There’s a bench.”

I took the coffee. It was station coffee, which tastes like the memory of coffee, and I drank half of it before I said anything else, because I have learned that the first thing you say in the morning sets the shape of the day.

“Tell me about the day,” I said.

“Which day?”

“The sixth of March, 2180. One day. You came in on the Rendang, you used a dead man’s name, and you stayed for twenty-two hours. I want it hour by hour.”

Rasya put his cup down.

“Hour by hour,” he said.

***
“I came in at six in the morning,” he said. “The Rendang docks at six. I had a bag with a change of clothes and a Biro field unit and eleven grams of credit in a broker’s escrow, and I had spent four months arranging the visit so that nobody in the Biro would know I was making it.”

“How do you arrange a visit so that nobody knows?”

“You go on leave,” he said. “You file the leave eleven weeks in advance. You take the cargo route instead of the passenger route. You pay for the ticket with a private account, which is legal, and you don’t tell your supervisor that you’re visiting the station at the centre of a file you’ve been pulling for nineteen years, which is also legal, and which is also the sort of thing that gets an auditor suspended if it comes out afterwards.”

“And the name?”

“R. Handoko. He was a colleague. He died in 2171 in a shuttle accident and his registry entry lapsed, which means the port system accepts the name and doesn’t flag it, because the port system is eleven years out of date and nobody has ever audited it.”

“You’re telling me the Biro’s own port system is out of date.”

“The Biro’s port system is a filing cabinet with a screen on it,” Rasya said. “Everything about the Biro is a filing cabinet with a screen on it. That’s the whole problem. That’s why we’re here.”

He drank his coffee.

“I came through the lock at six-twenty,” he said. “I stood in the corridor for two hours. Your quarters were the third door on the left, and there was a light under it, and at seven-fifteen somebody inside put a kettle on, and at seven-forty the door opened and a woman came out with wet hair and a jacket over her arm and walked past me without looking at me.”

“I remember that.”

“You don’t.”

“I remember a man in a grey coat in the corridor.”

“You remember a man in a grey coat,” he said. “There are four hundred men in grey coats on this station. You don’t remember me.”

I opened my mouth to argue and discovered that I could not.

***
“At nine,” he said, “I went to the relay room. You were on shift. You had your sleeves rolled up and a stylus behind your ear and you were arguing with the water plant about a coolant exchange that had been scheduled on top of a buoy window.”

“That happens every week.”

“It happened that week. You said —” he paused, and then he said it in a voice that was not his voice, a flat, fast, impatient voice that I have heard in recordings of myself and have never once recognised as mine — “I don’t care whose window it is, Boaz, the buoy doesn’t reschedule. The buoy has been turning for a hundred and forty years and it is not going to stop turning because your pipes are dirty.”

I put my cup down very carefully.

“That’s not what I said,” I said.

“It is exactly what you said.”

“I don’t talk like that.”

“You talk exactly like that,” Rasya said. “You talk like a woman who has been right too many times in a row and has stopped being polite about it.”

“I’m polite.”

“You’re efficient,” he said. “It reads as rudeness to people who are neither.”

I stared at him.

“You memorised it,” I said. “You memorised a sentence I said three years ago.”

“I didn’t memorise it,” he said. “I wrote it down. In the corridor, that evening, in a notebook, because I had spent nineteen years reading a file about a woman who might be alive, and there she was, alive, and rude, and arguing about pipes, and I wanted to have one true thing that wasn’t in the file.”

***
There are two clocks in this story and they have never once agreed.

At the same hour he was standing in the corridor outside my door, I was three hundred metres away with my hands inside a rack, and I know that because the rack log has a timestamp and the corridor camera has one too, and Vey lined them up for me afterwards without being asked. Meanwhile, across the ring, Danu was signing something. Elsewhere on the station, four hundred men in grey coats were doing what men in grey coats do. All of it at the same time, all of it recorded, none of it felt by me, because memory is not a recording. It is a selection made by somebody who was not consulted.

“At noon,” he said, “I ate in the mess. You came in with a tray and sat two tables over with a child.”

“Whose child?”

“I don’t know. A girl, about six. She had a toy rover and she was running it along the edge of the table and you were letting her, which nobody else was.”

I remembered the rover. I remembered it very clearly, actually — a cheap plastic thing with a wheel missing, and a child whose name I could not recall, and the way she had explained to me, at great length, that the rover was a survey vehicle and not a toy.

“She asked me a question,” I said slowly.

“She asked you why you sat by the buoy window every night.”

The room went quiet.

“And what did I say?”

Rasya looked at me.

“You said: some doors you keep open because they belong to you, and some you keep open because they belong to somebody else.”

I do not remember saying it.

I remember the rover. I remember the child’s hands, which were small and had a scab on the knuckle. I remember the taste of the soup, which was the station’s soup, which is a crime against soup. I remember the light coming in through the mess window at noon, which on Kemuning is a pale yellow and always looks like four in the afternoon.

I do not remember the sentence. I do not remember saying it. I do not remember the child asking the question, though I know, in the way you know the shape of a room you have just left, that she did ask it, and that I answered her.

“I don’t remember,” I said.

“I know.”

“Stop saying that.”

“It’s true.”

“I know it’s true, that’s not —” I stood up, and my chair went over, and neither of us picked it up. “You don’t get to sit there with your notebook and your nineteen years and tell me what I said. You don’t get to be the one who remembers.”

Rasya did not move.

“I know,” he said. “That’s the worst part of it. I know exactly.”

***
He left at four in the afternoon, twenty-two hours after he arrived.

“And what did you do in the last four hours?” I asked.

“I stood in the corridor outside the relay room,” he said, “and watched you work through the window.”

“For four hours.”

“For four hours.”

“That’s not normal.”

“No.”

“That’s —” I stopped. “What did I do?”

“You talked to the buoy,” he said. “Every twenty minutes or so, when you finished a diagnostic, you put your hand flat on the bench and looked out the window, and you said something to it. I couldn’t hear what. Just the shape of it. Like a person checking on a sleeping child.”

I sat down on the end of the bunk.

I do that. I have done it for nine years. I did not know anyone had ever seen me do it, and the fact that the only person who ever watched me do it was a stranger who had paid eleven grams for my file was, somehow, worse than everything else he had told me.

“Say the thing again,” I said.

“Which thing?”

“The thing I said. In the mess. To the child.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment. I watched him decide something, the way you can watch a man decide something when he has been awake for two days and has run out of the energy it takes to keep his face still.

Then he said, in that flat, fast voice that was mine and was not mine:

“Some doors you keep open because they belong to you, and some you keep open because they belong to somebody else.”

I sat with it. I turned it over the way you turn over a stone you have just picked up, looking for the wet side.

Nothing. Not a flicker. Not the shadow of a flicker. A child with a broken rover, a bowl of soup, four o’clock light at noon — and in the middle of it, a hole the exact shape of a sentence I had said with my own mouth.

He quoted me, word for word, from a conversation I do not remember.
