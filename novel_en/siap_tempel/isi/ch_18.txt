“How much?” I said.

“How much what?”

“How much have you lost. Total. Everything. Since you started.”

Rasya looked at me across the table in the administration office, which we had taken over at nine in the evening because it had a bigger screen than the relay room and because my father was asleep and I had stopped caring about his rules.

“You can’t measure it,” he said.

“You’re a Biro auditor. You measure things.”

“I measure things that have units.”

“Grams are a unit.”

“Grams are the unit of what you paid,” he said. “Not what you lost.”

***
We tried anyway. That is what people like us do.

The first thing we did was add up the payments. Rasya’s were in the notebook: eleven grams for the summary, forty for the first review fee, forty for the second, forty-four for the third, forty-four for the fourth. A hundred and seventy-nine grams.

The second thing we did was ask Vey to pull his registry account.

“I can pull it,” Vey said. “I would like it noted that pulling a personal memory account requires the owner’s consent, and that the owner is standing in the room, and that a station intelligence which watches two people add up a life on a table is a station intelligence that is going to be asked to keep quiet about it later.”

“Note it,” Rasya said.

The account was a single screen: a column of debits, each one with a date, an amount, a purpose, and a confirmation code. Nothing else. No inventory. No list of what had been taken.

“That’s it?” I said.

“That is the complete record,” Vey said. “The registry is not required to itemise the selection. The charter calls the selection incidental to the transaction, which is a phrase written by a lawyer who was paid by the gram.”

“So there’s no way to know.”

“There is a way to know,” Vey said. “It is simply not in the record. The registry cannot tell you what was taken, because nothing was transmitted: the no-communication result forbids it. A payment is not a message. A payment is a local act that a ledger in another room writes down. The only thing that travels is the number of grams. Everything else happens inside the payer, and no instrument on this station can read a person.”

“Wonderful,” I said. “So the whole thing is a black box.”

“The whole thing is a black box with a receipt,” Vey said.

***
The other ways took us four days.

The first was the notebook, which Rasya had been keeping for three years, and which was, in its way, an inventory of holes: sixty-one pages of facts about a girl, each one dated, each one annotated with a confidence mark. Certain. Probably. From the photograph. From the file. I think I remember this but I may be remembering the file.

The second was external records. Station logs. School registrations. Medical records. Photographs. Everything that a person leaves behind in the world, which is more than they think and less than they hope.

The third was Vey.

Vey had been the station intelligence at Tsiolkovsky from 2141 to 2170, and Vey had a visual archive — not the whole thing, because the consolidation had purged most of it, but the fragments that had been transferred to Kemuning in 2171: eleven thousand hours of corridor footage, compressed and indexed and mostly never watched by anyone.

“Eleven thousand hours,” I said.

“Of which perhaps four hundred contain the subject,” Vey said. “I have indexed them by face. It takes eleven minutes.”

“Everything takes eleven minutes.”

“It is a good number,” Vey said. “It is prime.”

***
We found her on the fourth day.

Not Ayu. We found Rasya, at eleven years old, in a corridor at Tsiolkovsky in the winter of 2160, walking behind a girl in a red coat.

The footage is bad. Corridor cameras in 2160 had the resolution of a smeared window, and the station lights in Ring C were the old sodium kind that turn everything the colour of weak tea. But you can see them: a boy with a satchel, walking the way boys walk when they are pretending not to follow someone, and ahead of him a girl in a red coat, shorter, her hair in two braids, her boots making no sound.

She turns around. She says something. The boy says something back. She laughs — you can see the shape of it, the head going back, the shoulders lifting — and then she takes his hand and pulls him along the corridor, and they go out of frame together.

Rasya sat in front of the screen with his hands on his knees and did not move for a long time.

“The red coat was hers,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I wrote in the book that I wasn’t sure.”

“I know.”

“I wrote is this real or is it from the photograph.”

“I know.”

He put his hand over his mouth. He did not cry. He sat with his hand over his mouth and watched the loop, which Vey had set to repeat without being asked, and after the fourth repetition he said:

“I don’t remember her laughing.”

The room was quiet.

“I know she’s laughing in the footage,” he said. “I can see it. I can see the shape of it. I have watched it eleven times. And there’s nothing —” he touched his chest — “there’s nothing here. No echo. No warmth. It’s like watching two children I have never met.”

“Rasya.”

“The boy is me. I know the satchel. I know the way he walks. And I am watching a stranger’s childhood.”

We kept going, because stopping was worse.

We found the school register, which listed a girl named Ayu Wardhana, aged nine, enrolled in the Ring C school in 2159. We found a medical record for a broken wrist in 2158 — hers — with a note from the treating officer: patient states she fell, brother states she was pushed, both accounts unreliable.

We found a photograph in the station’s civic archive: a family of four at a long table on Foundation Day, 2159. A man with Rasya’s jaw. A woman with Rasya’s hands. A boy. A girl in a red coat, laughing at something off-frame.

“That’s her,” Rasya said. “That’s — I know that face. I know that face. I don’t know what her voice sounds like but I know that face.”

“Write it down.”

“I have written it down. I wrote it down in 2180 and I wrote it down again in 2181 and I am writing it down now, and it doesn’t help, because writing it down doesn’t put it in me. It just puts it on a page. And the page is not a person.”

He closed the notebook.

“How much,” he said. “You asked. That’s the answer. A hundred and seventy-nine grams, and the number is meaningless, because the thing I lost doesn’t have a weight. It has a shape. And the shape is a girl laughing in a corridor, and I can watch it on a screen for the rest of my life and it will never be mine again.”

***
I did not sleep that night either, but for a different reason.

I was thinking about my own three years.

If Rasya’s losses could be partly reconstructed — if a notebook and a photograph and eleven thousand hours of corridor footage could give him back the shape of what he had lost, even if not the thing itself — then perhaps mine could be reconstructed too.

So at two in the morning I asked Vey to do for me what it had done for him.

“Index me,” I said.

“I have already indexed you,” Vey said. “I indexed you in 2176 when you arrived, because you were a new resident and my instructions require an identity baseline. I have re-indexed you every year since.”

“Show me the last three years.”

Vey showed me.

***
There were eleven thousand hours of footage of me.

I watched four of them, which was all I could bear, and I want to tell you what I saw, because it is the strangest thing that has ever happened to me, and I have had a strange month.

I saw myself in the relay room at three in the morning, sitting on the floor with my back against rack nine, staring at nothing.

I saw myself in the mess, laughing at something Boaz said, with my head tipped back and my hand over my mouth, and I have no memory of ever laughing like that in my life.

I saw myself in the corridor outside the dead room in February, standing with my forehead against the door, not moving, for eleven minutes.

I saw myself carrying a box to the dock at four in the morning in March, and the box was sealed, and I set it down on the cargo manifest line and walked away, and the camera lost me at the corner.

I do not remember the box. I do not remember the door. I do not remember the joke.

“Vey,” I said. “What was in the box?”

“I do not know,” Vey said. “The dock camera has a blind angle at the manifest line. It has had one since 2171. It is the only blind angle on this station, and I have never been able to have it repaired, because the man who can authorise the repair is the man who asked for the blind angle.”

“Who asked?”

“Administrator Prasetya,” Vey said. “In 2172. He said the dock crew needed somewhere to change their boots.”

I watched myself walk away from a sealed box at four in the morning, and I felt the floor of my life tilt.

What is missing does not feel missing. That was the cruellest part.
