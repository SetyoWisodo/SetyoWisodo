The supply ship was called Rendang, which is a joke that stopped being funny eleven years ago and has been kept alive by the sort of men who think repetition is a form of loyalty.

It comes every quarter. It is supposed to come on the second Tuesday, and it comes on the second Tuesday, and for eleven years the day before has been the same: the dock crew clears the approach, the quartermaster prints a manifest nobody reads, and the station spends an evening being polite to people who live somewhere else.

It came on Thursday. Four days early.

That is the thing about a ring: you cannot see it. You can see a wall, and a ceiling, and the far end of a corridor. You cannot see the shape of the thing you are standing inside, and after nine years the shape stops being available to you, the way you stop hearing your own name said in a queue.

The numbers are worse than the shape.

The Rendang is ninety metres of patched hull and eleven thousand tonnes of thrust assembly. It is, by the standards of the belt, a large object. Kemuning is one point two kilometres across, and the ring is a kilometre and a quarter of corridor that four thousand people share, and the Rendang is the size of the scratch a supply ship leaves on the paint. That is the scale of the thing we were living in. We had all agreed, without ever saying it, not to look at that comparison too long.

I heard it before I saw it. Kemuning is four thousand people welded into a ring, and a ship on approach finds every empty space in the structure and fills it with a noise like someone dragging a table across a floor above your head.

Kirana was already at the dock when I got there, standing on the yellow line with her tally slate against her chest like a shield.

“You’re early,” I said.

“You’re supposed to be in the dead room,” she said. “Everyone’s saying.”

“Everyone should find a hobby.”

“They’re saying Vey sealed a core and a Biro man came out of the last shuttle and there’s going to be an audit.”

“All true,” I said. “Only the last one hasn’t happened yet.”

“It’s happening at nine. In the common room. They pushed the tables to the walls.”

She said it with the particular relish of a nineteen-year-old delivering bad news she has not yet learned to be afraid of.

The gangway extended. The hull of the Rendang had been patched so many times it looked like a quilt, and somewhere in its belly there were three hundred kilos of food and medicine and machine parts and one crate of mail that had been in transit for nine months and would mostly be bills.

Kirana counted the crates out loud. She does this. She has a tally of every core she has touched — seventeen — and a tally of every cargo crate she has processed, and she will tell you both numbers if you make the mistake of asking how her day is going.

“Three,” she said, when the last of them went by. “They said three.”

“Three is what they said.”

“The manifest says four.”

I looked at the slate. It did say four. Three lines of cargo, one line blank except for a sequence number and a mass: sixty-one kilograms, sealed. Consignee: KEMUNING TRUST.

“Line four’s probably the water processor,” I said. “They always double-book the big pieces.”

“Sixty-one kilos isn’t the water processor. You put ‘water processor’ on line two. It says water processor on line two.”

“Then it’s a clerical thing.”

“Nothing on this ship is clerical,” Kirana said, with the terrible certainty of the young. “It came four days early. Shipments don’t come early. They get delayed, they get cancelled, they get lost in the belt for two years, but they don’t come early. It costs money to come early.”

She was right, and I knew it, and I did not want to say so, because agreeing with Kirana is like feeding a seagull.

“Who signed the manifest?” I asked.

“The master.”

“And the receiving officer?”

“There isn’t one yet.” She held up the slate. “Line four’s already struck through. Out before in.”

“Show me.”

She showed me. Someone had drawn a single line through the fourth entry in the paper register, and the timestamp on the strike was 05:58 — two minutes before the ship had docked.

“That’s not possible,” I said.

“I know.”

“You can’t log cargo out before it’s logged in.”

“I know.”

“Kirana, that’s a two-minute window while the ship is still on approach.”

“I know,” she said, and looked at me, and for the first time since I had known her she looked frightened. “What do I do?”

“Nothing,” I said. “You write it in your slate, and you keep your mouth shut, and you don’t tell anyone you showed me.”

“That’s not nothing.”

“No,” I said. “It isn’t.”

Across the dock, Danu was shaking hands with the Rendang’s master, and laughing at the end of it. Danu does the handshake-and-laugh well. He has a laugh he keeps for people from other stations, which is louder than his real one and starts half a second before the joke.

By nine, the common room had stopped being a common room.

Tables had been pushed to the walls. A screen stood at the centre, the kind that projects a flat white rectangle of light and makes everyone around it look ill. There were fourteen chairs. I counted them the way I count everything now.

Fourteen chairs, and fourteen people already in them. Mine was third from the left, next to the chair with the arm worn through, which is where the water plant supervisor sits, which meant the water plant supervisor had been told to sit somewhere else. Boaz looked at me when I came in. Boaz has been at Kemuning for thirty-one years and has the face of a man who has seen every kind of inconvenience and has decided to be polite to all of it.

“Sit,” Rasya said.

He was standing, which is what people do when they want the room to look up. His Biro coat was folded over the back of the empty chair at the head of the arrangement. On the screen was a list of dates.

“This is not a tribunal,” he said. “This is a reconciliation. The Biro has questions about a maintenance entry dated the ninth of October. Answering them is not an admission of anything. Not answering them delays the reconciliation, and the station pays for delay at the rate of nine grams a day while a core sits in the dead room.”

Fourteen people did the arithmetic at the same time. I could see it happen: the small shift of weight in the chairs, the hands going still. Nine grams a day is a small thing. Nine grams a day for a year is a child’s whole future in a jar.

“Now,” Rasya said. “The entry.”

Danu had come in behind me and taken the chair by the door, not the one at the head. I noticed that. He has always been able to look like the least important person in a room when it suits him.

“The entry was made at 02:14,” Rasya said. “It records an unscheduled opening of Buoy Seven, core extraction, inspection, and resealing, duration eleven minutes. It is signed D. Prasetya. Administrator, is that your signature?”

“It is.”

“Did you open the buoy at 02:14 on the ninth of October?”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“Scheduled diagnostic on the tether shroud. The buoy’s been complaining about temperature for eleven winters. I wanted a look at the shroud before I signed off on the replacement.”

“Was a relay engineer present?”

“No.”

“Does the procedure require one?”

“The procedure requires one for a core extraction,” Danu said, and his voice was easy, and I understood, with a cold drop in my stomach, that he had rehearsed this in the shower. “There was no core extraction. I opened the housing, inspected the shroud, and closed it.”

“And the reseal?”

“There was no reseal.”

Rasya put his stylus on the table. It made a sound like a small bone breaking.

“The core came out with a reseal on it,” he said. “Engineer Adisurya observed it, logged it, and it was confirmed under ash. The seal ring shows tool marks that do not match the factory pattern. Someone opened that core.”

“Then the log must be wrong,” Danu said.

“The log is signed with your name.”

“The log is a terminal fault.”

He said it flatly, without heat, the way you’d say the kettle was broken.

“A terminal fault,” Rasya repeated.

“The maintenance terminal in the relay room is a rebuilt unit. It has a known failure mode where it duplicates the previous entry and re-stamps it with the operator on shift. It’s in the maintenance bulletins. It’s been in them for two years.”

“Which bulletin?”

“I’d have to look it up.”

“Please do,” Rasya said. “After this.”

Nobody moved.

I want to explain what happened in that room, because it was not a dramatic thing, and it was worse than a dramatic thing. Fourteen people sat in a semicircle of chairs and looked at the floor, at their hands, at the screen, at the door. Fourteen people, and none of them looked at any of the others.

These were people I have eaten dinner with for nine years. Boaz, who taught me how to bleed a coolant line without losing a finger. Tima from the water plant, whose daughter is doing geology on the moon and calls her every second Sunday. Hendra, the medic, who has pulled two babies out of women on this station and once pulled a fishbone out of my throat at three in the morning while telling me about his mother’s garden.

Fourteen people, and the room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when everyone has the same thought and nobody wants to be the first to have it out loud.

Danu had just told them the record was a machine error. Fine. Machines fail. It happens.

But if the record was a machine error, then the core in the dead room had been resealed by nobody. And a core resealed by nobody is a core that somebody on this station touched without logging it, which means one of the fourteen people sitting in this semicircle did it, and the rest of them had just decided, in the space of a held breath, not to find out which.

“Then it’s a terminal fault,” said Boaz, to the floor.

“Terminal fault,” said Tima, to her hands.

“Machine error,” said Hendra, to the ceiling, and then, because he is a decent man, “for now.”

One by one the fourteen agreed, in the way people agree to things they do not believe, and it went around the room like a collection plate, and when it reached me I said nothing at all.

Rasya watched me say nothing. He did not write it down. That was the courtesy of a man who has already written it down.

“The reconciliation will resume at fourteen hundred,” he said. “Bring the terminal maintenance history. All of it. From the day it was installed.”

The chairs scraped. People left in a hurry that was careful not to look like a hurry.

Kirana caught me in the corridor, tally slate against her chest.

“The fourth crate,” she said. “It’s not on the digital manifest. Only the paper one. I checked twice.”

“Leave it.”

“Sixty-one kilos.”

“Kirana.”

“People don’t book sixty-one kilos and then not unload it,” she said. “They don’t book it and then log it out before they log it in. That’s not how the dock works. That’s not how anything works.”

I stopped walking.

“Say that again.”

“Line four’s been struck through,” she said. “Out before in. Like the ship was carrying it and then it decided it wasn’t carrying it after all.”
