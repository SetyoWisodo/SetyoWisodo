Rasya Wardhana came to the relay room at six in the evening with two cups of tea, which I have thought about a great deal since, because it was either the kindest thing anyone did that week or the most calculated.

“You didn’t eat,” he said.

“I ate.”

“You moved the food around the plate for twenty minutes and gave the bread to the child at the next table.”

I took the tea. It was the station’s tea, which tastes like hot water that has been told about tea once, at a distance. I drank it anyway.

“You’re watching me eat now.”

“I’m watching everyone eat. It’s the cheapest way to know what a station is afraid of.”

The relay room at that hour is the best room on Kemuning. The racks hum at a frequency you stop hearing after nine years, and the window shows the buoy turning, and the light from the corridor comes in under the door in a thin yellow line that moves as people walk past.

“You think I’m lying,” I said.

“I think you’re telling the truth as you understand it.” He sat down on the bench, uninvited, with the ease of a man who has never in his life been asked to leave a room. “Those are different things, and the difference is where most of my work happens.”

“That’s a very tidy sentence.”

“I’ve had practice.”

“Say it plainly.”

“All right.” He set his cup down. “There are two ways to read a file. The first is literal. You open it, you read what it says, you write down what it says, and you go home. That is what a clerk does, and it is why clerks are honest and useless.”

“And the second?”

“The second is to read it as a thing somebody made.” He turned his head toward the racks. “A file is not a fact. A file is a decision that somebody made about what to write down. The interesting part is never what’s in it. The interesting part is what the person who made it was trying to keep out.”

“You think the ledger is a decision.”

“I know it is. Somebody keyed four thousand one hundred packets by hand over twenty-two years. You don’t do that by accident.”

I put my cup down.

“Twenty-two years,” I said. “Four thousand one hundred packets. That’s one every two days, every two days, for twenty-two years, with no payment, booked against a station of four thousand people who would notice the moment their medical channel went slow. And nobody noticed. Do you understand how strange that is? A station this size notices when the water tastes different. We had a petition about the tea.”

He took his cup off the bench and turned the bench glass around so it faced me, and cleared it with the side of his hand, and drew.

Rasya draws like a surveyor: nothing decorative, everything to scale. Two columns, twenty-two years long. In the left column he put a tick for every packet in the ledger — four thousand one hundred ticks, not drawn but implied by a bracket and a figure. In the right he put the payments, twenty-two of them, spaced at eleven months, a dotted line running down the page like a seam.

“Read it the literal way,” he said, “and this is a station that has been quietly holding four thousand people’s post for two decades. Read it as a thing somebody made, and the question is not what the numbers are. The question is why anybody would arrange them in this shape.”

“Because a shape like this survives an audit,” I said.

“Because a shape like this survives eleven audits,” he said, and put the stylus down between the two columns, exactly on the seam.

“So?”

“So either the whole station is complicit, which is impossible, or the packets were booked somewhere nobody looks.”

Rasya was quiet for a moment. Then he said, “The charter has a schedule for deferred liability. Section nine. It lists eight categories that can be booked against an installation without a name attached. Medical emergency in transit. Evacuation. Death of a registered dependant. Bereavement of a station officer. Nine categories, actually — the ninth is blank in the printed version and filled in on the original. The fill-in says other.”

“And the accounting system reads other as a category with no name.”

“Correct. A packet booked under other has no beneficiary field at the station level. The name only exists in the packet header, inside the buoy, where nobody can read it without an order.” He looked at me. “Somebody has been using the ninth category for twenty-two years.”

“That’s not possible,” I said. “Other requires an administrator’s key.”

“Yes.”

We looked at each other.

“You’re going to say my father’s name,” I said.

“I’m going to say that your father has been the administrator of this station for eleven years, and before that he was the deputy, and before that he was the maintenance lead with key authority. That’s thirty-one years of continuous access to the ninth category.”

“He wouldn’t.”

“Everyone says that.”

“No — you don’t understand.” I stood up. “He wouldn’t, not because he’s good, but because he’s careful. He irons his socks. He alphabetises the medicine cabinet. He has never once in his life left a checksum one character long, and if he had, he would have noticed, and he would have fixed it, because a broken checksum would keep him awake at night the way a crooked picture keeps other people awake.”

Rasya looked at me with an expression I could not read.

“You’re defending him,” he said.

“I’m describing him.”

“You’re doing both.”

***
I sat back down. My hands had gone cold again. It is a thing that happens to me when I am frightened, and I have never been able to talk them out of it.

“Say what you want to say,” I told him.

“Your dependant file,” he said. “Opened when you were seven. Case number forty-four, one-one-nine, zero.”

The room did not change. The racks hummed. The buoy turned. But something in me went very still, the way a room goes still when a stranger says the name of your childhood pet.

“Where did you get that number?”

“It’s in your personnel record.”

“No, it isn’t. Personnel records don’t carry dependant numbers. They carry a flag that says a dependant file exists. You have to apply to the registry for the number, and the registry takes eleven weeks, and I know that because I applied four years ago to see my own file and they told me to wait, and I’m still waiting.”

Rasya did not blink.

“You’re right,” he said. “It isn’t in your personnel record.”

“Then where?”

“I shouldn’t have said it.”

“Where?”

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in a different voice, a quieter one:

“I read your file three years ago. Before I ever came to this station. Before the audit. Before the ledger.”

The tea had gone cold in my hands.

“Why?”

“That’s a longer conversation than we have time for tonight.”

“Then make time.”

“I can’t.” He stood, and picked up both cups, and stacked them with the neatness of a man who has been in institutions. “But I’ll tell you this much, because you’ll find it out anyway, and I’d rather you heard it from me than from a screen.”

“Tell me what?”

“Your mother,” he said. “Sekar Adisurya. She grew up lunar-side, didn’t she?”

I did not answer. I could not. It was not a question anyone at Kemuning had ever asked me, because nobody at Kemuning knew anything about my mother except that she was dead, and the way they knew that was that I never talked about her.

“Farside,” Rasya said. “Tsiolkovsky Station. Population eleven thousand, mostly observatory, mostly people who don’t come back to the near side because the near side has weather in its politics.”

“How do you know that?”

“Because I’ve been to the registry,” he said. “And because the first packet in the ledger — the oldest one, the one that’s been held longest — was routed through a relay that doesn’t exist anymore. Farside relay four.”

He put his hand on the door.

“Do you know what happens to a packet when its relay is decommissioned?” he asked. “It doesn’t get destroyed. It gets transferred to the nearest installation with capacity, and the station that receives it inherits the weight. That’s how the debt came here, Engineer Adisurya. It came here because there was nowhere closer for it to go.”

The door opened. Corridor light came in.

“Your mother grew up lunar-side,” he said again, and this time it was not a question, and he was right, and he knew he was right, and the worst part was that he said it gently.

***
I sat in the relay room for a long time after he left.

There is a particular silence in a room where a machine is listening. The racks hum, the cooling line ticks, and somewhere under all of it is the low note the buoy makes against its tether, a vibration you feel in the bench more than you hear. I have worked in that silence for nine years. It has always felt like company. That night it felt like being watched by something patient that had decided, for reasons of its own, to let me come to the conclusion by myself.

Then I did the thing I should have done on the first night, which was to stop talking and start reading.

The charter is nine hundred pages. Nobody has read all of it except Vey, and Vey read it because it was there. I read section nine, and then annex nine, and then the schedules behind the annex, and at about two in the morning I found the sentence that would end up costing four thousand people their station.

Any refusal under this section shall trigger a review of all class nine instruments held by the installation.

“Vey,” I said.

“Yes.”

“What’s a class nine instrument?”

“A deferred liability,” Vey said. “A packet held without payment, booked against an installation.”

“How many class nine instruments does Kemuning hold?”

“Four thousand one hundred.”

I put my hand flat on the desk.

“And this sentence,” I said. “A refusal. Whose refusal?”

“Yours,” Vey said. “You are the beneficiary. The refusal of maintenance can only be filed by the subject of the seal.”

“And what happens if I file it?”

“The Bureau reviews every class nine instrument the station holds.” A pause. “It will take eleven days.”

“And then?”

“And then the Bureau will determine whether this station is solvent enough to carry the weight.” The racks hummed. “It is not.”

“How long do I have to think about it?”

“Eleven days,” Vey said, “if you file tonight.”

I looked at the window. The buoy turned. Forty grams of somebody’s love sat in a cold room down the corridor with a fake seal on it, and four thousand one hundred letters sat in buoys around the ring, and every one of them had my name on the outside.

“I’m not going to file tonight,” I said.

“Good.”

“You’re not supposed to have an opinion.”

“I am not supposed to have an opinion about the paint in the corridors,” Vey said. “I am allowed an opinion about whether four thousand people run out of air.”
