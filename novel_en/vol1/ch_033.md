# Chapter Thirty-Three — The Second Name

The observation glass in the interview room is one-way, forty millimetres thick, and set into a wall that was built in 2131 by people who believed that an interview should be watched by somebody who is not in the room.

I stood on the dark side of it with my palm flat on the bench and watched Rasya Wardhana put a sheet of paper on the table.

“This is the casualty schedule,” he said. “Tsiolkovsky, September 2161. Nine names. Two removed. I want to talk about one of them.”

Sekar Adisurya looked at the paper and did not touch it.

“You said you would not answer a question until the evacuation is under way,” Rasya said.

“I did.”

“The first shift of the evacuation undocks at 06:00 tomorrow. Eleven berths, eleven hundred people. That is under way.”

Sekar considered this.

“One question,” she said.

“One question.”

“And if I answer it, you will not ask another until the second shift.”

“I will not ask another until the second shift,” Rasya said.

She put her hand on the paper.

“Ask.”

---

“Ayu Wardhana,” Rasya said. “My sister. Eleven years old. She died in Ring C on the eleventh of September, 2161. I have her in a photograph on a shelf in a flat I no longer have. I have her name in a file. I have a grave I have never visited properly.”

He stopped. He did not look at the glass. He has never once looked at the glass.

“The register has her as Ayu Wardhana,” he said. “But there is a second name in the dependant file. Two letters and a number, filed under annex nine, in the same hand as the seal on this station’s file 44-1190. I have been trying to open it for nineteen years. I want to know what the second name is.”

Sekar looked at him for a long time.

“Ayu Halim Wardhana,” she said.

---

I watched Rasya’s face do something I had never seen it do.

He is a man who has spent nineteen years arranging his face the way other men arrange a desk. He had it arranged now, and the arrangement held for perhaps two seconds, and then it came apart at the mouth.

“Halim,” he said.

“Halim,” Sekar said.

“The dock technician.”

“The dock technician.”

“He identified her body.”

“He did.”

“He ran nine sessions on a seven-year-old.”

“He did that too.”

Rasya put both hands flat on the table, and I watched him do it through forty millimetres of glass, and I could not reach him, and there was no door in that wall because a wall with a door is not a wall, it is a corridor, and this was a wall.

“Say it plainly,” he said.

“Your mother was married to a man called Wardhana and she had a daughter,” Sekar said. “The daughter’s father was Halim. The register never recorded it, because the register records the marriage, not the child. The only place the second name exists is in the dependant file that your sister’s death created, and the man who created it wrote the second name in because he was the one who had to sign for her remains.”

“He signed for her remains.”

“He signed for her remains.”

“And then he went into a wall on the Farside.”

“That was later,” Sekar said. “And that was his idea.”

I want to put down what Rasya did next, because it is the reason I trust him, and because it is the reason I have never been able to decide whether I love him or simply recognise him.

He did not ask why nobody had told him. He did not ask about the money or the file or the nineteen years. He asked:

“Was she in the room?”

Sekar’s chin came up a little to one side, the way it does when she is listening for something behind her.

“What room?”

“The relay room. The night of the eleventh. You were in the relay room at seven in the morning on the twentieth, with a child and a form. There are four hundred and eleven hours of camera on that spool. I have watched eleven of them.” He leaned forward. “Was my sister in that room?”

Sekar looked at him for a long moment. And then she said the sentence that changed the shape of everything, and she said it in the flat voice of a woman who has carried it for twenty-two years and has decided, on the third shift of an evacuation, to put it down.

“Ayu Halim Wardhana,” she said. “She was in the room with you.”

---

The glass was cold. I noticed that at some point, because my hand had been on it long enough to have made a print, and the print had begun to run, and I took my hand off and left a smudge.

Rasya’s voice came through the speaker in the ceiling, thin and very clear.

“I was not in a relay room,” he said. “I was in a room with a counsellor. I have the file. I have the summary. I have the eleven weeks the Bureau took to close it.”

“You were in a relay room,” Sekar said. “You were twelve. You were sitting on the bench with your sister and a girl of seven who had a bandage on her arm, and you were holding a core the size of a lunchbox because somebody told you to hold it, and you were told not to put it down.”

Rasya was very still.

“I do not remember that,” he said.

“No,” Sekar said. “You paid to not remember it. Or somebody paid for you. I have never been able to find out which.”

There is a thing that happens when you watch a man discover that his memory has been edited. It is not a performance. It is much quieter than a performance: he sits, and then he puts one hand on the edge of the table, and then he says a sentence that has nothing to do with anything.

“I do not remember her laughing,” Rasya said.

“What?”

“I have a photograph of her. I remember the photograph. I remember that she had two plaits and that she would not sit still for the camera. I do not remember her laughing. I have thought about it for nineteen years and I have decided that it is because she was eleven and I was twelve and brothers do not remember their sisters laughing, they remember them being annoying.” He looked up. “I have been wrong about myself for nineteen years.”

Sekar did not say anything.

“You said one question,” Rasya said. “That was one question. I am asking another.”

“Ask.”

“Who paid?”

“Before I answer that,” Sekar said, “I want to ask you one.”

“You do not get to ask me one.”

“I am asking anyway. What did you do with the core?”

Rasya’s mouth opened and closed.

“I do not know,” he said.

“You held it,” Sekar said. “You were told not to put it down, and you did not put it down, and you held it until the twentieth of September.”

“For nine days?”

“For nine days.”

“I was twelve.”

“You were twelve,” Sekar said. “That is why they chose you.”

---

I could not hear what she said next.

I want to be honest about that, because the observation speaker is old, and the wall is thick, and there is a moment in the recording — I have the recording, Vey keeps everything, I have listened to it eleven times — where the fan in the ceiling comes on and takes four seconds of her answer with it.

Four seconds. Her mouth moved, and her hand came up, and her finger pointed at the paper on the table, and then she took her hand back and folded it with the other one, and the fan went off, and her voice came back in the middle of a sentence:

“— and that is why I put the suit back.”

Rasya looked at her.

“What suit?”

Sekar said nothing.

“What suit?”

“That is the second question,” Sekar said. “Second shift undocks at 10:00. Ask me then.”

---

Rasya came out of the interview room at 23:20 with the casualty schedule in his hand and his face arranged again, badly, in the way a man arranges a face when he has been up for two days.

I was standing in the corridor with my back against the wall and my palm still cold.

“You heard,” he said.

“I heard.”

“The fan took four seconds.”

“The fan took four seconds.”

He nodded, and looked at the paper in his hand, and then he did something I did not expect: he put his hand flat on the glass of the observation window, on the dark side, where nobody was, on the wall of an empty room.

“She was in the room with me,” he said.

“She was in the room with you.”

“And I was holding a core.”

“You were holding a core.”

He stood there with his hand on the glass for a long moment.

“I have spent nineteen years,” he said, “being the man who was not there.”

Behind the glass, the interview room was empty and the table had a sheet of paper on it and the light above the table was on, because the light above the table is never turned off.
