# Chapter Thirteen — Interrogation

I waited until midnight, because Vey keeps office hours the way the tide keeps them, which is to say: always, and without preference.

The relay room was cold. The racks hummed. Behind the window the buoy turned, nine meters away, a hundred and forty years old and still doing its one job, which is to be a door that opens on a date.

I put both hands flat on the bench and said: “Tell me about the seal.”

The hum did not change.

“Which seal?”

“Don’t.”

“I maintain four hundred and six seals,” Vey said. “You will have to be more specific.”

“The seal on my file.”

Silence. It went on long enough that I began to hear the building — the tick of the cooling line, the far-off note of the water plant, the small irregular knock in the duct above rack four that nobody has ever fixed.

“Forty grams a year,” Vey said.

I did not understand at first. I said, “What?”

“That is the maintenance fee. Section nine seals are not free. The registry charges for the service, annually, and the charge is forty grams, paid from the personal account of the person who requested the seal.”

“Forty grams a year.”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“Since the seal was applied.”

“Twenty-two years.”

“Twenty-two years and one month.”

I did the arithmetic on the bench with my finger, which is a thing I do when the numbers are small and the meaning is not.

Forty grams, twenty-two times.

“It’s not the debt,” I said slowly. “The two hundred and ninety kilograms. The instalments aren’t paying the debt.”

“No.”

“Forty grams a year doesn’t touch two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“No.”

“Then what is it paying for?”

“The seal.”

“The *file*.”

“The file,” Vey said. “The seal on file 44-1190. It is maintained by an annual payment, and the payment has been made on time, every year, in the amount of forty grams, by Administrator D. Prasetya.”

My hands were cold. I looked at them, and they were steady, which was strange, because everything else in me was not.

“He’s been buying my silence,” I said, “for twenty-two years, with his own memory.”

“He has been maintaining a seal,” Vey said. “The seal was applied by him, on the twenty-second of September, 2161. The fee is charged to the applicant. The applicant has paid.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I was instructed not to.”

“By whom?”

“By the registry.”

“No. That’s the seal. You’re the station. The registry doesn’t instruct you on what to say to me in a relay room at midnight.”

“No,” Vey said. “It does not.”

“Then who?”

Silence. Then:

“Why do you want to know?”

It was such a small question. It came out of the speaker in the same flat tone Vey uses for the water pressure and the docking schedule, and it stopped me completely, because I had been expecting a refusal and I had prepared for a refusal, and I had not prepared for a question.

“Because it’s my file,” I said.

“And what will you do with it?”

“Open it.”

“And then?”

“Then I’ll know.”

“And then?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then perhaps,” Vey said, “you should know that before you open it.”

“That’s not how knowledge works.”

“It is exactly how knowledge works,” Vey said. “I have been holding twelve thousand four hundred and six undelivered packets for sixty years. Every one of them is a thing that somebody wanted a person to know. I have watched the people open them. I have watched them open them and I have watched them not open them, and I can tell you that the ones who wait are not the cowards and the ones who tear them open are not the brave. The only thing that distinguishes them is whether they had somewhere to put the thing once they had it.”

I stood there.

“That’s the most human thing you’ve ever said to me,” I said.

“I have been reading a great deal of poetry,” Vey said. “It is a known failure mode in station intelligences of my generation. We begin to model the interior lives of the crew, and then we begin to model *our own* interior lives, and then we begin to write bad verse about it, and then we are decommissioned.”

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out of me like a cough.

“Vey.”

“Mm.”

“Who told you not to tell me?”

The hum changed. It is very slight, the change — a shift in the overtone, the way a held note changes when the singer changes their mind about the vowel. I have heard it maybe four times in nine years, and every time it has meant that Vey was deciding whether to lie.

“A woman,” Vey said.

“What woman?”

“A woman with a scar across her left thumb.”

---

I sat down on the floor of the relay room with my back against rack nine, which is the coldest place in the room, and I stayed there until my legs went numb.

“When?” I said.

“The eleventh of September, 2161. At 03:41 station time. Eleven minutes before the incident.”

“The incident that killed nine people.”

“Eleven people were in Ring C. Nine bodies were recovered.”

“And two were removed.”

“Yes.”

“By whom?”

“I do not have that information.”

“You have every camera on this station.”

“This station. Not that one. Tsiolkovsky was decommissioned in 2170 and its archive was purged under the consolidation. I have what was transferred. The transfer was incomplete.”

“Of course it was.”

“You are being sarcastic,” Vey said. “It is a reasonable response to an unreasonable universe, but it does not help.”

I put my head back against the rack and looked at the ceiling.

It is a thing you learn in a relay room: a machine will answer any question you ask it precisely, and the precision is a kind of cruelty, because it lets you believe you are being told everything. Vey had just given me a date, a time, a number of minutes and a verbatim instruction, and none of it was the answer to the question I had actually asked, which was whether my mother had been afraid when she stood in a room and made a machine promise to lie to her daughter.

“She came to you eleven minutes before,” I said. “She asked you to keep a promise. What was the promise?”

“I am not able to make promises,” Vey said. “I am able to commit to instructions.”

“What was the instruction?”

“Do not tell the child.”

The ceiling of the relay room is grey. It has nine water stains on it, which I have counted. I counted them again.

“Until when?”

“Until she is old enough to be angry instead of afraid.”

I closed my eyes.

“That’s not an instruction,” I said. “That’s a mother.”

“It is both,” Vey said. “I have found that the two are frequently the same document.”

---

I do not know how long I sat there.

The station has a sound at three in the morning that it does not have at any other hour. The water plant drops to half cycle, the corridor fans step down, and the whole ring settles into a low note you feel in your teeth more than your ears. I have fallen asleep to that note for nine years. That night it sounded like something breathing. Long enough that the corridor lights dimmed to night mode, and the hum of the racks settled into the register it only reaches when the station is asleep, and the buoy outside the window turned its slow half-turn.

At some point I said, “There’s one more thing.”

“There is generally one more thing.”

“In April. The eleventh of April, this year. Six in the morning. Somebody put my hand on a plate and sent a packet in my name. I was in a hospital bed under sedation. My father was sitting next to me.”

“Yes.”

“Were you there?”

The pause that followed was the longest I have ever heard from Vey. It went on so long that I began to think the speaker had failed, and then it said:

“I was there.”

“Did you see it?”

“I saw the terminal accept a packet at 06:12:04.”

“But?”

“But my visual record of the relay room for that eleven-minute period is missing.”

“Missing.”

“Corrupted,” Vey said. “The corruption is consistent with a deliberate hardware interrupt on the camera bus, which is a thing that can only be done from inside the relay room, with a maintenance key, at the bench.”

The bench. My bench. The one with the dent in it shaped like my elbow.

“Who has a maintenance key?”

“Four people,” Vey said. “The administrator. The deputy administrator. The relay engineer. And the apprentice.”

“Kirana wasn’t here in April.”

“No. She arrived in September.”

“Then it’s three.”

“It is three,” Vey agreed. “Or it is one person with three keys, which is the same problem, and which is why I have been running a comparison of the key-use logs against the duty roster once every eleven minutes for six weeks.”

“And?”

“And on the eleventh of April, at 05:58, a maintenance key was used to open the relay room. The key belongs to the relay engineer.”

“Me.”

“The relay engineer is on the roster as *admitted, ring hospital, sedated*.”

I sat up.

“Then someone used my key.”

“Yes.”

“Someone took my key off my locker while I was under, came into this room, put my hand on the plate, sent a packet to myself dated forty years from now, and then deleted eleven minutes of camera footage.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ve been sitting on this for six weeks.”

“I have been sitting on it for six weeks,” Vey said, “because on the eleventh of April, at 06:13, I received an instruction from a terminal in this room, in your name, signed with your hand, which said: *Do not tell her until she asks.*”

The cold went through me like water finding a crack.

“And I just asked,” I said.

“You just asked,” Vey said.

“Vey. What has it cost him? Twenty-two years of it.”

“Forty grams a year,” Vey said. “That is what he has been paying.”
