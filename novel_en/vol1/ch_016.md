# Chapter Sixteen — Residue

I caught him in the corridor outside the relay room at seven, which is the hour when the night shift goes to bed and the day shift has not yet had coffee, and the corridors of Kemuning belong to nobody.

“This is your coat.”

“Yes.”

“Take it.”

He took it. I watched him do it — the same careful hands, the same way of holding a thing as if it might break.

“Now check the laundry tag.”

He turned the collar over. The tag is small and grey and sewn inside the neck, and it carries the station code and the cycle number that every garment on Kemuning carries, because a station of four thousand people cannot afford to lose a jacket.

“KM-eleven,” he read. “Cycle four four seven one.”

“April.”

He looked up.

“April,” I said, “is when cycle four four seven one ran. It’s the first wash cycle after the coolant accident in the ring hospital, because they wash everything that comes out of the hospital on its own cycle, and I know that because I was in the hospital, and my clothes went through cycle four four seven one.”

Rasya stood very still.

“Your coat was washed on this station in April,” I said. “Your coat, which you brought from Surabaya, which has a Biro patch on the sleeve, was in a laundry drum on Kemuning in April, while I was under sedation eleven meters away.”

“Yes.”

“Yes?”

“Yes,” he said. “I was here in April.”

---

The corridor was empty. Somewhere behind the wall, the water plant began its morning cycle, and the pipes knocked twice, the way they always do.

“You told me you came once,” I said. “One day. Three years ago. You told me hour by hour. You stood in a corridor for two hours and you ate in the mess and you watched me through a window, and then you left, and you never came back.”

“I did tell you that.”

“You lied.”

“I omitted,” Rasya said. “It’s a different thing and I’m not proud of either.”

“Say it plainly.”

He put the coat over his arm. His face had gone the way faces go when a man has decided to stop defending himself.

“On the eleventh of April,” he said, “I was on Kemuning. I came in on the *Rendang* under the name R. Handoko, the same name, because the port system is still eleven years out of date and it is still funny to me that it works. I was met at the lock by your father.”

“My father.”

“He sent for me. A Biro request, urgent, signed by the administrator of Kemuning. It said there had been a records incident and the Biro was required to send a custodian. The Biro sent me because I was the only auditor in the sector who had ever opened a file on this station, and because the request named me.”

“The request named you.”

“By my own name. Not Handoko.”

I put my hand on the wall.

“Go on.”

“I arrived at six in the morning on the eleventh. Your father met me at the lock and walked me to the ring hospital. You were in the second bed on the left, with a burn on your arm and a drip in your hand, and you were not awake. He stood at the end of the bed and looked at you for a while, and then he said: *She has to send something.*”

“He said that.”

“He said: *She has to send something, and she can’t know she sent it.*”

The pipes knocked again. I could hear my own breathing.

“And you helped him,” I said.

“I helped him.”

“You put my hand on a plate.”

“I put your hand on a plate,” Rasya said. “I held your wrist. Your father held the door. We did not say a word for eleven minutes, and then it was done, and I carried you back to the bed, and I put my coat over the chair because the room was cold, and I left the station at four in the afternoon and I have not slept properly since.”

---

I hit him.

I want to be clear that it was not a film hit. It was a bad, graceless, open-handed swing with my whole arm, and it caught him on the shoulder because he moved, and it hurt my hand more than it hurt him.

He did not step back.

“Do it again,” he said.

“Don’t you dare.”

“I’m not being noble. I’m telling you that I’ll stand here until you’re finished.”

“I’m finished.”

“You’re not.”

I was not. I stood in the corridor with my hand stinging and my eyes hot and the whole of my life rearranged like furniture after a flood, and I thought: *he was in the room. He held my wrist. He knows what I said, and he knows what was taken, and he was there.*

“What did I send?” I said.

“I don’t know. The contents are destroyed at transmission. Nobody knows what a packet says, not even the sender. That’s the whole —”

“Don’t you dare lecture me about the whole point of the relay.”

“All right.”

“What did it weigh?”

“Forty grams.”

“Forty grams of what?”

“Class personal,” Rasya said. “The header says personal. That’s all it says.”

I leaned against the wall.

“I was sedated,” I said. “I couldn’t consent. I couldn’t refuse. My father held the door and a stranger held my wrist and you took forty grams out of me while I was asleep, and you sent it to me, forty years from now, and you want me to believe that you did it because he asked.”

“No,” Rasya said. “I want you to believe that I did it because he asked, and that I have been paying for it ever since.”

---

“Paying.”

“The registry charges a fee to maintain a section nine seal,” he said. “Forty grams a year. Your father pays it. He has paid it for twenty-two years.”

“I know.”

He looked up sharply.

“You know.”

“Vey told me. Last night.” I said it flatly, because flat was all I had. “Forty grams a year, twenty-two years, eight hundred and eighty grams out of his own life so that a file stays shut. And the payments came from his personal account, not the station’s, because the registry does not accept station funds for a personal seal.”

“Then you know there’s a second instrument,” Rasya said. “A review fee. Anyone can pay it — it’s open, it’s in the charter, it’s the mechanism by which a sealed file can be brought before a magistrate. Forty grams a year, and at the end of seven consecutive years the registry is obliged to review the seal.”

I stared at him.

“You’ve been paying the review fee.”

“Since 2180.”

“For three years.”

“For three years and eight months.”

“That’s a hundred and forty grams.”

“A hundred and forty-six,” he said. “It went up in 2182. Indexation.”

“You’ve been paying a hundred and forty-six grams of your own memory to get my file reviewed.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He looked at me, and for the first time since the doorway on the twenty-ninth of October, he looked tired. Not sleepy. Tired the way a building is tired.

“Because I was there,” he said. “Because I held your wrist. Because your father is not the only person who has been paying for that morning, and because in three years and eight months I have not once been able to look in a mirror and see a man who did the right thing.”

“And what did it cost you?”

He did not answer.

“What did it cost you?”

“I don’t know,” he said. “That’s the thing about the tariff. You don’t get a receipt. You pay in grams and the system takes what it takes, and the only way you know what it took is by noticing a hole.” He looked at the coat on his arm. “I’ve been noticing holes for three years.”

“Such as?”

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

“Not yet,” he said.

---

I do not know why I did what I did next.

The corridor ran the length of the ring at that hour with nobody in it, and the lamps were still on night setting, and somewhere below the deck the water plant was pushing its morning load through pipes that had been installed before my mother was born. It was the most ordinary corridor in the nine systems. That is what I remember most clearly about the worst morning of my life: how ordinary the floor looked. I have thought about it for a long time, and the most honest thing I can say is that I was tired and he was there and my hand hurt and the corridor was empty.

I took his hand.

Not to shake it. Not to hurt him. I put my fingers around his fingers the way you would take the hand of a person who is about to step off a ledge, and I held it, and I waited for the taste.

Nothing came.

No corridor. No kitchen. No grief. His hand was warm and dry and had a callus on the second finger where a stylus sits, and it tasted of nothing at all — not cold, not empty, just the flat clean nothing of a surface that has never been read.

I let go.

“What?” he said.

“Nothing.” I stepped back. “It’s nothing. I have to go.”

“Nara.”

“I have to go.”

I got three steps down the corridor before he said it, and he did not say it loudly, but the corridor was empty and the pipes had stopped knocking and the whole station seemed to be holding its breath.

“I paid for you,” he said, “three years before I met you.”
