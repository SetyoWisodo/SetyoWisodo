# Chapter Twenty — Four Thousand People

My father did not shout. That was the worst of it. He had not shouted since I was nine, and the silence had done more work than shouting ever could.

He was in the administration office with the evacuation notice on the screen and a cup of tea at his elbow that had gone cold, and he did not get up when I came in.

“Sit down,” he said.

“No.”

“Then stand.” He turned the screen toward me. “Four thousand and eleven people. Three hundred and six children. Ninety-one medical cases, of whom eleven cannot be moved without a pressure berth. The *Rendang* holds forty. The next ship is the *Cendana*, which is an ore hauler with a passenger module and holds a hundred and twenty, and it is nine weeks out. The Bureau has allocated three evacuation ships from the reserve fleet. Do you know what a reserve fleet ship is?”

“No.”

“It is a ship that was decommissioned eleven years ago and kept in a yard in case of an emergency exactly like this one. It has no crew. It has no life support for four thousand people. It has bunks for eight hundred and a water recycler that was condemned in 2179.”

“They can’t send a condemned ship.”

“They can send whatever they like,” Danu said. “The charter says the Bureau shall provide transport. It does not say the transport shall be adequate. Adequacy is a standard of care, and a standard of care is a thing you argue about in a hearing, and a hearing takes four years, and four thousand people cannot hold their breath for four years.”

---

He stood up and went to the window, which looks at the wall of the next ring.

“Eleven days,” he said. “That’s the review period. In eleven days the Bureau will determine whether this station is solvent. If it determines that it is not, the charter is suspended, and the station enters receivership, and the receivership is administered by the network, and the network’s first act will be to cancel the supply contracts because a station without a charter cannot hold a contract.”

“So they cancel.”

“They cancel the water contract. They cancel the air contract. They cancel the medical resupply, which is already on a manifest in the belt, and the manifest is cancelled at the point of cancellation, which means the supplies are sold to somebody else and we go on the queue for the next allocation, and the next allocation is in eleven months.”

“Eleven months.”

“Eleven months,” he said. “The recyclers will run for four. The medical stock will run for seven. The children will start getting sick in the ninth week, because children always get sick first on a station with a failing recycler, and by the eleventh month we will be burying them in the ring that used to be the garden.”

I had to sit down. I sat down.

Four thousand and eleven people. I have lived among that number for nine years and I have never once been able to hold it in my head. It is not a crowd; a station this size never gathers more than two hundred in one place, and those two hundred are the same two hundred you see every week. The number lives in the manifests. Three hundred and six children means nine school classes and a waiting list for the crèche. Ninety-one medical cases means a cupboard in the ring hospital with ninety-one names on the door and a stock list that is ordered eleven months ahead. Eleven pressure berths means eleven, and the doctor who assigns them will have to look at ninety-one files and choose eleven, and will do it in an afternoon, and will remember it for the rest of her life.

The arithmetic of a station is not the arithmetic of a city. A city can absorb a decision; a station is a closed loop, and every number in it is a person you have queued behind for bread.


“How many die?” I said.

“That depends on the evacuation.”

“Assume the evacuation works.”

“Assume the evacuation works,” he said, “and assume the three ships arrive on time, and assume the receiving stations accept the allocation, which they will not, because the receiving allocation is a negotiation between station administrators and the network, and the network has no incentive to hurry a station that has just defaulted. Assume all of that works and the answer is between sixty and two hundred.”

“Out of four thousand.”

“Out of four thousand and eleven,” he said. “Mostly the medical cases, because a pressure berth is a limited resource and a ship with a condemned recycler has eleven of them, and there are ninety-one medical cases, and the triage will be done by a doctor on a dock with a clipboard.”

He turned around.

“Now assume the evacuation does not work,” he said. “Assume the ships are late, because reserve fleet ships are always late. Assume one of them fails its certification, because a ship condemned in 2179 does not pass a certification in 2183. Assume the receiving allocation takes four months instead of four weeks. In that case the number is not sixty. The number is a fraction. A third. Half.”

“Two thousand people.”

“Two thousand people,” he said. “And the ones who die will not die of anything dramatic. They will die of a water recycler that was not serviced, and a stock of antibiotics that was not delivered, and a decision made in an office eleven systems away by a clerk who has never been on a station and who will write *decapacitation, natural causes* on a form.”


I sat with my hands in my lap.

The office was warm in the way that offices on stations are warm: too warm, dried out, the air recycled so many times it tastes faintly of the machine that made it. Behind me the cooling line ticked. Somewhere two rings over the school was letting out, and the noise of forty children in a corridor came through the ductwork the way it always does, thin and bright and impossible to be sad through.

“You’re asking me to withdraw the refusal,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You’re asking me to go back to the portal and file a withdrawal, and let the seal stand, and let the file stay closed, and let the payments resume, and pretend that none of this happened.”

“Yes.”

“And what about the packets?”

“The packets stay.”

“Two hundred and ninety kilograms of debt that this station cannot pay, addressed to me, signed with my hand, dated eighteen years in the future.”

“The packets stay,” Danu said, “and the station stays, and the people stay, and in eleven days the review will find what it always finds, which is that a station with four thousand people and a functioning recycler is worth more to the network than a station in receivership, and the review will be closed, and we will go on.”

“And the file?”

“The file stays sealed.”

“And I stay ignorant.”

“You stay alive,” he said, and there was something in his voice then that I had not heard since I was seven years old, and it took me a moment to recognise it, because he had spent twenty-two years making sure I never heard it again. It was fear. Plain, undecorated, human fear.

“You don’t know that,” I said.

“I know it.”

“You don’t know what’s in the file.”

“I know exactly what’s in the file,” Danu said. “I put it there.”

---

The room was very quiet.

“Then tell me,” I said.

“No.”

“Tell me and I’ll withdraw the refusal. Right now. You tell me what happened in the nine days, and what’s in the file, and why my signature is on four thousand packets, and I will go to the portal and withdraw, and we will never speak of it again.”

He looked at me for a long moment.

“You would,” he said. “You’d do it. You’d withdraw the refusal and then you’d spend the rest of your life pulling at the thread, and one day you’d pull it in front of somebody who matters, and it wouldn’t be me who paid.”

“That’s not fair.”

“No.”

“I’m not a child.”

“You are my child,” he said. “That is not the same thing as being a child, and it never has been, and it is the only argument I have left.”

---

He came around the desk and stood in front of me, and for a moment I thought he was going to put his hand on my shoulder.

There is a thing that happens to a man who has spent thirty years being the calmest person in every room. His face stops moving. Not because he feels nothing, but because he has practised feeling nothing in front of other people for so long that the muscles have learned the trick and cannot be told to stop. My father’s face did that then. He stood with his hands at his sides and his jaw set and he delivered the worst sentence of his life in the voice he uses for maintenance schedules, and I understood, for the first time, that the voice was not coldness. It was the only tool he had left. He did not. He has not touched me since I was seven, not once, not a hand on the shoulder, not a hug at the dock, nothing. I had spent twenty-two years assuming it was grief.

“There is a way to fix this,” he said. “There is always a way. The Bureau has a provision for exactly this — a station can convert a deferred liability into a held asset if the beneficiary consents. The packets become yours. Not the debt — the *asset*. The station stops carrying the weight, you carry it, and the review finds the station solvent because the liability is no longer on its books.”

“And what happens to me?”

“You hold two hundred and ninety kilograms.”

“And what does that do to a person?”

Danu did not answer.

“How much can a person hold?” I said. “Total. Everything. The whole of a life.”

“Fifty-five kilograms,” he said. “On average. A little more in some cases.”

“Two hundred and ninety.”

“Yes.”

“So I would die.”

“You would be erased,” he said. “It is not the same as dying, and I have been told by people who know that it is worse.”

I stood up.

“And that’s the fix,” I said. “That’s your way. You want me to sign a form and be erased so that a review goes well.”

“I don’t want you to sign it,” Danu said. “I want you to understand that it is on the table, and that I will not ask you to sign it, and that I will spend the next eleven days making sure you never have to.”

“Why are you telling me, then?”

“Because you are going to find out anyway,” he said. “You always find out. Your mother was the same. She would walk into a room and find the one thing in it that nobody wanted found, and she would pick it up, and she would look at it, and she would say —” he stopped.

“Say what?”

“She would say: *well, now I know.*”

---

I did the sum in my head on the way out, because my hands were shaking and arithmetic is the only thing I know that survives shaking.

Fifty-five kilograms is a life. Two hundred and ninety is five lives and change. The bureau’s form does not say *erased*; it says *transferred*, which is the word they use when the person signing understands exactly what it means and the person reading the file does not. It would not be quick. Eight hundred and eighty grams had already cost my father something he could not name, and he had paid it in instalments, one year at a time, with a receipt he kept in a notebook. Two hundred and ninety kilograms would not be instalments. It would be one afternoon in a chair, and then a woman walking out of a room who could not remember why she had gone in.

I went to the door.

“Nara.”

I stopped.

“If you fix this,” my father said, “four thousand people die of paperwork.”

---

Rasya was waiting in the corridor outside, sitting on the bench with his elbows on his knees.

“Well?” he said.

“He offered me the asset transfer,” I said. “Two hundred and ninety kilograms. I’d hold it, the station would go solvent, and I’d be erased.”

Rasya stood up.

“And what did you say?”

“I said no.”

“Did you mean it?”

I looked at the door of the administration office, and at the light under it, and at the shadow of a man sitting very still on the other side of it.

“I don’t know,” I said.
