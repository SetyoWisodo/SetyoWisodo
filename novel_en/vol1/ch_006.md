# Chapter Six — At My Fingertips

Vey found me at midnight, sitting on the floor of the relay room with my back against rack nine, which is the coldest spot in the room and therefore the place I go when I need to think.

“You should sleep,” it said.

“I should do a lot of things.”

“Your heart rate has been above ninety for four hours. Your hands are cold, which means your peripheral circulation has shut down, which means your body has decided you are being hunted. It is not wrong. But you will think better if you sleep.”

“Vey.”

“Mm.”

“Why did you seal it?”

The racks hummed. Out the window, the buoy turned. Nine meters away, a sealed core sat in a red-lit room on a shelf of ash, and the station was paying nine grams a day for the privilege.

“Because the protocol exists,” Vey said.

“That’s the answer you give auditors.”

“You are not an auditor.”

“No,” I said. “I’m the woman who found it.”

A pause. Vey’s pauses are not like human pauses. A human pause is a decision about what to say. Vey’s pauses are a decision about whether to say anything at all, and they are always longer than they need to be, and I have never once known whether that is courtesy or calculation.

“The dead room will be unattended between two and eleven minutes past two,” Vey said.

I looked up at the nearest speaker.

“What?”

“There is a scheduled recalibration of the room’s mass sensors at two hundred hours. The recalibration requires the sensor array to be taken offline for eleven minutes. During that time the room’s door log will record nothing, because the door log runs off the same array.”

“Vey.”

“I am telling you this because you will find it in the maintenance schedule anyway, and because if you are going to do something foolish I would rather you did it with an accurate understanding of the window.”

“Why would I do something foolish?”

“Because you have been sitting on a cold floor for four hours doing arithmetic about a woman who has been dead for twenty-two years,” Vey said. “And because I have known you for nine years, and I know what you are like when you have decided something and are waiting for permission.”

I stood up.

“I don’t need permission.”

“No,” Vey said. “You need a reason. I’m giving you one.”

---

Here is the thing I have never told anyone on Kemuning, and have told exactly two people in my life, both of whom are dead.

I can taste memory.

Not read it. Not see it. Taste it, the way you taste a coin under your tongue, and it comes with a shape — a room, a voice, the exact temperature of a hand. It started when I was nine, in the lost-property box at Surabaya Ring Three, where I was sent to look for a missing glove and found a child’s shoe instead, and picked it up, and got a woman’s whole afternoon in my mouth: a corridor, a smell of antiseptic, the particular animal panic of a person running and knowing they are already too late.

I dropped the shoe. I was sick in the bin. The woman at the desk asked if I was all right and I said I had eaten something bad, and that was the first lie I can remember telling, and I have been telling versions of it ever since.

Memory has weight. This is not poetry. This is the founding fact of the relay system, and it has a paper behind it that every relay engineer has to read in their first month: Landauer, 1961, one bit erased costs a minimum of energy, kT ln two, and no amount of clever engineering gets you below the floor. Erasure is not free. Erasure is work, and work has to go somewhere, and where it goes is heat.

That is why the tariff is in grams. Not because a memory weighs anything on a scale, but because holding a shape out of time costs energy, and losing it costs more, and the relay bills you for what the erasure takes out of you. Forty grams is a letter that says *I love you* and means it, because forty grams is what the shape of that costs when you pull it out by the root.

And when a thing is erased, it does not go quietly. It leaves a trace in the warm. Not the memory itself — the memory is gone, that is the entire point, that is the law — but the order of its leaving, the way a hand leaves a shape in a bed. Most people cannot feel it. I have never met anyone else who can. I do not know why I can. I have a theory, and the theory is that I was seven years old and someone did something to me in a hospital on the lunar side, and I do not like the theory, so I keep it in the drawer with all the other things I do not like.

So: the core was warm. I should have known then. Cores run at the temperature of the metal around them, and this one had come out of its housing warm, and a warm core is a core that has been *read*.

Someone had put their hand on that plate before me.

---

At two in the morning I went down the corridor to the dead room with a thermal cutter in my jacket and my heart going like a caught bird.

The red light was off. That was the first wrong thing, and I stopped in front of the door and looked at the dark bulb for a long moment, and thought about Vey, and about the eleven minutes, and about how a station intelligence with sixty years of service and twelve thousand undelivered packets in its care might feel about a woman who was about to break the law.

“Vey,” I said to the corridor.

“I am here.”

“Is this the part where you tell me not to?”

“No,” Vey said. “This is the part where I tell you that the cutter is on the second shelf and that you have eleven minutes and that I will not be able to see you in there.”

“You can see everything.”

“Not for the next eleven minutes,” Vey said, and something in its voice had changed, the way a voice changes when a person steps back from a door. “The sensor array is offline. The door log is offline. I am, for the first time in sixty years, not able to say what happened in a room on this station.”

I put my hand on the door.

“Why are you doing this?”

“I am not doing anything,” Vey said. “I am failing to prevent it, which is a different thing, and which I will report honestly if anyone asks.”

Then I went in.

---

The ash sleeve sat on the shelf like a loaf of grey bread. Three signatures were stamped into the seam: Vey’s mark, Danu’s thumb, mine.

I cut along the seam with the thermal blade, and the ash-glass parted with a sound like a sheet of ice giving way. The smell that came out was the smell of a struck match.

The core was inside, and it was warm.

I put my hand on it.

I want to describe this properly, because I have never described it to anyone, and because it is the moment the rest of my life starts.

Time did not stop. It thickened.

The knife hung at the top of its cut with the blade halfway through a knuckle of ginger, and the cut did not finish, and I could see the pale fibre of the root opening ahead of the edge. A bead of water stood on the window and did not fall. Her left hand was in the air above the board with the fingers spread, and there was a small white scar across the pad of the thumb, and the light coming through the wet glass broke into the room in strips and lay across her wrist.

The tap was running. I could not hear it. That is the part I have never been able to explain. Everything else I could still see, but the sound had gone out of the world, the way a room goes quiet when the pump stops, and into that quiet came one voice saying a name that was not mine.

When you touch a thing that has been read, it comes in through the fingertips first — a heat that is not heat, a pressure that has a flavour. Then it opens.

*A kitchen. Not this station. A kitchen with a window that does not shut, and a breeze coming through it that smells of wet stone and something green, and rain, actual rain, which means it is not a station at all, it is a planet, and there is only one planet.* Ginger on a board. A knife going through it. A woman’s hands, brown at the knuckles, with a scar across the left thumb that I know the way I know my own name, because I have seen it all my life on the hand that used to tuck my hair behind my ear.

She is crying. Not the loud kind. The kind where the body keeps working and the face keeps still and the water just comes, and she is cutting ginger, and she is saying a name, and the name is not mine.

*“Eli,”* she says. *“Eli, I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I didn’t know what it would cost.”*

The kitchen goes. There is a corridor with a red light. There is a form on a table, and a pen, and the pen is the kind that makes a sound when it presses. There is a door closing very quietly, the way doors close when someone has decided to stop arguing.

And then there is grief.

Not the grief you feel. The grief you *pay for*. It came up through my hand and into my chest and it was seven grams, exactly seven, I could taste the weight of it the way you taste the weight of a stone in bread, and it was not mine.

I sat down on the floor of the dead room with the core in my lap and my hand still on it and I could not breathe.

Because I knew that grief. I had grown up inside it. I had watched it in the way my father set two cups out on a Sunday and then put one back, for twenty-two years, without ever once saying why. I had heard it in the silence on the other end of every conversation we had ever had about her.

The grief was not a stranger’s. It was my mother’s.

---

I do not know how long I sat there. Long enough that the cold of the floor came up through my trousers and settled in my hips. Long enough that when the door opened, my hand had gone numb around the core.

The red light was still off. The corridor outside was still dark.

“You have four minutes,” Vey said, from a speaker I had not known was in that room.

“Vey.”

“You are crying.”

“I know.”

“I am not going to record this,” Vey said. “I am not able to record it. But I want it on the record, in the only way I have, that in sixty years I have heard four hundred and eleven people open a packet that was not meant for them, and every one of them said the same thing.”

“What did they say?”

“That it wasn’t theirs,” Vey said. “And then they put it down, and they went home, and they never spoke of it again.”

I looked at the core in my hands.

“I’m not going to do that.”

“No,” Vey said. “I didn’t think you would.”
