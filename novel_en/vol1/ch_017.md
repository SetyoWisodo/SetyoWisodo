# Chapter Seventeen — Installments

He brought the notebook the next day, which was not what I expected.

I had spent the night not sleeping, which is a thing I am good at, and by morning I had arrived at a plan of sorts: I would go to the administration office, I would take the seal file out of my father’s cabinet with my own hands, and I would read it, and whatever was in it would be mine.

The plan lasted until I saw Rasya in the corridor with a notebook.

“What’s that?”

“A ledger,” he said.

“You’re a Biro auditor. You own four ledgers.”

“This one’s mine. Not the Biro’s.” He held it up. It was small, cloth-bound, the kind of thing you buy at a station shop because it is the only notebook there is. “I want to show you something, and I want to do it in private, and I want you to promise not to say anything until I’ve finished.”

“I don’t promise things I can’t keep.”

“Then I’ll ask you to try.”

We went to my quarters. He sat on the chair. I sat on the bunk. He opened the notebook at the first page and turned it around so I could see.

---

The first page said:

*Ayu. Wardhana. Born 2150? Died 2161, Tsiolkovsky, Ring C, aged 11.*

*Sister. Younger than me. (Check: file says born 2150, I am 2149. So younger. Yes.)*

*She had a red coat. (?) — is this real or is it from the photograph?*

*She could whistle through her teeth.*

*She was afraid of the dark and would not say so, and slept with the light on and told our mother it was because she liked to read.*

The handwriting was small and even and had been gone over in places where the pen had pressed too hard.

I turned the page.

*Things I am no longer sure about:*

*— whether she called me Rasya or Ya.*

*— whether the red coat was hers or mine.*

*— the name of the street outside the dome. (Why would I know a street? We lived inside. There was no street. But I remember a word that sounds like a street.)*

*— whether she was left-handed.*

*— what her voice sounded like.*

The last item was underlined twice.

I looked up at him.

“Don’t say anything,” he said.

---

The notebook was sixty-one pages long.

Some of it was a list of facts, dated, sourced, cross-referenced to the file. Some of it was questions. Some of it was a record of payments — date, amount, purpose — in three columns, the way a man keeps accounts when he is trying to prove to himself that he is still solvent.

*2180.03.06 — 11 g — dependant file 44-1190, summary*
*2180.04.01 — 40 g — review fee, year 1*
*2181.04.01 — 40 g — review fee, year 2*
*2182.04.01 — 44 g — review fee, year 3 (indexed)*
*2183.04.01 — 44 g — review fee, year 4*

And then, on the page after the accounts, in the same careful hand:

*After the review fee, I lost the whistling.*

*I know she could whistle through her teeth because I wrote it down. I do not know what it sounded like. I know that I used to know. There is a difference and it is the worst difference there is.*

---

“Four payments,” I said.

“Five, counting the summary.”

“A hundred and seventy-nine grams.”

“A hundred and eighty-three, with the indexation.”

I closed the notebook.

“And you did this,” I said, “for me.”

“Don’t.”

“You paid a hundred and eighty-three grams of your sister to open my file.”

“I paid it to a registry clerk,” Rasya said. “The registry clerk did not care about either of us. That’s what I keep trying to explain. I did not trade my sister for you. I traded my sister for a *receipt*. The trade was with a filing system. The filing system took what it took and gave me nothing but a date.”

His voice had gone rough.

“And the worst part,” he said, “is that it worked. Not the way I wanted. But it worked. The file moved. The registry scheduled the review. And in the middle of all of it, a core came out of Buoy Seven warm, and a woman stood in a relay room with her mother’s grief in her mouth, and I was already here. I was already here because I had paid.”

---

“Say her name,” I said.

“Ayu.”

“Her whole name.”

He opened his mouth.

“Ayu,” he said. “Ayu —”

The room was quiet. The water plant was quiet. Even the duct above rack four, which knocks every eleven seconds, seemed to have stopped knocking.

“Ayu Wardhana,” he said, and stopped, and I watched him reach for the second name the way a man reaches for a word in a language he used to speak.

“There were two names,” he said. “I know there were two names. I have it written down somewhere in this book.”

He turned the pages. He turned them faster. Then he stopped, and read, and his face did something I would rather not have seen.

“It’s not in the book,” he said.

“Rasya.”

“It’s not in the book. I wrote everything in the book. I wrote the red coat and the whistling and the light on in her room, and I did not write her second name, because when I started the book I still knew it.”

He closed the notebook very slowly, the way you close a door in a house where somebody is sleeping.

“I could not remember whether she had been older or younger,” he said. “That was the first thing. Six months after the first payment. I woke up one morning and I could not remember whether I had been the older one. I sat on the edge of my bed and I did the arithmetic on my fingers — I knew the year she was born, I knew the year I was born — and I got the answer, and it was the right answer, and it did not feel like anything. It felt like reading a fact about a stranger.”

He put the notebook on the table.

“So I started writing things down. And then I started writing down what I was no longer sure about. And then I started writing down the payments, because I wanted to know how much I had spent, and because a man who is losing things needs a number to hold on to.”

---

I did not know what to say, so I said nothing for a while, and the silence did what silence does in a small room, which is to make the objects in it louder: the sound of his thumbnail against the table edge, the click of the water plant cycling behind the wall, the small dry tick of the notebook’s cloth cover settling where he had put it down.

“The relay can’t do that.”

He looked up.

“What?”

“A packet can’t carry information faster than light,” I said. “That’s the no-communication result. Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1980: no arrangement of entangled states carries a signal by itself. It’s the first thing they teach you, and it’s the only law the network has never broken. The relay holds and schedules. It never sends. So when you pay grams for a file, nothing is transmitted. Nothing comes back. You’re not buying information at all. You’re buying a *decision* — you’re paying a clerk to move a piece of paper from one cabinet to another.”

“Yes.”

“And the grams come out of you anyway.”

“Yes.”

“So you paid with your sister for a piece of paper to be moved.”

Rasya looked at the notebook.

“That’s the tariff,” he said. “Nobody ever says it that way, but that’s what it is. The relay is a door. The door doesn’t carry the message. The door just decides when it opens. And every time you make it open, it takes something out of you and gives it to the heat.”

I put my hand flat on the table.

“How do you live with it?” I said.

“You write things down,” he said. “And you stop trusting the feeling of certainty. Certainty is the thing that goes first. You don’t lose the memory and feel a hole. You lose the memory and feel *fine* — and then one day you read a sentence in your own handwriting and you know that the person who wrote it knew something you don’t, and there is no way to get it back, because the thing you’d have to pay with is the thing you’re trying to find.”

He stood up and picked up the notebook.

“I have to go and be an auditor,” he said. “The licence review is tomorrow.”

“The what?”

He stopped in the doorway.

“The station’s licence renewal,” he said. “Every four years. It was scheduled before any of this. It’s routine.”

“Nothing on this station is routine.”

“No,” Rasya said. “It isn’t.”
