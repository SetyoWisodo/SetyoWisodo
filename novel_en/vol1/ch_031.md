# Chapter Thirty-One — The Ash Locker

The station went into lockdown at 14:00 on the fourteenth of November, and I want to say at the start that lockdown on a ring is not what it is on a ship.

On a ship, lockdown means doors. On Kemuning, lockdown means that four thousand people are told, in the flat voice of a machine that has been given the order, that the ring is closed for a maintenance inspection, that all shifts are suspended, that the water will be off between 16:00 and 18:00, and that nobody is to enter the service crawl. It means the docks are sealed with a pressure barrier and a guard who has been given a written instruction. It means the schools keep going, because a school is a place where four hundred children can be counted, and counting is what lockdown is actually for.

It does not mean that anybody stops. It means that everybody is where the register says they are.

---

We met in the administration office at 14:20: my father, Rasya, Kirana because she refused to leave, and me.

The table had on it four things. I want to describe them in the order they were laid down, because the order is the briefing.

Danu put the ash locker’s seal photograph on the table first. Then the mass kit, closed. Then a case — the case that Sekar had left in the cold-well room, empty, its foam cut to the shape of a core. Then he put down a sheet of paper with eleven names on it, and the eleven names were the eleven people who had been issued keys to the dead room since 2171.

“Eleven keys,” he said. “Four returned on retirement. Two are dead. One is a magistrate in Surabaya and has not been on this station since 2178. Three are in this room. One is Vey’s maintenance override, which cannot be keyed without a second signature, and the second signature is mine.”

I looked at the eleven names. My own was on the list. So was Kirana’s, in the hand of a nineteen-year-old apprentice who had signed for it in August, and Danu’s, in his small forward-leaning script, and the fourth, at the bottom, in a hand I did not recognise because the ink had faded.

“Who is that?” I said.

“Halim,” my father said.

The room was quiet.

“Halim has been dead for twenty-two years.”

“Halim’s key was returned on the twenty-fourth of September, 2161,” Danu said. “The return is logged. The key is in the drawer. I have looked.”

“Then it is a name on a sheet of paper.”

“It is a name on a sheet of paper,” he said, “and a name on a sheet of paper is the beginning of every audit I have ever conducted.”

---

Here is what the briefing said, in the order it was said.

The ash locker’s seal was intact. The seal photograph, taken at 06:00 on the fourteenth, matched the pattern pressed on the twelfth of October. Nobody had opened the locker from outside.

The dead room’s door log recorded one entry between 22:00 on the thirteenth and 08:00 on the fourteenth.

The entry was keyed with my credential.

“Is that true?” Danu said.

“I was asleep,” I said.

“That is not what I asked.”

“Then ask what you mean.”

“I mean,” my father said, “that the door log records a credential and a time, and it does not record a face. It records that a key was used. Was your key used?”

I put my hand flat on the table.

“I do not know,” I said.

---

Rasya picked up the ash photograph and held it to the light.

“There is a second explanation,” he said. “The locker was opened from inside.”

“From inside?” Kirana said.

“The drawer is out four centimetres. The ash on the lip is disturbed from the inside. The seal on the outside is intact.” He put the photograph down. “The door log records the room. It does not record the cabinet. So the question is not who opened the room. It is how the cabinet was opened without breaking the seal.”

“There is only one way,” I said. “The cabinet has a maintenance port in the back, and the maintenance port opens onto the cold-well junction, and the well junction has a crawl.”

“Then the thief came through the crawl.”

“Then the thief came through the crawl,” I said, “which is where I found her.”

Danu looked at me.

“You found her,” he said. “You did not report her.”

“I reported her at 09:06.”

“You found her at 08:40.”

“Then I did not report her for twenty-six minutes,” I said. “I spent twenty-six minutes in a service crawl following a smell because I did not want to be the woman who raised an alarm about her own mother, and if you would like that in the log, Vey can put it in.”

The room was very quiet. Kirana was looking at the table. Rasya had gone still in the way he goes still, which is the way of a man who is deciding whether to be an auditor or a person.

“I would like it in the log,” my father said.

---

The interview room is on the administration level, and it has a table with a ring on the underside for restraints, and a camera above the door, and no window.

Sekar Adisurya sat on the far side of the table with her hands folded and her chin a little to one side and her hair pinned up with the one pin that never holds.

She had been searched. She had been given a station jacket because hers had solvent on it. She had been given water, and had not drunk it. She had been read her rights under annex nine by Vey in the flat voice Vey uses for weather and deaths, and she had listened to the whole of it, and at the end she had said, “I know my rights. I wrote half of them.”

Rasya did the interview. I watched from the observation side, through the glass, with my palm flat on the bench.

“Your name,” he said.

“Sekar Adisurya.”

“Date of birth?”

“Third of June, 2122.”

“Place of birth?”

“Tsiolkovsky Station, Ring C.”

“Status?”

“Deceased,” she said, “according to you.”

Rasya wrote it down.

“You were found in the cold-well room of Kemuning Station at 08:40 on the fourteenth of November, with a core case in your possession. The core it was cut for is missing from the evidence locker in the dead room. Do you understand the charge?”

“Yes.”

“Do you wish to make a statement?”

“Yes.”

The room was quiet. I put both hands flat on the bench.

“I wish to make a statement,” Sekar said, “which is that I will not answer a single question until the evacuation is under way. Not one. Not about the core, not about the crawl, not about the case, not about the wall. There are four thousand people on this ring and five days left, and I have spent twenty-two years learning what happens to a station when the Bureau decides to look at it, and I am not going to spend the last five days being looked at.”

Rasya put his stylus down.

“That is not a statement,” he said. “That is a refusal.”

“It is a refusal,” she said. “You can put it in the log.”

The weighing was at 17:00.

I did it myself, because I am the relay engineer, and because a scale is only worth the woman who checks it. I took the calibration slug out of the top tray of the mass kit and put it on the brass pan, and I did the thing I have done ten thousand times: my thumb went to the serial number on the base, worn to a shadow, and rubbed it once.

Forty grams. The scale said forty grams, and I put the slug back in the tray, and then I weighed the ash locker.

There are two ways to weigh an ash locker. You weigh the cabinet, and you weigh the evidence, and the difference is the tare. The tare of that cabinet has been 118.400 kilograms since 2171, and it is painted on the frame in three generations of hand, and every engineer who has ever certified a seal on this station has written that number at the top of a sheet.

The cabinet weighed 118.404.

Four grams.

“Kirana,” I said. “Read me the tare.”

“One hundred and eighteen point four.”

“Read it again.”

“One hundred and eighteen point four.” She looked up. “The scale says one eighteen point four oh four.”

“Which means what?”

“Which means the cabinet is four grams heavier than it was when it was certified.”

“Which means what?”

Kirana looked at the cabinet, and then at me, and her face did the thing that faces do when a nineteen-year-old arrives at the same conclusion as an adult and does not want to be the one to say it.

“Which means there is four grams of something in it that is not supposed to be there,” she said. “Or four grams of something missing that used to weigh four grams more.”

“Say the second one.”

“Someone put something in that weighed four grams less than what they took out,” Kirana said.

I put my hand on the cabinet.

“No,” I said. “Someone put something in that weighs exactly what the *core* weighs. Four grams is the tolerance. The core is four grams plus or minus nothing, because a certified core is weighed at the factory and the weight is etched on the base.”

“Then the cabinet would weigh the same.”

“The cabinet would weigh the same,” I said, “if the thing inside it had the same weight as the core. It does not. It weighs four grams more. Which means there are two things in there now, or one thing that has been modified, or —”

I stopped.

“Or what?” Kirana said.

“Or the thief has been back,” I said, “and put something in, and taken nothing out, and the four grams is the weight of a message.”

---

We opened the locker at 17:20 by breaking the seal under camera, with my father reading the number out loud and Rasya recording it on the field unit and Vey logging the time.

The drawer came out four centimetres and then came out all the way, and there was nothing in it.

Not empty in the way a thing is empty when it has been taken. Empty in the way a room is empty when somebody has cleaned it. The foam was flat. The shape of the core, the shape that had been pressed into the foam in October and never smoothed out, was smooth. Somebody had pressed the foam flat with a tool, and then dusted the dust back over it, and the dust was the ash from the lip and the dust was the wrong colour.

And in the bottom of the drawer, under the foam, where the certified weight of the core is stamped into the steel in the factory and never touched again, there was a new stamp.

It was four grams.

It was the weight of a thing that had been put there so that a scale would read true, by somebody who understood that a scale is checked before it is trusted, and that the person who checks it will be the person who is blamed.

The door log had one entry, and it was my key, and I had been asleep.
