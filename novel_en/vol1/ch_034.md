# Chapter Thirty-Four — The Terminal Sale

My father’s office at four in the morning on the sixteenth of November was the emptiest I had ever seen it, because he was packing.

Not moving out. Packing the *record*: the station’s paper, eleven years of licences, the seal registers, the manifests, the maintenance logs, the medical list, the casualty schedule, everything that a dechartered installation is required to surrender to the Bureau and everything it is not, folded into crates with the crates numbered in his small forward-leaning hand.

“You are packing,” I said.

“I am packing.”

“There are two days.”

“There are two days, and on the seventeenth this station stops existing as a legal person, and a legal person’s papers belong to its successor, and its successor is the Bureau, and the Bureau’s archive is a room in an office on the inner routes that has never been opened to a magistrate.” He put a crate down. “So I am packing what I intend to keep.”

I sat on the corner of his desk, which I have not done since I was nine.

“What do you intend to keep?”

“The casualty schedule,” he said. “The seal register. The dependant file 44-1190. And one sheet of paper with four thousand one hundred numbers on it, which I have been keeping in a drawer since 2171 and which nobody has ever asked me about.”

“What is it?”

“It is an inventory,” Danu said. “Would you like to see what it cost me to keep it?”

---

Here is what he told me, and I am going to put it down in the order he said it, because the order is the truth and the truth has been rearranged enough.

Tsiolkovsky Station was decommissioned in 2168. The observatory had been losing its funding since 2163, the ring had a structural rating that would not survive a second review, and the Bureau’s decision was not a tragedy, it was an arithmetic: eleven thousand people, a failing ring, a relay that had been superseded by the Farside array.

The station was closed over nine months. The people were moved. The equipment was inventoried, and what the Bureau wanted was taken, and what the Bureau did not want was sold on the open market to anybody who would pay the freight.

“The relay room,” Danu said, “had a terminal.”

“The terminal.”

“Four tonnes of it. A class four relay terminal with a write-once store, which is the kind of store that cannot be erased except by a Bureau technician with a Bureau order and a Bureau charge of eleven kilowatts for the erasure, which is more than the terminal was worth.” He put another crate down. “So the Bureau did not erase it. The Bureau sold it.”

“To whom?”

“To a dealer on Bukit,” my father said, “called Suryadi. He buys old terminals and strips them for the cores, and he has been doing it for thirty years, and he came to Tsiolkovsky in 2171 with a cargo hull and a crew of four and a cash offer of eleven hundred grams.”

Eleven hundred grams.

“That is a lot of memory,” I said.

“It is eleven years of an ordinary life,” Danu said. “It is also the seal fees for the next twenty-seven years at the 2171 rate, before the compounding in table three, which nobody had explained to me, and which I did not understand until 2179.”

I looked at the crates.

“You sold the terminal.”

“I sold the terminal.”

“The terminal my mother put her hand on.”

“The terminal your mother put her hand on,” Danu said, “with a stylus in her other hand and a child on the bench behind her, at seven in the morning on the twentieth of September, 2161.”

---

This is where I have to put the two clocks, because my father told the story in two of them at once, and I have never worked out whether he did it on purpose.

The first clock was 2171. A station with eleven thousand people was being taken apart by crews who had been hired by the month, and the sea of the observatory dome was being cut into sections, and the relay room was stripped, and the terminal was loaded onto a cargo hull by four men and a crane.

The second clock was 2183. In the same hours of the same day, four hundred million kilometres away, a station with four thousand people was being prepared for a different kind of decommissioning, and a queue had formed at the north lock, and Kirana was standing on the yellow line with her tally slate against her chest, counting crates.

At the same hour in 2171, I was seventeen and at the academy in Bukit, learning to weigh things.

At the same hour in 2183, a woman I had met in a cold-well room was sitting in an interview room refusing to answer a question until the second shift undocked.

Meanwhile, on the inner routes, a tender with a cutting crew had been at its second relay since the fourteenth.

“The dealer paid in grams,” I said. “What did you spend it on?”

“The fees,” Danu said. “The compounding seal fees on file 44-1190, and the maintenance on your mother’s account, and the bond on your dependant file, and eleven other things that I have in this drawer and have never once justified to anybody.”

“And the rest?”

“There is no rest,” he said. “I sold a terminal for eleven hundred grams and it bought eleven years, and then the tariff ate the eleven years, and in 2179 I started selling the station’s spare cores, and in 2181 I sold the ring’s reserve buoy, and in 2182 I stopped sleeping.”

---

“Why are you telling me this now?” I said.

“Because you asked who paid.”

“I asked Sekar who paid.”

“And she pointed at a sheet of paper,” my father said, “and the paper was the casualty schedule, and the casualty schedule has nine names on it and one of them is Halim’s, and Halim paid for something and I do not know what it was.”

He opened the bottom drawer and took out a folded sheet, and unfolded it on the desk between us.

It was an inventory. It was four pages long. Column one was a list of equipment, column two was a weight, column three was a destination, column four was a date. The dates ran from 2169 to 2182. The destinations were Bukit, Surabaya, the inner routes, a dealer in the belt, a dealer in the belt, a dealer in the belt.

At the bottom of the fourth page, in the same small forward-leaning hand, was a line that read:

*CLASS 4 RELAY TERMINAL — TSIOLKOVSKY RELAY ROOM — 4,011 kg — SURYADI, BUKIT — 2171.04.11 — 1,100 g.*

I looked at the date for a long time.

“The eleventh of April,” I said.

“The eleventh of April.”

“That is my birthday.”

“It is your birthday,” Danu said. “Seventeen years to the day.”

“That is a coincidence.”

“That is not a coincidence,” he said. “Suryadi wanted the terminal loaded and out before the decommissioning audit, and the decommissioning audit was on the twelfth, and the only crane slot available was the eleventh. I have told myself for twelve years that it was a coincidence, and I have never once believed it.”

---

I put my hand flat on the inventory.

“Where is it now?” I said. “The terminal.”

“In a yard on Bukit,” Danu said. “Suryadi strips terminals. He has a yard with forty of them in it, and he strips them for the cores and sells the shells for scrap, and he does not strip fast, because there is no hurry in a yard.”

“Then it might still exist.”

“It might still exist.”

“And if it exists,” I said, “it has the write-once store, and the write-once store has the original entries, and the original entries include the payment of the twentieth of September, 2161, with a time of 07:00:11 and a bond of forty grams and a name.”

“They include the name,” my father said. “They include the weight. They include the origin stamp. They include, I am almost certain, the handwriting.”

“The handwriting.”

“A class four terminal records the stylus,” he said. “Every stroke. It is how the register proves that the hand that signed is the hand that was there. Your mother’s hand is in that store, on a payment slip, on the morning of the twentieth of September, 2161, and the store is write-once, and the only way to destroy it is to erase it with a Bureau technician and a Bureau order and eleven kilowatts.”

He folded the inventory and put it in my hand.

“One more thing,” he said.

“What?”

“Why I never sold it to be erased. Why I have been paying the maintenance on a dead woman’s account since 2161, and selling cores, and lying to a magistrates’ office, and letting my daughter believe that her mother stopped loving her.”

“Why?”

My father looked at the four crates and the eleven years of paper.

“I sold it because it still had her handwriting in it,” he said.
