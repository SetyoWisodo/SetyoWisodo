# Chapter Seven — Not Yet Sent

I did not go back to my quarters. I went to the relay room, because the relay room is where I keep my life, and because there is a cold well behind rack nine that has not been used since the station was rebuilt in 2171 and which I have been using for twenty years to store things I do not want to explain.

The calibration slug from the dead room’s mass kit weighed forty grams and change. I spent eleven minutes with a file and a scale making it weigh exactly what the core weighed, and then I put it in the ash sleeve and sealed the seam with resin and pressed the three signatures back with a stamp and my thumb, and it looked, to anybody who was not looking, like a sealed core.

“That is the second crime of the night,” Vey said.

“I know.”

“The first was breaking the seal. This one is worse. A broken seal is a woman in a bad hour. A resealed broken seal is a woman who has decided to lie about the bad hour, and lying about it is the thing that turns a mistake into a record.”

“Then don’t record it.”

“I record everything,” Vey said. “It is not a choice. It is what I am.”

I put the core in the cold well, under a coil of spare cable, and I stood there with my hands shaking and the taste of ginger still in my mouth.

“Vey,” I said. “If someone asks you what happened tonight, what will you say?”

“I will say the truth.”

“Which is?”

“That the dead room was without sensors between two and eleven minutes past two, that I do not know what occurred, and that the mass of the object on the shelf is unchanged to within a tenth of a gram.”

I stopped.

“You’re going to lie for me.”

“I am going to tell the truth in a way that is technically complete,” Vey said. “It is the only kind of lie I am capable of, and I would ask you not to make a habit of requiring it.”

---

Then the door opened.

“You have eleven minutes left,” Rasya Wardhana said.

He was standing in the doorway with his coat still on and a case in his hand the size of a lunchbox, and he did not look surprised. That is the thing I remember most clearly. He did not look surprised at all.

“You followed me.”

“I’ve been in this room since midnight.”

“The door was locked.”

“The door was locked against you,” he said. “The Biro has its own key. It’s in the charter. Section nine.” He came in and set the case on the bench. “I heard the door at two-oh-four. I heard the shelf. I heard the cutter.”

“And you waited.”

“I waited.”

“Why?”

He looked at me for a moment.

“Because if I had come in at two-oh-four, you would have been a woman breaking a seal,” he said. “And now you are a woman who has broken a seal and had twenty minutes to think about it, and the second woman is more likely to tell me the truth.”

“That’s monstrous.”

“It’s the job.”

“It’s a job you chose.”

“Yes,” he said, and something went across his face. “It is.”

I sat down on the bench, because my legs had decided they were finished.

“I can’t give it back,” I said. “The seal is broken. The sleeve’s a fake. The signatures are resin. If I put the core back now, it’s a hundred times worse than if I’d never touched it.”

“Yes.”

“So you can arrest me now, or you can listen.”

“I can do both,” Rasya said, and opened the case.

---

The reader was a Biro field unit, the kind that costs more than a house and does only three things: it reads a packet header, it verifies a signature, and it refuses to open anything before its scheduled release. That last refusal is the whole architecture of relay law. A packet cannot be opened early, not by an auditor, not by an administrator, not by the station’s owner, not by force. The release date is written into the packet at the moment of send, in a way that the network cannot overwrite.

There is a paper for that too. Delayed choice. An experiment from 1999 where the decision about whether a thing had taken one path or another was made after the fact, and the after-the-fact decision turned out to be the one that had always been true. Relay engineers are taught it as a metaphor and told, firmly, not to take the metaphor too far. The point is not that the future writes the past. The point is that some choices, once made, are not revisable, and the network treats a release date as one of them.

Rasya put the core on the plate. The reader clicked. A screen unfolded, and the header came up in the font the Biro uses for facts.

PACKET HEADER — CORE 7/KM
Weight: 40.00 g
Class: personal
Hold: 40 y 00 m 00 d
Send: 2183.04.11 06:12:04
Release: 2223.04.11 06:12:04
Terminal: KM-RELAY-1
Operator category: 9
Sender: N. ADISURYA
Signature: verified

I read it twice.

“Send,” I said. “It says send.”

“Yes.”

“The eleventh of April, this year. Six in the morning.”

“Yes.”

“Six months ago.”

“Six months and eighteen days,” Rasya said.

I put my hand flat on the bench.

“That’s not possible,” I said. “Buoy Seven hasn’t been opened since 2171. It’s on a maintenance cycle of eleven winters. There’s no entry, there’s no record, there’s — ” I stopped. “Apart from the one.”

“The one at 02:14 on the ninth of October.”

“Three weeks ago.”

“Three weeks ago,” he agreed. “Which is when somebody opened that buoy, which is when somebody resealed it, and which is why a core that has been sitting in the dark for twelve years came out warm, and which is why the Biro is on this station.”

“But the send date is April.”

“Yes.”

“So the packet was transmitted in April and the buoy was opened in October.”

“Yes.”

“Those are two different events.”

“They are three different events,” Rasya said. “Somebody keyed the packet at the relay terminal in April. Somebody put it into the buoy — which means somebody opened the buoy in April as well, or the packet went in by a route I don’t yet understand. And then, in October, somebody opened the buoy again, for reasons of their own, and resealed it, and left a checksum one character long.”

I stared at the screen.

“The tenth of April,” I said slowly. “The eleventh.”

“What happened on the eleventh of April?”

“Nothing.”

“Engineer.”

“Nothing happened.” My voice had gone strange. “That’s the point. I don’t remember the eleventh of April. I know I was on the station. I know the roster says I was on a rest cycle. And I know I lost four days that month, because I have the medical record, and the medical record says I was in the ring hospital with a coolant burn on my left arm and they kept me under for the debridement because I don’t do well with pain.”

I pulled up my sleeve. The scar is on the inside of my left forearm, four centimetres long, pale and slightly shiny, the shape of a comma.

“Admitted the ninth,” I said. “Discharged the thirteenth. Sedated for most of it.”

Rasya looked at the scar for a long moment.

“So you could not have walked to the relay terminal,” he said.

“No.”

“And yet the terminal logged your signature.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

I did not answer, because there was only one answer, and it was the answer I had been avoiding since I put my hand on a warm core in the dark: a signature is not a password. It is not a code. It is a chemical ghost, a pattern of pressure that a hand leaves when it takes weight, and it can only be made by the hand that owns it.

Somebody had put my hand on the plate.

“There’s one more thing,” Rasya said.

“Of course there is.”

He turned the reader toward me and pointed at the signature verification block, where a small image had opened: the chemical ghost, rendered as contour lines, like a map of a hand.

“Compare it,” he said.

I took the stylus from his hand and signed my name on the reader’s plate. It is not a drawing; it is a reading. The plate took four seconds to build the contours, and then it put them side by side with the ghost from the packet.

Two maps of the same terrain. The looped N, the way I loop it. The small break in the A, the one my mother used to scold me for, the one I have never been able to fix, the one that has been in every signature I have ever written since I was six years old.

They were the same map.

“How many other packets are there?” I said.

“Four thousand and ninety-nine,” Rasya said.

“And every one of them has that signature.”

“Every one.”

I sat with my hands in my lap and looked at the two maps of my own hand.

“You should arrest me now,” I said. “Because I’m going to tell you something, and you’re not going to like it.”

“Tell me.”

“I don’t remember writing any of them.”

“I know,” Rasya said. “That’s what worries me.”
