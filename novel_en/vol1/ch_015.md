# Chapter Fifteen — The Wrong Trust

The core had to be kept cold, and the cold well behind rack nine had been running at four degrees above its rating since the station was rebuilt, which meant that every eleven hours somebody had to take the core out, weigh it, log the weight, and put it back with a fresh gel pack.

That somebody was me. That somebody was now also Rasya Wardhana, because the Biro had taken custody of the core as evidence and the Biro’s custodian was not going to let it out of his sight, and I was not going to let it out of mine.

So we did the night shift together.

---

There is a particular intimacy to working with someone in a relay room at two in the morning that has nothing to do with anything either of you wants.

The room is small. The light is one lamp. The racks hum, and after an hour the hum gets into your chest, and you stop being able to tell whether you are tired or whether the building is tired. You hand each other things without speaking. You learn the other person’s rhythm — when they breathe out before they reach, when they pause before they lift — and after four hours you know more about their body than you would after a month of dinners.

He was careful. That was the first thing. He was careful in the way that mechanics are careful, not in the way that managers are careful: he put his hands where he said he would put them, and he did not touch anything he had not been told to touch, and when he was uncertain he stopped and said so.

“Gel pack,” I said.

The cold well has a timer on it, because a core that has been left too long in a warm room is not evidence any more, it is a rumour. The timer counts in eleven-hour blocks and it had 04:19:02 on it, and it was counting down whether or not anybody was looking at it, and I found that I was looking at it the way you look at a clock in a room where somebody is about to say something true.

“Four hours nineteen,” I said.

“I know.”

“It’s not much time left.”

“It’s enough time left,” he said, and handed me the gel pack.

He handed me the gel pack.

“Weigh it.”

He weighed it. “Four hundred and eleven grams.”

“Log it.”

He logged it.

“Say the number back to me,” I said.

“Four-eleven. Entry one oh seven.”

“Again.”

He looked up. “Why?”

“Because the log is the only thing we have,” I said. “Because if the number in your head and the number in the log are different, we find out now and not in front of a magistrate.”

“Four-eleven. Entry one oh seven.”

“Good.”

We did this eleven times in the first four hours. At some point he took off his coat and hung it on the back of the chair, and at some point I stopped noticing that he was there, which is the highest compliment I know how to give a person in a relay room.

At four in the morning I said, “Do you want to hold it?”

He looked up.

“The core?”

“No, the gel pack.” I put the core on the bench between us. “Yes, the core. It’s evidence, and you’re the custodian, and I’m the engineer, and at some point somebody is going to ask whether the custodian ever actually handled the evidence or whether he just stood there being bureaucratic about it.”

“That’s not why you’re asking.”

“No,” I said. “It isn’t.”

“Why are you asking?”

I looked at the thing on the bench. Forty grams of somebody’s love, sealed in a housing the size of a fist, cold as a stone in a river.

“Because I want to know what it feels like to you,” I said.

---

He put both hands around it, the way you hold a bird.

I watched his face. That is a thing I have learned to do over the years — watch the face, not the hands. The hands will lie to you; the face does not know how.

Nothing happened.

He held it for a minute. Two. His brow furrowed slightly, in concentration rather than anything else, and then he said, “It’s cold.”

“That’s all?”

“It’s cold and it’s heavy and it’s making my palms ache.” He looked up. “What was supposed to happen?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re a bad liar.”

“I’m a very good liar,” I said. “I’ve been doing it since I was nine. I’m just tired.”

He put the core down and did not let go of it.

“Tell me,” he said.

And here is the thing. I have never told anyone. Not my father, not Kirana, not the medic who has known me since I was twenty. There is a reason for that, and the reason is that the last person I told was my mother, and she is dead, and I have spent twenty-two years making sure that the number of people who know is exactly one.

But it was four in the morning, and the room was warm, and the man across the bench had spent four hours handing me gel packs without once getting it wrong, and I was so tired that the truth came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked pipe: sideways, and all at once.

“I can taste memory,” I said.

He did not laugh. He did not lean back. He did not do any of the things I had spent twenty years imagining people would do.

He said, “Tell me what that means.”

---

So I told him.

The lost-property box at Surabaya Ring Three, when I was nine. The child’s shoe. The woman’s afternoon, the corridor, the antiseptic, the animal panic. The bin I was sick in. The lie I told the woman at the desk, which was the first lie I can remember telling, and which I have been telling versions of ever since.

“It’s residue,” I said. “That’s the word I use for it. When a thing is read, it leaves a trace — an order in the heat, the way a hand leaves a shape in a bed. Landauer. The erasure has to go somewhere; it goes into the warm. Most people can’t feel it. I can. It comes in through my fingertips and it tastes like —” I stopped. “It tastes like the thing itself. Not a picture. The whole afternoon. The temperature of the room and the smell and the particular way the person was standing.”

“And when you touched the core,” Rasya said.

“I got a kitchen,” I said. “Ginger. Rain. A woman cutting ginger and crying and saying a name that wasn’t mine.”

“Your mother’s kitchen.”

“My mother’s kitchen.”

He was quiet for a while. The racks hummed. Outside the window the buoy turned.

“There’s no rain on a station,” he said.

“No.”

“So the kitchen was on a planet.”

“There’s only one planet,” I said. “And my mother never went there. She was born lunar-side, she worked lunar-side, she died lunar-side, and there’s no rain on the moon.”

Rasya looked at the core.

“Then the memory isn’t hers,” he said. “It’s someone else’s, and she was in it.”

“That’s what I keep coming back to,” I said. “The grief was hers. I’d know it anywhere; I grew up inside it. But the kitchen wasn’t. The rain wasn’t. So she was remembering something that happened to somebody else, or she was imagining it, or —”

“Or she was remembering a place she had been,” Rasya said, “and someone had taken the place out of her.”

I looked at him.

“That’s a very specific thing to say.”

“I’ve read a great deal about the tariff,” he said. “Everyone who pays in grams loses something. Nobody knows what. The system takes the thing that is least attached.”

“Least attached?”

“That’s the theory. The registry says the algorithm selects the memory with the fewest associative links — the one that can be removed with the least damage. That’s how they justify it. They call it the least-loss principle.” He turned the core over in his hands. “What they don’t tell you is that a memory with the fewest links is not the least important memory. It’s the one that only has one link. A rainy afternoon that nobody else remembers. A name nobody says anymore. The algorithm doesn’t take what you can spare. It takes what nobody will notice is gone.”

The lamp buzzed. The room was very quiet.

“That’s the cruellest thing I’ve ever heard,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And you paid eleven grams for my file.”

“Yes.”

“What did it take from you?”

He was silent for a long moment.

“I don’t know,” he said. “That’s the point.”

---

We worked until six. At some point I put my head down on the bench for a minute, and the minute became an hour, and when I woke the room was blue with the station’s morning light and there was a coat over my shoulders.

It was his coat. The grey one, with the Biro patch on the sleeve and the lining worn through at the elbow.

I sat up. He was at the far end of the bench with his back to me, running a diagnostic on the second rack, and he did not turn around, and I understood that he had put the coat over me and then turned around so that I would not have to be seen waking up.

I pulled the collar up to my face, because it was cold, and because I was still half asleep, and because I have a bad habit that I have had since I was nine: when I am not thinking, my hands tell me things.

The coat was warm from the rack.

And it tasted like me.

Not like a memory of me. Not like a thing I had touched. Like *me* — the particular taste of my own body, the way the inside of your own wrist smells, the way your own breath tastes in the morning. A corridor. A door. My own hands hanging loose. Somebody’s voice, not close enough to understand, saying something about eleven minutes.

I dropped the coat.

My heart was going like a caught bird.

His jacket tasted like her. Not like a memory of her. Like her.
