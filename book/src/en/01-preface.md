# Preface {.unnumbered}

This is a book about living through something that is already happening.

For most of the twentieth century, climate change was discussed in the future tense. Scientists warned that rising concentrations of carbon dioxide would eventually warm the planet, and governments debated whether, when and how to respond. That period is over. The world has already warmed by roughly 1.4 °C compared with the late nineteenth century. The year 2024 was the hottest ever measured, at about 1.55 °C above the pre-industrial average, and the eleven years from 2015 to 2025 are the eleven warmest in a record that stretches back 176 years (WMO, 2026). The question is no longer whether the climate will change during our lifetimes. It is how much it will change, how fast, and how well we will cope.

Three facts make further warming certain. The first is physics: carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases trap heat, a property measured in laboratories since the 1850s. The second is accumulation: carbon dioxide persists in the atmosphere for centuries, and its concentration has risen from about 278 parts per million before industrialisation to nearly 424 parts per million today (NOAA, 2025a). The third is inertia: the oceans, which have absorbed around 91 percent of the extra energy trapped by greenhouse gases, release that heat slowly, while ice sheets and sea levels respond over centuries (WMO, 2026). Even in the most optimistic scenario, the coming decades will be hotter than the past ones.

None of this means the future is hopeless. Whether the world stabilises at 1.7 °C or drifts towards 3 °C makes an enormous difference to every number in this book — to the frequency of deadly heat, to the height of the sea, to the yield of rice fields and the reach of disease-carrying mosquitoes. And within any level of warming, preparation saves lives. On Simeulue Island, off the coast of Aceh, an oral tradition about the tsunami of 1907 taught islanders to run to the hills when the ground shook and the sea withdrew. When the far larger tsunami of December 2004 struck, seven people died out of a population of about 78,000 (McAdoo et al., 2006). Knowledge, passed on and acted upon, is a survival tool.

## Why this book

There are many excellent books about climate science and many practical manuals about disaster preparedness. Few connect the two, and fewer still are written from the perspective of the tropics — of countries like Indonesia, where the population is large, the coastline is long, the economy is growing and the margin for error is thin. This book tries to fill that gap. It moves from the science of warming, through the specific hazards that warming amplifies, to the concrete steps that households, communities and societies can take.

The book has three commitments. First, every significant factual claim is drawn from a published, checkable source: the assessment reports of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), peer-reviewed journals, and official Indonesian data from agencies such as BMKG, BNPB and the Ministry of Health. Sources are cited in the text by author and year and listed in full at the end. Where the evidence is uncertain, the text says so.

Second, the book is practical. Understanding the greenhouse effect is valuable, but it does not keep a grandmother cool during a heat wave or protect a family's documents during a flood. Each chapter in Parts III and IV ends with actions that can be taken this week, this year and this decade.

Third, the book treats resilience as a collective achievement. Individuals can prepare, but no household is resilient alone. The most robust protection against climate shocks has always been strong communities, competent institutions and fair policies. The Indonesian tradition of *gotong royong* — mutual cooperation — is not a sentimental idea in this context; it is a proven strategy.

## How to use this book

The book is organised in five parts.

**Part I, Understanding the Crisis,** explains how we know the climate is changing, where it is heading, and why Indonesia and Southeast Asia are especially exposed.

**Part II, The Hazards,** examines heat, water, food, health and the cascading risks that link them to the economy, migration and conflict.

**Part III, Preparing Households,** is a practical guide to assessing your own risk and preparing your home, family and finances.

**Part IV, Stronger Together,** looks at communities, cities and villages, technology and mental health.

**Part V, Changing the Trajectory,** turns to policy, finance and justice, to the skills of thinking clearly in an age of misinformation, and finally to a personal plan for a changing century.

Readers who want the practical material first can begin with Part III and return to the science later. The appendices contain checklists, emergency contacts and a glossary of technical terms. Boxes marked **Key points** summarise each chapter; boxes marked **Indonesia focus** highlight local data and examples; boxes marked **Take action** list concrete steps.

A note on numbers: climate statistics are revised every year as new data arrive. The figures here reflect sources published up to mid-2026. The trends they describe are robust; the exact values will change. Readers are encouraged to check the latest reports, and Chapter 18 explains how.

The future described in this book is demanding. But it is not yet written. What we know, what we prepare, and what we choose to do together will shape it.
