# The Mind in Crisis: Anxiety, Trauma and Psychological Resilience

::: lead
Climate change affects not only bodies, homes and livelihoods but minds. Fear, grief, trauma and exhaustion are natural responses to a threatening situation. Learning to recognise and manage them is part of surviving the future.
:::

## Climate anxiety

Many people, especially the young, feel worry, fear, anger, sadness, guilt or helplessness when they think about climate change. A 2021 survey of 10,000 young people aged 16 to 25 in ten countries found that 59 percent were very or extremely worried about climate change, and more than 45 percent said their feelings about climate change negatively affected their daily lives and functioning. Many felt betrayed by governments' inaction (Hickman et al., 2021).

Climate anxiety is not a mental illness. It is a rational emotional response to a real threat, and it can motivate action. But when it becomes overwhelming — when it disrupts sleep, concentration, relationships or the ability to function — it needs attention.

Helpful strategies include:

- **Acknowledge feelings.** Naming emotions and sharing them with others reduces their intensity. You are not alone.
- **Balance information.** Stay informed, but limit exposure to distressing news, especially before sleep. Seek out stories of solutions and progress as well as problems.
- **Take action.** Action is one of the most effective antidotes to anxiety. Choose actions within your control — preparing your household, joining a community group, changing habits, supporting good policies. Collective action is particularly powerful because it builds connection as well as impact.
- **Connect with nature.** Spending time in green spaces, gardens, parks or by the sea supports mental well-being.
- **Look after the basics.** Sleep, physical activity, healthy food and time with loved ones build resilience to stress.
- **Seek help when needed.** If anxiety becomes overwhelming, talk to a health professional.

## Trauma after disasters

Disasters can be deeply traumatic. Survivors may have faced the threat of death, lost loved ones, homes and possessions, witnessed terrible events or been separated from family. In the days and weeks afterwards, most people experience some distress: shock, fear, sadness, anger, guilt, difficulty sleeping, nightmares, being easily startled, or feeling numb. These are normal reactions to abnormal events, and for most people they gradually fade.

For a minority, distress persists or worsens, developing into conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, anxiety disorders or substance misuse. Signs that professional help may be needed include symptoms lasting more than a month, severe difficulty functioning at home, school or work, thoughts of self-harm or suicide, and heavy use of alcohol or drugs.

## Children and young people

Children are particularly vulnerable after disasters, but they are also remarkably resilient when they receive support. They may react by becoming clingy, regressing to earlier behaviours (bedwetting, thumb-sucking), having nightmares, being irritable or withdrawn, or re-enacting the disaster in play.

Adults can help by:

- staying calm and reassuring children that they are safe and that adults are taking care of them;
- restoring routines — meals, sleep, school, play — as quickly as possible;
- listening and answering questions honestly, in age-appropriate language;
- limiting exposure to disturbing images on television and social media;
- giving children simple, meaningful tasks to help, which restores a sense of control;
- watching for signs that a child needs extra support.

Schools play a crucial role in recovery, providing routine, social contact, safe spaces and access to psychosocial support.

## Psychological first aid

Psychological first aid (PFA) is a humane, supportive response to people who are suffering after a crisis. It is not professional counselling; anyone can learn it. The World Health Organization's guide summarises PFA in three action principles: **Look, Listen and Link** (WHO, 2011).

- **Look** — check for safety, identify people with obvious urgent basic needs, and notice people showing signs of serious distress.
- **Listen** — approach people who may need support, ask about their needs and concerns, listen without pressuring them to talk, and help them feel calm.
- **Link** — help people address basic needs and access services, help them cope with problems, give accurate information, and connect them with loved ones and social support.

PFA emphasises what *not* to do as well: do not force people to talk about what happened, do not make promises you cannot keep, do not give false information, and do not judge people's reactions.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: psychosocial support after disasters" kind="local"}
After major disasters in Indonesia — the 2004 Aceh tsunami, the 2006 Yogyakarta earthquake, the 2018 Lombok earthquakes and the 2018 Central Sulawesi earthquake, tsunami and liquefaction — psychosocial support programmes involving health workers, psychologists, teachers, religious leaders and volunteers have helped communities recover. Religious faith, community rituals and the support of extended families are important sources of resilience for many Indonesians. The Ministry of Health, the Indonesian Psychological Association (HIMPSI), PMI and many NGOs provide psychosocial support services, and community health centres (*puskesmas*) can refer people to mental health care.
:::

## Building psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is the ability to adapt well to adversity, trauma and stress. It is not a fixed trait but a set of skills and resources that can be developed. Research on resilience after disasters highlights several factors (Hobfoll et al., 2007):

1. **A sense of safety** — physical safety and the feeling of being safe.
2. **Calming** — strategies to reduce physiological arousal, such as breathing exercises, relaxation and routine.
3. **Self- and community efficacy** — the belief that one's actions, individually and collectively, can make a difference.
4. **Connectedness** — relationships with family, friends and community.
5. **Hope** — the belief that things will work out, grounded in realistic expectations.

Preparation itself builds resilience. People who have planned and practised for emergencies feel more in control and respond more effectively, which in turn reduces trauma.

## Supporting the supporters

Health workers, rescuers, volunteers, community leaders and family caregivers carry heavy burdens during and after disasters. They are at risk of burnout, secondary trauma and exhaustion. Organisations should provide rest, rotation, debriefing and access to support; individuals should recognise their limits, take breaks and seek help. Looking after oneself is not selfish; it is necessary to keep helping others.

## From despair to purpose

There is a difference between optimism (expecting things to turn out well) and hope (believing that one's actions can make a difference, even in difficult circumstances). The climate crisis calls for hope of the second kind — "active hope" grounded in reality and expressed through action. Many people who work on climate change find that engagement, community and purpose transform their anxiety into determination.

::: {.box title="Take action: a breathing exercise for stressful moments" kind="action"}
Sit comfortably. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of four. Hold for a count of four. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Repeat five to ten times. Slow breathing activates the body's relaxation response and can help in moments of acute stress — before an evacuation, after receiving bad news, or when anxiety rises at night.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Climate anxiety is a rational response; action, connection and balanced information help.
- Most people recover from disaster distress; some need professional help.
- Children need safety, routine, honest information and meaningful tasks.
- Psychological first aid: Look, Listen, Link.
- Safety, calm, efficacy, connectedness and hope build resilience.
:::
