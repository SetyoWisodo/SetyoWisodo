# Health in a Hotter World

::: lead
The World Health Organization has called climate change the single biggest health threat facing humanity. It acts through heat, disease, air, water, food and the mind — and it strains the very systems meant to protect us.
:::

## Many pathways, one body

Climate change affects health directly, through heat, floods and storms, and indirectly, through changes in infectious diseases, air quality, water and food security, displacement and mental health. These pathways interact. A heat wave raises demand on hospitals at the same time as it causes power cuts; a flood spreads waterborne disease while damaging clinics and roads; a drought reduces food intake and forces families to drink unsafe water.

The *Lancet* Countdown, an annual assessment by more than 100 experts, tracks dozens of indicators of how climate change affects health. Its 2025 report found that many indicators had reached record levels, including heat-related deaths, labour losses and the climatic suitability for dengue transmission (Romanello et al., 2025).

## Dengue and other mosquito-borne diseases

Dengue is the fastest-spreading mosquito-borne viral disease in the world, and Indonesia carries one of the heaviest burdens. The virus is transmitted mainly by the *Aedes aegypti* mosquito, which breeds in clean standing water in and around homes — in water storage jars, flower vases, discarded tyres, gutters and plastic waste. Warmer temperatures speed up the mosquito's life cycle and shorten the time the virus needs to replicate inside it, making transmission more efficient. Rainfall creates breeding sites, but so does drought, when households store water in open containers.

Indonesia recorded its highest-ever number of dengue cases in 2024: more than 250,000 cases and around 1,400 deaths (Kemenkes, 2025). Most dengue infections cause mild illness or none at all, but a small proportion progress to severe dengue, with bleeding, plasma leakage and shock. Warning signs — severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, restlessness or lethargy — usually appear as the fever falls, around days three to seven. Anyone with these signs needs urgent medical care.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: Wolbachia mosquitoes" kind="local"}
In Yogyakarta, researchers from Universitas Gadjah Mada and the World Mosquito Program tested a novel approach: releasing *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes carrying *Wolbachia*, a naturally occurring bacterium that blocks the dengue virus from replicating. In a randomised controlled trial, areas receiving *Wolbachia* mosquitoes saw a 77 percent reduction in dengue cases and an 86 percent reduction in dengue hospitalisations (Utarini et al., 2021). The approach has since been expanded to other Indonesian cities. It complements, rather than replaces, the classic *3M Plus* approach: *menguras* (drain), *menutup* (cover) and *mendaur ulang* (recycle) water containers, plus measures such as repellents and larvicides.
:::

Malaria, once widespread in Indonesia, has been eliminated from many districts but remains endemic in parts of eastern Indonesia, particularly Papua. Changing temperature and rainfall patterns can shift its range, including to higher elevations. Chikungunya and Zika, also spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes, may follow similar patterns to dengue.

## Waterborne and foodborne diseases

Floods contaminate drinking water with sewage; droughts concentrate pathogens and push people towards unsafe sources; heat accelerates the growth of bacteria in food and water. Diarrhoeal diseases, including cholera and typhoid, rise after floods and during hot periods, especially where sanitation is poor. Leptospirosis, a bacterial infection spread through water contaminated by the urine of rats and other animals, often surges after floods in Indonesian cities.

Basic protection is effective: safe drinking water (boiled, filtered or treated), handwashing with soap, safe food storage, and proper sanitation. Oral rehydration solution — clean water with the right balance of salt and sugar — prevents most deaths from diarrhoea. The WHO's home recipe is one litre of clean water, six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt; commercial oral rehydration salts (*oralit*) are better where available.

## Air: smoke, haze and pollution

Climate change and air pollution are closely linked. Burning fossil fuels causes both. Heat speeds up the formation of ground-level ozone. Droughts increase the risk of forest and peatland fires, whose smoke contains fine particles (PM2.5) that penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream. Haze episodes in Indonesia have caused surges in respiratory infections, asthma attacks and cardiovascular problems, and long-term exposure to fine particles shortens lives. The *Lancet* Countdown estimated that fine-particle pollution from wildfire smoke caused a record 154,000 deaths worldwide in 2024 (Romanello et al., 2025).

During haze or heavy pollution, stay indoors with windows closed where possible, reduce physical exertion, use a properly fitted N95 or equivalent mask when outdoors (ordinary cloth or surgical masks offer little protection against fine particles), and seek medical advice if you have asthma, heart or lung disease. Air quality information is available from BMKG and the Ministry of Environment.

## Heat and chronic disease

Chapter 4 covered heat illness. Beyond acute heat stroke, heat worsens many chronic conditions. It strains the heart, raises the risk of heart attacks and strokes, worsens kidney disease (repeated dehydration damages the kidneys, and chronic kidney disease among agricultural workers in hot regions is a growing concern), and is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth. Heat also affects mental health, with studies linking high temperatures to increases in emergency visits for mental health conditions, aggression and suicide.

## Mental health

Disasters cause trauma, grief and loss. People who lose homes, livelihoods or loved ones in floods, fires or storms face elevated risks of depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder. Slow-onset changes — repeated crop failures, loss of land to the sea, the disappearance of familiar landscapes — cause chronic stress and a form of grief sometimes called *solastalgia*: distress at the degradation of one's home environment. And many people, especially the young, experience anxiety about the future of the planet. Chapter 16 addresses these issues and how to cope with them.

## Health systems under strain

Hospitals and clinics are themselves vulnerable. Floods can inundate ground floors, damage equipment and cut access; heat can overwhelm cooling systems and spoil medicines and vaccines; power cuts disable equipment; and surges in patients during heat waves, epidemics or disasters stretch staff thin. Climate-resilient health systems need robust infrastructure (raised generators, backup power and water, flood-proof designs), surveillance systems that link climate data with disease data to predict outbreaks, trained staff, stockpiles of essential supplies, and strong primary care that reaches communities before crises escalate.

The health sector is also a source of emissions — globally around 4–5 percent of the total — and can lead by example through energy efficiency, renewable power and reducing waste.

::: {.box title="Take action: protecting your family's health" kind="action"}
- Practise *3M Plus* weekly: drain, cover and recycle water containers; use repellent and window screens.
- Know the warning signs of severe dengue and seek care immediately if they appear.
- Keep oral rehydration salts, a thermometer and basic medicines at home.
- During haze, stay indoors when possible and use N95 masks outdoors.
- After floods, avoid wading in water, use boots and gloves for cleaning, and watch for fever and muscle pain.
- Keep vaccinations up to date and keep copies of medical records in your emergency documents.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Climate change affects health through heat, infectious disease, air, water, food and mental health.
- Dengue reached record levels in Indonesia in 2024; *Wolbachia* mosquitoes cut cases by 77 percent in a Yogyakarta trial.
- Floods bring diarrhoeal disease and leptospirosis; safe water and hygiene are the best defences.
- Wildfire smoke and pollution harm lungs and hearts; N95 masks protect against fine particles.
- Health systems must be strengthened to withstand climate shocks.
:::
