# Water: Too Much, Too Little

::: lead
Most climate impacts reach people through water: through floods that destroy homes, droughts that empty wells, storms that surge ashore and salt that creeps into fields and aquifers.
:::

## A more intense water cycle

The physics is simple. Warmer air can hold about 7 percent more water vapour for every degree Celsius of warming. More moisture in the atmosphere means that when conditions favour rain, more of it can fall in a short time. At the same time, higher temperatures increase evaporation from soils, plants and reservoirs, drying the land faster between rains. The result is an intensified water cycle: wetter wet periods, drier dry periods, and more abrupt swings between them.

The IPCC concludes that heavy precipitation has become more frequent and intense over most land regions with good observational coverage, and that this trend will continue with further warming (IPCC, 2021). Agricultural and ecological droughts — periods when soils are too dry for crops and plants — have increased in some regions and will become more severe in many others.

## Floods

Floods come in several forms, each with different warning times and dangers.

**River floods** occur when prolonged rain fills river channels beyond capacity. They usually develop over days, allowing time for warnings, but can affect very large areas.

**Flash floods** develop within hours, or even minutes, of intense rain, especially in steep catchments and urban areas with sealed surfaces. They are the deadliest type because they leave little time to react.

**Urban (pluvial) floods** occur when rainfall overwhelms drainage systems, regardless of river levels. They are increasingly common as cities expand over natural wetlands and green spaces.

**Coastal floods** result from high tides, storm surges and waves, increasingly aggravated by sea-level rise and subsidence. Tidal flooding along Java's north coast is a daily reality in some neighbourhoods.

Floodwater is dangerous in ways people often underestimate. Just 15 centimetres of fast-moving water can knock an adult off their feet; 60 centimetres can carry away a car. Floodwater may hide open drains, debris and downed power lines, and is often contaminated with sewage, chemicals and disease-causing organisms such as the bacteria that cause leptospirosis.

::: {.box title="Take action: during a flood" kind="action"}
- Follow official warnings and evacuation orders from BPBD, BNPB and BMKG.
- Move people, documents and valuables to higher floors or higher ground early.
- Switch off electricity at the main switch if water is likely to enter the house — but never touch electrical equipment while standing in water.
- Never walk, swim or drive through floodwater. "Turn around, don't drown."
- Keep children away from floodwater and drains.
- After the flood, wear boots and gloves when cleaning; disinfect surfaces; discard food that has touched floodwater; and watch for fever, muscle pain or jaundice, which may signal leptospirosis.
:::

## Landslides

In mountainous and hilly regions, intense rain saturates soils and triggers landslides, especially on slopes stripped of forest, cut for roads, or loaded with houses and terraces. Indonesia experiences hundreds of landslides each year. Warning signs include new cracks in the ground or walls, tilting trees or posts, doors and windows that suddenly jam, springs that appear or become muddy, and rumbling sounds from the slope. Communities on steep slopes should identify safe areas and evacuation routes, monitor rainfall and warning signs during the rainy season, and avoid building at the foot or crest of steep slopes.

## Drought and water scarcity

Drought is a slow-onset disaster. It begins with below-normal rainfall (meteorological drought), then dries the soil (agricultural drought), lowers rivers, reservoirs and groundwater (hydrological drought), and finally affects incomes, food supply and health (socioeconomic drought). Because it develops gradually, its impact is easy to underestimate until wells run dry and crops fail.

In Indonesia, droughts are closely linked to El Niño and positive phases of the Indian Ocean Dipole. During these years, the dry season starts earlier, lasts longer and is more intense, especially in Java, Bali, Nusa Tenggara and southern parts of Sumatra, Kalimantan and Sulawesi. Village wells dry up, women and children spend more time collecting water, farmers delay planting, and the risk of fires rises.

Water scarcity is not only about rainfall. It also reflects how water is managed: leaking pipes, polluted rivers, deforested catchments, over-extracted aquifers and unequal access. In many places, improving water management offers greater gains than any new source of supply.

## Salt in the water

As sea levels rise and groundwater is pumped from coastal aquifers, seawater moves inland underground. Coastal wells become brackish, rice fields and fish ponds become too salty, and drinking water must be bought or trucked in. River deltas face saltwater pushing further upstream during dry seasons. Salinity is a quiet but serious threat to coastal food production and health — high salt intake from drinking water has been linked to high blood pressure, including in pregnant women.

Responses include stopping excessive groundwater extraction, harvesting rainwater, planting salt-tolerant rice varieties, shifting from rice to aquaculture or salt-tolerant crops in affected areas, and protecting mangroves and coastal wetlands that buffer the land from the sea.

## Storms and storm surges

Indonesia lies mostly near the equator, where tropical cyclones rarely form because the Earth's rotation is too weak to spin them up. However, cyclones do affect the southern parts of the archipelago. In April 2021, Tropical Cyclone Seroja caused devastating floods and landslides in East Nusa Tenggara, killing more than 180 people. Cyclones passing near Indonesia can also bring heavy rain, high waves and strong winds far from their centres. Globally, the proportion of tropical cyclones reaching the most intense categories is projected to increase with warming, and rainfall rates within them will rise (IPCC, 2021). Combined with higher sea levels, storm surges will reach further inland.

## Securing water at home and in the community

Water security begins at home and in the neighbourhood. Practical measures include:

- **Rainwater harvesting** — collecting runoff from roofs in clean, covered tanks provides a backup supply during dry spells and reduces flooding. Tanks must be covered to prevent mosquito breeding.
- **Infiltration wells and biopores** — small holes or wells that allow rainwater to soak into the ground recharge groundwater and reduce runoff. Many Indonesian cities promote *sumur resapan* and *lubang biopori*.
- **Water storage for emergencies** — keeping enough safe drinking water for at least three days, ideally longer (see Chapter 10).
- **Household water treatment** — boiling, filtering or chlorinating water when supply is uncertain.
- **Protecting springs and catchments** — community forest and spring protection, as practised in many Indonesian villages, preserves water at its source.
- **Reducing waste** — fixing leaks and reusing greywater for gardens.

At the city scale, the key measures are expanding reliable piped water to stop groundwater extraction, restoring rivers and retention ponds, keeping drains free of waste, preserving green space and wetlands, and planning development away from floodplains.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: the sponge city idea" kind="local"}
Instead of relying only on concrete channels to push water away as fast as possible, "sponge city" approaches — pioneered at scale in China — use parks, wetlands, permeable pavements, green roofs and retention ponds to absorb, store and slowly release rainwater. Elements of this approach are visible in Indonesian programmes for retention ponds (*waduk* and *embung*), river normalisation combined with green space, and infiltration wells. The approach works best when combined with strict control of building on floodplains and riverbanks.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Warmer air holds more moisture, intensifying both heavy rain and drought.
- Flash floods are the deadliest flood type; never enter floodwater.
- Drought develops slowly; El Niño years bring higher risk in much of Indonesia.
- Sea-level rise and groundwater pumping push salt into coastal water and soils.
- Rainwater harvesting, infiltration, storage and good management build water security.
:::
