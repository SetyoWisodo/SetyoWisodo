# Understanding the Crisis {.part number="I"}

# A Warming World: What We Know and How We Know It

::: lead
Climate change is not a matter of belief. It rests on physics understood for more than 150 years, on measurements taken every day across the planet, and on independent lines of evidence that all point in the same direction.
:::

## A discovery older than the light bulb

The idea that certain gases warm the Earth is not new. In the 1820s the French mathematician Joseph Fourier reasoned that the planet was warmer than it should be, given its distance from the Sun, and suggested that the atmosphere acted as an insulating layer. In 1856 the American scientist Eunice Foote showed that a glass cylinder filled with carbon dioxide heated more in sunlight than one filled with ordinary air. Three years later, the Irish physicist John Tyndall measured precisely how water vapour and carbon dioxide absorb infrared radiation — the heat that the Earth's surface gives off. And in 1896 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius calculated that doubling the amount of carbon dioxide in the air would warm the planet by several degrees.

These scientists were not activists. They were curious about how the world worked, and the physics they uncovered has been confirmed thousands of times since. Today, the same principles are used to design heat-seeking sensors, weather satellites and the infrared thermometers found in clinics.

## How the greenhouse effect works

Sunlight arrives at the Earth mostly as visible light. About 30 percent is reflected back to space by clouds, ice and bright surfaces; the rest is absorbed by the land, the ocean and the atmosphere, warming them. A warm surface emits energy in turn, but at longer, infrared wavelengths. Nitrogen and oxygen, which make up 99 percent of dry air, are transparent to this infrared radiation. Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and a few other gases are not. They absorb the outgoing heat and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the surface.

This natural greenhouse effect is essential to life. Without it, the average surface temperature of the Earth would be around −18 °C instead of about 15 °C. The problem is not the existence of the greenhouse effect but its intensification. By burning coal, oil and gas, clearing forests and draining peatlands, humanity has added enough greenhouse gases to thicken the insulating blanket measurably.

::: {.box title="Key gases at a glance" kind="note"}
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** — from burning fossil fuels, cement production and deforestation. The largest single driver of warming. A substantial fraction of each emission remains in the atmosphere for centuries.
- **Methane (CH₄)** — from livestock, rice paddies, landfills, wetlands and leaks from oil, gas and coal operations. About 80 times more powerful than CO₂ over 20 years, but it breaks down within about a decade.
- **Nitrous oxide (N₂O)** — mainly from nitrogen fertilisers. Long-lived and potent.
- **Water vapour** — the most abundant greenhouse gas, but it acts as an amplifier rather than a driver: warmer air holds more moisture (about 7 percent more per degree Celsius), which traps more heat.
:::

## The rise in carbon dioxide

In 1958 the American scientist Charles David Keeling began measuring carbon dioxide at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii, high above local sources of pollution. His record, now known as the Keeling Curve, is one of the most important data sets in science. It shows a steady saw-tooth rise: concentrations dip each northern summer as plants absorb carbon and rise each winter, while the underlying trend climbs relentlessly.

When Keeling began, the concentration was about 315 parts per million (ppm). Before the Industrial Revolution it had been about 278 ppm, a level that ice cores show was never exceeded during the previous 800,000 years. In 2024 the global average reached 422.8 ppm, and the increase during that single year — 3.75 ppm — was the largest ever recorded (NOAA, 2025a). The rise is unambiguously human in origin. Carbon from fossil fuels has a distinctive isotopic signature, and the falling ratio of carbon-13 to carbon-12 in the atmosphere, together with the slow decline in atmospheric oxygen, matches the chemistry of combustion.

## The evidence of warming

If greenhouse gases are trapping more heat, the Earth's energy budget should be out of balance, and that extra energy should show up as warming of the air, the ocean and the land, melting of ice and rising seas. All of these changes are observed.

**Air temperature.** Thermometer records maintained independently by agencies in the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan and Europe agree that the global average surface temperature has risen by more than 1 °C since 1850–1900. The WMO's consolidated analysis puts 2024 at about 1.55 °C above that baseline and 2025 at about 1.43 °C, the second or third warmest year on record (WMO, 2026). Each of the past four decades has been warmer than any decade before it.

**Ocean heat.** The ocean has absorbed around 91 percent of the excess energy trapped since the 1970s. Ocean heat content reached a new record in 2025, and the rate of ocean warming over the past two decades is more than double that seen earlier (WMO, 2026). Because water expands as it warms, this heat also raises sea level.

**Ice.** Mountain glaciers are retreating on every continent, including the small tropical glaciers near Puncak Jaya in Papua, which have shrunk dramatically and are expected to disappear entirely. The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass. Arctic sea ice in late summer covers far less area than it did in the 1980s.

**Sea level.** Global mean sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC, 2021). The rate is accelerating: from 2012 to 2025, it averaged about 4.75 mm per year, nearly twice the rate of 1993–2011 (WMO, 2026).

**Extremes.** Hot days and nights have become more frequent and cold extremes less frequent across most land areas. Heavy rainfall has intensified in many regions, consistent with a warmer atmosphere holding more moisture.

## How we know humans are responsible

Natural factors do influence the climate. The Sun's output varies slightly; volcanic eruptions inject particles that temporarily cool the planet; ocean cycles such as El Niño and La Niña shift heat between the ocean and the atmosphere. Scientists have examined each of these explanations carefully. Solar output has been flat or slightly declining since the 1950s, while temperatures have risen. Volcanoes cause cooling, not warming, and their effects fade within a few years. El Niño and La Niña move heat around but do not create it.

Several fingerprints point specifically to greenhouse gases. The upper atmosphere (the stratosphere) is cooling while the lower atmosphere warms — the pattern expected when heat is trapped near the surface, not when the Sun brightens. Nights are warming faster than days. Satellite measurements show less infrared radiation escaping to space at exactly the wavelengths absorbed by carbon dioxide.

Based on this evidence, the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report concluded in 2021 that "it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land" (IPCC, 2021). A 2025 update by more than 60 scientists estimated that human activity was responsible for about 1.36 °C of warming in 2024, with the remainder of that year's heat due to natural variability, chiefly El Niño. Human-caused warming is now increasing at about 0.27 °C per decade, a rate unprecedented in the instrumental record (Forster et al., 2025).

::: {.box title="Scientific consensus" kind="note"}
Surveys of the peer-reviewed literature have repeatedly found overwhelming agreement among climate scientists. A 2021 analysis of more than 88,000 studies found that over 99 percent of papers agreed that recent climate change is mainly caused by humans (Lynas et al., 2021). Every national academy of science of a major country, and every major scientific organisation in the relevant fields, accepts this conclusion.
:::

## Attribution: connecting extremes to warming

A newer branch of science, called extreme-event attribution, asks how climate change has altered the likelihood or intensity of a particular heat wave, flood or drought. Researchers compare the real world with simulations of a world without human greenhouse gas emissions. The results are now routine. The IPCC estimates that a hot extreme that occurred once every 50 years in the pre-industrial climate now occurs nearly five times as often, and would occur almost nine times as often at 1.5 °C of warming and about 14 times as often at 2 °C (IPCC, 2021).

Attribution matters because it turns an abstract global average into specific, local consequences. When a heat wave kills hundreds of people, when a monsoon dumps unprecedented rain, it is now possible to say with quantified confidence how much more likely climate change made it.

## Certainty and uncertainty

It is important to be honest about what science does not know. Clouds remain a major source of uncertainty: depending on how they respond to warming, they could amplify or dampen it somewhat. The precise behaviour of ice sheets under sustained warming is uncertain, which makes long-term sea-level projections wide. Regional rainfall changes are harder to predict than temperature changes, especially in the tropics.

But uncertainty is not ignorance, and it cuts both ways. The central estimate for how much the planet warms when carbon dioxide doubles — about 3 °C — has been refined over decades, and the IPCC now judges it very unlikely to be below 2 °C (IPCC, 2021). Uncertainty about ice sheets means sea-level rise could be considerably worse than central projections, not only better. A prudent person does not wait for perfect knowledge before buying insurance or fastening a seat belt.

## The remaining budget

Because carbon dioxide accumulates, global temperature rises roughly in proportion to the total amount emitted. This gives rise to the idea of a carbon budget: the amount of carbon dioxide that can still be released while keeping warming below a given level. At the start of 2025, the remaining budget for a 50 percent chance of limiting warming to 1.5 °C was estimated at about 130 billion tonnes of CO₂ — little more than three years of emissions at the current rate of around 42 billion tonnes per year (Forster et al., 2025). In practical terms, the world is very likely to exceed 1.5 °C of long-term warming around 2030.

This does not mean the effort is futile. Every tonne of carbon dioxide avoided reduces future warming. The difference between 1.6 °C and 2.0 °C, or between 2.0 °C and 2.7 °C, is measured in millions of lives and livelihoods. As the WMO's Secretary-General Celeste Saulo put it in late 2025, it is now "virtually impossible" to avoid temporarily overshooting 1.5 °C, but "it's still entirely possible and essential to bring temperatures back down to 1.5 °C by the end of the century" (WMO, 2025a).

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- The greenhouse effect is basic physics, measured since the 1850s.
- CO₂ has risen from about 278 ppm to nearly 424 ppm, entirely because of human activity.
- The world has warmed by roughly 1.4 °C; 2024 was the hottest year on record at about 1.55 °C.
- Multiple independent fingerprints show the warming is human-caused.
- The 1.5 °C carbon budget is nearly exhausted, but every fraction of a degree avoided matters.
:::
