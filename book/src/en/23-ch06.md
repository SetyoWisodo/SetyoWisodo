# Food Systems Under Stress

::: lead
Climate change threatens food at every step — in the field, on the boat, in storage, on the road and at the market — and it hits hardest those who already spend most of their income on food.
:::

## Food security in four dimensions

Food security exists, in the definition used by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), when all people at all times have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food. It has four dimensions: **availability** (is enough food produced?), **access** (can people afford it and reach it?), **utilisation** (is it safe and nutritious, and can the body use it?), and **stability** (are the other three reliable over time?). Climate change affects all four.

## Crops in a warmer world

Plants are sensitive to temperature at critical stages. High temperatures during flowering can sterilise pollen; hot nights increase respiration, burning energy that would otherwise fill grains; drought stunts growth; floods drown seedlings; and new pest and disease patterns emerge. A large synthesis of studies estimated that, without effective adaptation, each degree Celsius of global warming reduces average global yields of maize by about 7.4 percent, wheat by 6.0 percent, rice by 3.2 percent and soybeans by 3.1 percent (Zhao et al., 2017).

Rice deserves special attention in Indonesia, where it is the staple food and the basis of rural livelihoods. Research at the International Rice Research Institute found that rice yields fell by about 10 percent for each 1 °C increase in night-time temperature during the dry season (Peng et al., 2004). Rice is also vulnerable to drought, to flooding and to saltwater intrusion in coastal areas. At the same time, flooded rice paddies are a significant source of methane, so rice systems sit at the centre of both adaptation and mitigation.

Carbon dioxide itself has mixed effects. Higher concentrations can boost photosynthesis in some crops, but experiments show that rice and wheat grown under elevated carbon dioxide contain lower concentrations of protein, zinc and iron — a hidden threat to nutrition for populations that depend on these staples.

## Fisheries and aquaculture

Fish are an essential source of protein and micronutrients for Indonesians, and the fisheries sector supports millions of livelihoods. Warming waters, marine heat waves, coral bleaching, ocean acidification and declining oxygen in parts of the ocean are reshaping marine life. Many fish species are shifting towards cooler, deeper or more distant waters, and tropical regions are projected to see some of the largest declines in potential catch (Cheung et al., 2010). Small-scale fishers with small boats cannot easily follow fish further offshore, and rougher seas reduce the number of days they can safely work.

Aquaculture — fish, shrimp and seaweed farming — has grown rapidly in Indonesia and offers opportunities, but coastal ponds are exposed to flooding, salinity changes and disease outbreaks linked to temperature. Seaweed farms, important for many coastal communities, have suffered from heat-related disease ("ice-ice") during warm periods.

## Price shocks and access

For poor households, the most immediate impact of climate change on food is often price. When droughts or floods damage harvests in a major producing region, prices rise locally and sometimes globally. Rice, cooking oil, chillies, shallots and other staples in Indonesia are sensitive to weather, and price spikes in these commodities are felt immediately in household budgets. Poor families spend a much larger share of their income on food, so a price increase that is an inconvenience for a middle-class household can force a poor family to eat less, switch to cheaper and less nutritious foods, sell assets, or take children out of school.

Global food markets connect distant weather events. In 2007–2008 and again in 2010–2011, a combination of droughts in exporting countries, export restrictions, high energy prices and speculation drove sharp rises in world food prices, contributing to unrest in several countries. As climate extremes become more frequent, the chance of simultaneous harvest failures in multiple breadbaskets rises.

## Nutrition and health

Food insecurity is not only about calories. Indonesia still faces a significant burden of childhood stunting — children who are too short for their age because of chronic undernutrition — alongside rising obesity and diet-related disease. Climate shocks can worsen both by reducing access to diverse, nutritious foods and pushing families towards cheap, energy-dense processed foods. Heat can also spoil food faster, increasing the risk of food poisoning, especially where refrigeration is limited.

## Adapting farming

Farmers have always adapted to variable weather. The challenge now is to adapt faster and further. Proven approaches include:

- **Improved varieties** — rice and other crops bred for tolerance to heat, drought, flooding (for example "submergence-tolerant" rice that survives two weeks under water) and salinity.
- **Water management** — small reservoirs (*embung*), efficient irrigation, and alternate wetting and drying in rice paddies, which saves water and reduces methane emissions.
- **Adjusted planting calendars** — using seasonal forecasts from BMKG to time planting. Indonesia's *Kalender Tanam* (planting calendar) system and climate field schools (*Sekolah Lapang Iklim*) help farmers interpret forecasts.
- **Diversification** — growing a mix of crops, integrating trees, livestock and fish ponds (as in traditional *mina padi* rice-fish systems), and diversifying income sources.
- **Soil health** — organic matter, cover crops and reduced burning improve the soil's ability to hold water.
- **Crop insurance** — Indonesia's subsidised rice farm insurance (*Asuransi Usaha Tani Padi*) compensates farmers for losses from floods, droughts and pests, though uptake remains limited.
- **Post-harvest improvements** — better drying, storage and cold chains reduce losses that grow as heat and humidity rise.

## Food systems and emissions

Food systems — including land clearing, agriculture, processing, transport and waste — account for roughly a third of global greenhouse gas emissions. In Indonesia, the conversion of forests and peatlands for agriculture has been a major source of emissions. Reducing food loss and waste, protecting forests and peatlands, improving fertiliser use, managing rice paddy water and shifting diets towards more plant-based foods where appropriate can cut emissions while also improving resilience.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: local food diversity" kind="local"}
Before rice became dominant almost everywhere, many Indonesian regions relied on diverse staples: sago in Maluku and Papua, maize in Madura and Nusa Tenggara, cassava, sweet potato, sorghum and taro elsewhere. Many of these crops are more tolerant of drought or poor soils than rice. Renewed interest in local food diversity (*diversifikasi pangan*) is not nostalgia; it is a strategy for reducing dependence on a single climate-sensitive crop.
:::

## What households can do

Households cannot control global food markets, but they can reduce their exposure. Keeping a modest stock of non-perishable staples (see Chapter 10), growing vegetables, herbs or fruit trees where space allows, joining or supporting local farmers' groups and cooperatives, reducing food waste, and learning to cook with a variety of staples all increase resilience. At the community level, village food barns (*lumbung pangan*), cooperative purchasing, and links between urban consumers and nearby farmers can buffer price shocks.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Each degree of warming reduces average global yields of major crops, including rice.
- Hot nights, drought, flooding and salinity are key threats to Indonesian rice.
- Fisheries are shifting as seas warm; small-scale fishers are especially vulnerable.
- Price shocks hit poor households hardest and can worsen nutrition.
- Improved varieties, water management, forecasts, diversification and insurance help farmers adapt.
:::
