# The Pathways Ahead: Scenarios, Tipping Points and Committed Change

::: lead
The future climate is not a single forecast but a range of possibilities. Which one we live in depends on decisions being made now — though some changes are already locked in.
:::

## Why scientists use scenarios

Weather forecasts predict the state of the atmosphere a few days ahead. Climate projections answer a different question: if the world follows a particular path of emissions, land use and development, how will the climate system respond over decades? Because human choices cannot be predicted with physics, scientists use scenarios — internally consistent stories about how population, the economy, technology and policy might evolve.

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report uses a family of scenarios called Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). Each is labelled with a number describing the socioeconomic story and a number describing the approximate heating effect, in watts per square metre, reached by 2100. Five are used most often:

- **SSP1-1.9** — very rapid emission cuts, net-zero CO₂ around 2050, and removal of carbon dioxide thereafter.
- **SSP1-2.6** — strong mitigation, net-zero CO₂ shortly after 2070.
- **SSP2-4.5** — a "middle of the road" world, roughly in line with current policies.
- **SSP3-7.0** — regional rivalry, slow technological change and rising emissions.
- **SSP5-8.5** — fossil-fuelled development with emissions that roughly double by 2050.

## How much warmer?

The table below summarises the IPCC's assessment of global warming in the late twenty-first century under each scenario, relative to 1850–1900 (IPCC, 2021).

| Scenario | Best estimate, 2081–2100 | Very likely range |
|---|---|---|
| SSP1-1.9 | 1.4 °C | 1.0–1.8 °C |
| SSP1-2.6 | 1.8 °C | 1.3–2.4 °C |
| SSP2-4.5 | 2.7 °C | 2.1–3.5 °C |
| SSP3-7.0 | 3.6 °C | 2.8–4.6 °C |
| SSP5-8.5 | 4.4 °C | 3.3–5.7 °C |

Several points stand out. In every scenario, warming continues for at least the next two decades, and the 1.5 °C level is reached or exceeded in the early 2030s or sooner. Only the lowest scenarios bring temperatures back down towards 1.5 °C by 2100, and they depend on removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere on a large scale. The highest scenario now looks less likely than it did a decade ago, thanks to the falling cost of renewable energy and the climate policies already in place. Independent analyses of current policies suggest a world heading for roughly 2.5–3 °C by 2100 — far better than the worst case, but far from safe.

These numbers can sound small. A day that is 3 °C warmer than yesterday hardly feels like a catastrophe. But global averages hide enormous variation. Land warms faster than the ocean; high latitudes warm faster than the tropics; and the extremes change far more than the average. During the last ice age, about 20,000 years ago, the global average was only around 5–6 °C colder than in pre-industrial times — yet much of North America and Northern Europe lay under ice sheets several kilometres thick, and sea level was about 120 metres lower. A few degrees of global change transform the world.

## What changes with each increment

The IPCC emphasises that every increment of warming brings larger and more widespread changes. Compared with 1.5 °C, a world at 2 °C would see:

- more frequent and intense hot extremes over most land areas, with a once-in-fifty-years heat event becoming roughly 14 times as frequent as in the pre-industrial climate, against about 9 times at 1.5 °C;
- heavier rainfall events and more severe droughts in many regions;
- the loss of most tropical coral reefs, which are projected to decline by 70–90 percent at 1.5 °C and by more than 99 percent at 2 °C (IPCC, 2018);
- greater declines in crop yields, fisheries and water availability in vulnerable regions;
- higher sea levels, with the long-term commitment growing substantially.

For a person living in Jakarta, Surabaya or Makassar, these are not abstract differences. They decide how many nights are too hot to sleep without cooling, how often streets flood, whether a fishing family's catch holds steady, and whether the local hospital is overwhelmed with dengue patients.

## Sea level: the slow-motion emergency

Of all climate impacts, sea-level rise is the most certain and the most persistent. It is driven by the thermal expansion of warming seawater and by the melting of glaciers and ice sheets. Both respond slowly, which means that sea level will keep rising for centuries even after temperatures stabilise.

By 2100, global mean sea level is projected to rise by about 0.28–0.55 metres relative to 1995–2014 under the lowest scenario and 0.63–1.01 metres under the highest (IPCC, 2021). These are "likely" ranges; the IPCC cannot rule out a rise approaching 2 metres by 2100 under high emissions if ice-sheet processes that are poorly understood kick in. Over the next 2,000 years, sea level could rise by 2–3 metres if warming is limited to 1.5 °C, and by 19–22 metres with 5 °C of warming.

For low-lying coastal areas, the global average is only part of the story. Local factors — especially land subsidence caused by groundwater extraction, as in northern Jakarta and Semarang — can multiply the effective rise many times over. Chapter 3 returns to this.

## Tipping points

Most climate changes scale roughly with warming: more heat, more impact. But some parts of the Earth system may respond abruptly once a threshold is crossed, shifting to a new state that is difficult or impossible to reverse on human timescales. These are known as tipping points.

Candidates include the collapse of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, which together hold enough ice to raise sea level by more than 10 metres over centuries to millennia; the widespread die-off of warm-water coral reefs; abrupt thawing of permafrost, releasing carbon dioxide and methane; the dieback of parts of the Amazon rainforest; and the weakening or collapse of major ocean circulation patterns such as the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC).

A 2022 assessment concluded that several tipping points could be triggered within the Paris Agreement range of 1.5–2 °C, and that the risk rises sharply beyond that (Armstrong McKay et al., 2022). The 2025 Global Tipping Points Report went further, judging that warm-water coral reefs have likely already passed their thermal tipping point (Lenton et al., 2025). Since early 2023, the world has experienced its fourth and most extensive global coral bleaching event on record, affecting reefs in more than 80 countries and territories (NOAA, 2025b).

Tipping points carry deep uncertainty: scientists cannot say exactly when they will be crossed, and some may take centuries to play out. But the possibility of irreversible change is a powerful argument for limiting warming as much as possible, and for preparing for outcomes beyond the central projections.

::: {.box title="Committed change versus avoidable change" kind="note"}
Some impacts are already locked in by past emissions: several decades of further warming, centuries of sea-level rise, the loss of many glaciers and much coral. Others remain avoidable: how hot the late twenty-first century becomes, how fast the seas rise, and whether additional tipping points are crossed. Adaptation is necessary to cope with committed change; mitigation — cutting emissions — is necessary to avoid the worst of what is still avoidable. They are not alternatives.
:::

## Overshoot and the long view

Because the 1.5 °C budget is almost spent, many scenarios now involve an "overshoot": temperatures exceed 1.5 °C for some decades before being brought back down by reaching net-zero emissions and then removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Overshoot is not harmless. Some damage — species extinctions, ice-sheet loss, sea-level rise — will not reverse when temperatures fall. The longer and higher the overshoot, the greater the irreversible harm.

The WMO estimates an 86 percent chance that at least one year between 2025 and 2029 will exceed 1.5 °C, and a 70 percent chance that the five-year average for that period will do so (WMO, 2025b). Exceeding the threshold in individual years does not mean the Paris goal has formally been breached, which refers to long-term averages. But it signals that the world is entering the zone where the agreement's ambitions become harder to meet.

## Living with deep uncertainty

What should an individual or a community do with this information? Three principles help.

**Plan for the likely, prepare for the plausible.** It is reasonable to plan for a world of roughly 2–3 °C warming by the end of the century, with continued sea-level rise. It is prudent to make sure that critical decisions — where to build a house, what kind of land to buy, where to locate a school or hospital — remain sound if outcomes are worse.

**Favour flexible, low-regret actions.** Many adaptation measures bring benefits under almost any future: protecting mangroves, improving drainage, building emergency savings, strengthening community networks, improving public health systems. They are worth doing regardless of the precise scenario.

**Watch the indicators.** Climate risk changes over time. Paying attention to updated assessments, local observations and official warnings allows plans to be adjusted. A plan that is reviewed annually is far more useful than a perfect plan that is never revisited.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Future warming depends on emissions: from about 1.4 °C to over 4 °C by 2100 across IPCC scenarios; current policies point to roughly 2.5–3 °C.
- Every increment matters: the difference between 1.5 °C and 2 °C is large for heat, coral reefs, food and water.
- Sea level will keep rising for centuries; local subsidence can multiply the effect.
- Tipping points may already be in play for coral reefs; others become more likely above 1.5–2 °C.
- Prepare for the likely, protect against the plausible, and choose low-regret actions.
:::
