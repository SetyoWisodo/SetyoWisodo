# Preparing Households {.part number="III"}

# Know Your Risk: A Personal and Local Assessment

::: lead
Preparation begins with knowing what you are preparing for. A family on a riverbank faces different threats from a family on a hillside or in a high-rise apartment. An hour spent assessing your risks will guide every decision that follows.
:::

## Risk is more than hazard

Disaster specialists describe risk as the product of three factors:

- **Hazard** — the event that could cause harm: a flood, a heat wave, a landslide, a drought.
- **Exposure** — whether people and assets are in harm's way: living in the floodplain, working outdoors, owning a house on a slope.
- **Vulnerability** — how susceptible those people and assets are to harm: the strength of the house, the health of the people, their savings, knowledge and social connections.

This framework is empowering because it shows that risk can be reduced even when the hazard cannot be prevented. We cannot stop the monsoon, but we can avoid building in the floodplain (reducing exposure) or raise the floor and keep documents in waterproof bags (reducing vulnerability). We can also strengthen **capacity**: the skills, resources and networks that allow people to cope and recover.

## Step 1: Identify your hazards

Start by listing the hazards that could affect your home, workplace and the places your family spends time, such as schools. Consider both climate-related and other hazards — in Indonesia, earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanic eruptions must be part of any household plan.

Useful sources of information include:

- **InaRISK** (inarisk.bnpb.go.id and the InaRISK Personal app) — BNPB's portal showing hazard and risk maps for floods, landslides, earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, droughts, forest fires, extreme weather and more, down to the village level.
- **BMKG** (bmkg.go.id and the *Info BMKG* app) — weather forecasts, early warnings for extreme weather, seasonal climate outlooks, earthquake and tsunami information, and air quality.
- **MAGMA Indonesia** (magma.esdm.go.id) — volcanic activity and alert levels from the Centre for Volcanology and Geological Hazard Mitigation (PVMBG), as well as landslide susceptibility information.
- **Local BPBD** (regional disaster management agency) — local hazard maps, evacuation routes and contingency plans.
- **Your own community** — older residents remember past floods, landslides and droughts: how high the water reached, which roads were cut, which slopes moved. This knowledge is invaluable.

## Step 2: Assess your exposure

For each hazard, ask how exposed your household is.

- **Flood:** Is your home in a low-lying area, near a river, canal or coast? Has the neighbourhood flooded before? How high did the water reach? Is the ground floor below street level?
- **Heat:** Is your home poorly ventilated, under a metal roof, or on a top floor? Do family members work outdoors? Is there shade and green space nearby?
- **Landslide:** Is your home on or below a steep slope? Are there cracks in the ground or walls? Has the slope been cut for construction?
- **Drought and water:** Does your water come from a shallow well that dries up in the dry season? Is piped water reliable?
- **Fire and haze:** Do you live near peatlands, forests or plantations that burn in dry years?
- **Coastal hazards:** Are you within reach of tidal flooding, storm surges or tsunami inundation zones?

## Step 3: Assess your vulnerability and capacity

Next, consider the characteristics of your household that make it more or less able to cope:

- **People:** Are there infants, older people, pregnant women, people with disabilities or chronic illnesses, or people who need regular medication? Who would need help to evacuate?
- **Building:** What is the house made of? Is it built to withstand earthquakes and strong winds? Are electrical outlets and appliances above likely flood levels?
- **Finances:** Do you have savings to cover an emergency? Is your income dependent on weather — farming, fishing, outdoor trade? Do you have insurance?
- **Knowledge and skills:** Does everyone know the warning signs, evacuation routes and basic first aid? Can someone swim?
- **Connections:** Do you know your neighbours? Is there a community organisation, RT/RW leadership, mosque, church or village disaster team that would help?

## Step 4: Rank and prioritise

With this information, rank your risks. A simple method is to score each hazard on likelihood (how often might it happen?) and impact (how bad would it be?), each from 1 to 5, and multiply the two. Focus first on the highest scores, but do not ignore low-likelihood, high-impact events such as tsunamis in coastal areas — these require planning even if they are rare.

| Hazard | Likelihood (1–5) | Impact (1–5) | Score | Priority actions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Urban flood | 4 | 3 | 12 | Raise valuables; flood plan; waterproof documents |
| Heat wave | 5 | 2 | 10 | Improve ventilation; check on grandmother |
| Dengue | 4 | 3 | 12 | Weekly 3M Plus; screens; know warning signs |
| Earthquake | 2 | 5 | 10 | Secure furniture; family meeting point |

*An example household risk matrix. Your own scores will differ.*

## Step 5: Look ahead

Climate change means that past experience is an imperfect guide. A neighbourhood that flooded once in twenty years may flood every few years in the future; a well that has never run dry may fail in a severe El Niño. When making long-term decisions — buying land, building or renovating a house, choosing a school — consider how risks may change over the next 20–30 years. Ask whether the site is likely to be affected by sea-level rise, more intense rainfall or subsidence. If in doubt, choose the safer option.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: warning signs from nature and community" kind="local"}
Many Indonesian communities hold detailed knowledge of local warning signs: the smell or colour of river water before a flash flood, the behaviour of animals before an eruption, the withdrawal of the sea before a tsunami, cracks in rice-field terraces before a landslide. The story of *smong* on Simeulue Island (Chapter 13) is the most famous example. Combining this knowledge with modern warnings from BMKG and BPBD gives communities two independent sources of alerts — redundancy that can save lives.
:::

## Revisit regularly

A risk assessment is not a one-time exercise. Revisit it at least once a year — ideally before the rainy season — and whenever your circumstances change: a new baby, an older parent moving in, a new house or job, or new information about local hazards. Keep a written copy with your emergency plan.

::: {.box title="Take action: your one-hour risk assessment" kind="action"}
1. Look up your home address on InaRISK and note the hazard levels.
2. Install the *Info BMKG* app and enable notifications for your area.
3. Ask an older neighbour about past disasters in your area.
4. List the people in your household who would need extra help.
5. Fill in a simple risk matrix like the one above.
6. Choose the top three actions you will take in the next month.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; you can reduce risk by reducing exposure and vulnerability.
- Use InaRISK, BMKG, MAGMA and your local BPBD, plus community memory.
- Assess people, buildings, finances, skills and connections.
- Rank risks by likelihood and impact; plan for rare but catastrophic events too.
- Climate change means the future may differ from the past; revisit your assessment yearly.
:::
