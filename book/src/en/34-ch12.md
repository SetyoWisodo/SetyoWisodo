# Financial Resilience

::: lead
Disasters destroy property, but they also destroy finances — and financial damage often lasts far longer than physical damage. Money that is saved, insured and diversified before a shock is the fastest route to recovery afterwards.
:::

## Why financial preparation matters

After a disaster, households face a double blow: sudden expenses (repairs, medical bills, temporary accommodation, replacing belongings) and lost income (damaged businesses, destroyed crops, days off work). Families without savings or insurance are forced into harmful coping strategies: borrowing at high interest rates from informal lenders, selling productive assets such as livestock, tools or land, cutting food consumption, or taking children out of school. These choices can trap families in poverty long after the floodwaters recede.

Research on disasters consistently finds that the poorest households lose a smaller absolute amount but a much larger share of their wealth, and take far longer to recover. Financial resilience is therefore not a luxury; it is a central part of climate adaptation.

## Build an emergency fund

The foundation of financial resilience is an emergency fund: money set aside specifically for unexpected events, kept in a safe and accessible form. Financial planners commonly recommend saving enough to cover three to six months of essential expenses; for households with irregular incomes, such as farmers, fishers and informal traders, a larger buffer is wise.

For many families, that target seems out of reach. Start small. Setting aside a fixed amount each week or month — even a few tens of thousands of rupiah — builds the habit, and the fund grows over time. Practical tips:

- Keep emergency savings separate from daily spending money, in a savings account that is accessible but not linked to everyday spending.
- Keep a small amount of cash at home in your emergency bag, because ATMs and digital payments may not work during power or network outages.
- Use community savings groups (*arisan*, cooperatives, credit unions) where trusted structures exist.
- Do not use the emergency fund for non-emergencies; replenish it after use.

## Protect your documents and digital access

Lost documents can block access to bank accounts, insurance payments, government aid, land rights and medical care. Keep originals in a waterproof, fire-resistant container in a high place, copies in the emergency bag, and scanned copies in secure cloud storage or an encrypted drive. Important documents include identity cards (KTP), family cards (KK), birth and marriage certificates, land and house certificates (*sertifikat tanah*), vehicle documents (BPKB, STNK), insurance policies, BPJS cards, bank account details, educational certificates and medical records.

Write down important account numbers and contact details for your bank and insurer. Enable secure access to mobile banking, and make sure at least one trusted family member knows how to access essential accounts in an emergency.

## Insurance

Insurance transfers the financial risk of rare but costly events to an insurer in exchange for regular premiums. It is most valuable for losses that would be devastating if they occurred: the destruction of a house, a serious illness, the death of a breadwinner.

**Health insurance.** BPJS Kesehatan, Indonesia's national health insurance programme, covers a wide range of medical care. Make sure every family member is registered and that contributions are up to date.

**Property insurance.** Standard property insurance policies in Indonesia typically cover fire and some other risks, but flood, earthquake and other natural hazards often require additional coverage. Read the policy carefully to see what is and is not covered.

**Agricultural insurance.** Farmers can access subsidised rice farm insurance (*Asuransi Usaha Tani Padi*, AUTP) and livestock insurance, which compensate for losses from floods, droughts, pests and disease. Ask the local agriculture office or farmers' group about eligibility.

**Life insurance.** For families dependent on one or two earners, life insurance provides protection if a breadwinner dies.

**Microinsurance and parametric insurance.** Newer products pay out automatically when a measured trigger — such as rainfall below a threshold or wind speed above a level — is reached, without the need for damage assessment. They can provide fast, low-cost protection, though they may not match actual losses exactly.

At the national level, Indonesia has developed a disaster risk financing strategy, including insurance for state assets and a pooled disaster fund (*Dana Bersama Penanggulangan Bencana*) established by Presidential Regulation No. 75 of 2021, to ensure that public funds are available quickly after disasters.

## Diversify income and assets

Households that depend on a single source of income — especially one tied to the weather — are highly vulnerable. Diversification spreads risk:

- Combine farm and off-farm income where possible.
- Develop skills that are useful in multiple settings.
- Hold savings in more than one form and place.
- Avoid concentrating all wealth in a single asset in a high-risk location, such as a house in a flood-prone area.

## Manage debt wisely

Debt can help households recover, but expensive debt can trap them. Avoid high-interest informal lenders and illegal online lending (*pinjol ilegal*), which often prey on people in distress; check whether lenders are registered with OJK, Indonesia's financial services authority. After disasters, banks and microfinance institutions sometimes offer loan restructuring or payment holidays; ask before falling behind on payments.

## Know what help is available

After major disasters, governments, humanitarian organisations and community groups provide assistance: emergency relief, cash transfers, housing reconstruction grants and livelihood support. Registering with authorities at evacuation sites, keeping documents accessible and documenting damage with photographs make it easier to receive help. Social protection programmes such as *Program Keluarga Harapan* (PKH) and food assistance programmes may be expanded after disasters.

## Think long term

Climate change affects long-term financial decisions as well as emergency preparedness. Before buying land or a house, check hazard maps and ask about past floods and landslides. Consider how property values in high-risk areas may change as risks become better understood and insurance becomes more expensive. When choosing careers or businesses, consider how they may be affected by climate change — and the opportunities that the transition to a low-carbon economy creates in fields such as renewable energy, efficient buildings, sustainable agriculture and climate services.

::: {.box title="Take action: your financial resilience checklist" kind="action"}
- Open a separate emergency savings account and set up a regular deposit, however small.
- Keep some cash in your emergency bag.
- Scan all important documents and store them securely in the cloud.
- Check that every family member is registered with BPJS Kesehatan.
- Review your property insurance: does it cover flood and earthquake?
- If you farm, ask about AUTP crop insurance.
- Check any lender's registration with OJK before borrowing.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Financial shocks after disasters can trap families in poverty; preparation prevents this.
- Build an emergency fund, starting small; keep some cash at home.
- Protect documents physically and digitally.
- Use insurance for catastrophic risks: health, property, crops and life.
- Diversify income and avoid high-interest debt.
:::
