# Stronger Together {.part number="IV"}

# Community Resilience: Social Capital, Mutual Aid and Local Knowledge

::: lead
In almost every disaster, the first responders are not professionals but neighbours. The strength of the ties between people — who knows whom, who trusts whom, who will knock on whose door — is one of the most powerful predictors of who survives and how quickly a place recovers.
:::

## The hidden infrastructure

When researchers study why some communities recover from disasters faster than others, they often find that physical damage and financial aid explain less than expected. A major factor is social capital: the networks, norms and trust that enable people to act together. The political scientist Daniel Aldrich, studying recovery after disasters in Japan, India and the United States, found that communities with dense social networks recovered faster and more fully than those with weaker ties, even when they received similar outside assistance (Aldrich, 2012).

Social capital takes different forms:

- **Bonding** ties connect people who are similar — family, close friends, neighbours. They provide immediate support in a crisis: shelter, food, childcare, emotional comfort.
- **Bridging** ties connect people across different groups — ethnic, religious, economic. They bring in new information and resources and help communities avoid divisions.
- **Linking** ties connect communities to institutions with power and resources — local government, agencies, NGOs, businesses. They help communities access aid and influence decisions.

Resilient communities have all three.

## Gotong royong: an Indonesian strength

Indonesia has a deep tradition of collective action, expressed in the concept of *gotong royong* — mutual cooperation, working together to lighten a shared burden. It appears in communal work to clean drains and village roads (*kerja bakti*), in the rotating savings groups of *arisan*, in the neighbourhood structures of RT and RW, in religious communities and in countless informal networks of support.

After disasters in Indonesia, these traditions have repeatedly proved their value: neighbours sheltering neighbours, communal kitchens (*dapur umum*) feeding the displaced, volunteers clearing debris, and religious and community organisations mobilising relief faster than formal systems. The challenge is to sustain and strengthen these networks before disasters strike, and to link them with formal early warning and response systems.

## The lesson of smong

On 26 December 2004, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off the west coast of Sumatra triggered a tsunami that killed more than 160,000 people in Indonesia alone, most in Aceh. Simeulue Island lay only about 60 kilometres from the epicentre, and waves exceeding 10 metres struck parts of its coast within minutes. Yet only seven of the island's roughly 78,000 inhabitants died in the tsunami (McAdoo et al., 2006).

The reason was *smong* — the local word for tsunami in the Devayan language, and the name of a body of oral tradition passed down since a deadly tsunami in 1907. Through lullabies, poems and stories told by grandparents, the people of Simeulue had learned that when a strong earthquake strikes and the sea withdraws, one must run immediately to the hills. When the ground shook in 2004, the cry of "*smong!*" spread along the coast, and people fled to higher ground.

The *smong* story shows that local knowledge, embedded in culture and repeated across generations, can be as effective as any technology. It also shows the importance of keeping such knowledge alive and connecting it to modern science.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: Disaster Resilient Villages" kind="local"}
BNPB's *Desa/Kelurahan Tangguh Bencana* (Destana) programme, based on BNPB Regulation No. 1 of 2012, supports villages in assessing their risks, preparing contingency plans and evacuation routes, forming village disaster preparedness teams, establishing early warning systems and integrating disaster risk reduction into village development plans and budgets. The *Program Kampung Iklim* (ProKlim), run by the Ministry of Environment, recognises and supports communities that undertake climate adaptation and mitigation actions such as water conservation, waste management, urban farming and tree planting. Both programmes provide a framework for organising community action.

## Mutual aid

Mutual aid is the practice of people organising to meet each other's needs directly, on the basis of solidarity rather than charity. It ranges from informal arrangements — neighbours sharing food, checking on older residents, organising childcare — to structured networks with shared resources and plans.

Mutual aid became widely visible during the COVID-19 pandemic, when communities around the world, including in Indonesia, organised food distribution, support for people in isolation and information sharing. The same networks can be mobilised for climate shocks.

Effective mutual aid networks typically:

- map the needs and skills in the community — who has a boat, a generator, medical training, a vehicle; who lives alone, has a disability or needs regular medicine;
- establish clear communication channels, such as neighbourhood messaging groups and phone trees;
- maintain shared resources — tools, first-aid supplies, a communal kitchen, a store of food;
- meet regularly, not just in crises, to build trust;
- connect with local government and organisations to avoid duplication and access support.

## Inclusion: leaving no one behind

Disasters do not affect everyone equally. Women, children, older people, people with disabilities, the poor, migrants and marginalised groups often face greater risks and receive less help. In many disasters, women die at higher rates than men because of differences in mobility, access to warnings, clothing, caregiving responsibilities and the ability to swim. People with disabilities may be unable to hear sirens, see warning signs or reach evacuation sites without help.

Inclusive preparedness means involving these groups in planning, not merely planning for them. It means accessible warnings (visual, audible, in local languages), accessible evacuation routes and shelters, attention to safety and privacy for women and girls in shelters, and registration systems that identify who will need assistance.

## Local knowledge and science together

Indigenous and local knowledge offers detailed, place-based understanding of environmental change accumulated over generations. Examples across Indonesia include traditional calendars that guide planting and fishing, such as the *pranata mangsa* of Javanese farmers; customary rules (*sasi*) in Maluku and Papua that close fishing grounds or harvests for periods to allow recovery; the *subak* irrigation system of Bali, a UNESCO World Heritage cultural landscape that manages water cooperatively through water temples; and traditional house designs adapted to earthquakes, floods and heat.

Such knowledge is not infallible, and some traditional indicators may become less reliable as the climate shifts beyond historical experience. The most robust approach combines local knowledge with scientific data — for example, climate field schools in which farmers learn to interpret seasonal forecasts alongside their own observations. Respecting and documenting local knowledge, and ensuring that communities have a voice in adaptation decisions, are both ethical obligations and practical necessities.

## Building community resilience: where to start

Community resilience does not require waiting for a government programme. Any group of neighbours can begin:

1. **Get together.** Use an existing gathering — an RT meeting, a religious study group, a *kerja bakti* — to discuss local risks.
2. **Map risks and resources.** Draw a simple map of the neighbourhood showing hazards (flood-prone streets, unstable slopes), safe places, evacuation routes, and resources (people with skills, equipment, vehicles).
3. **Identify who needs help.** Make a confidential list of residents who will need assistance in an emergency, and assign buddies.
4. **Set up communication.** Create a messaging group and a phone tree; agree on warning signals.
5. **Act on prevention.** Clean drains, plant trees, eliminate mosquito breeding sites, build infiltration wells.
6. **Practise.** Hold a simple evacuation drill.
7. **Connect.** Contact the local BPBD, village government and health centre (*puskesmas*) to link your efforts with official systems.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Social capital — bonding, bridging and linking ties — strongly predicts disaster recovery.
- *Gotong royong* and community structures are an Indonesian strength to cultivate.
- *Smong* on Simeulue shows how local knowledge saves lives.
- Mutual aid networks map needs and resources and act together.
- Inclusion of women, older people, people with disabilities and the marginalised is essential.
:::
