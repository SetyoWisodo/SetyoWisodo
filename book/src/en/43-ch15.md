# Technology for Resilience: From Solar Panels to Artificial Intelligence

::: lead
Technology will not solve the climate crisis by itself, but it can make a profound difference — from the solar lamp that lights a house after a storm to the satellite that warns a village of a coming flood.
:::

## The renewable revolution

Over the past fifteen years, the cost of renewable electricity has fallen faster than almost anyone predicted. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the global average cost of electricity from new utility-scale solar power plants fell by about 90 percent between 2010 and 2023, and onshore wind by around 70 percent (IRENA, 2024). In most of the world, new solar and wind power is now the cheapest source of new electricity. Battery costs have fallen similarly, making it increasingly practical to store solar energy for use at night.

For Indonesia, which receives abundant sunshine year-round and has vast geothermal, hydro and wind resources, these trends offer an opportunity to meet growing electricity demand while reducing dependence on coal, which still generates around two-thirds of the country's power. Indonesia has some of the world's largest geothermal resources, thanks to its volcanic geology. Floating solar on reservoirs, such as the Cirata floating solar plant in West Java — among the largest in Southeast Asia when it opened in 2023 — shows the potential.

Renewable energy also strengthens resilience. Decentralised systems — rooftop solar with batteries, village mini-grids — can keep lights, phones, refrigerators for medicines and water pumps running when the central grid fails. For remote islands that rely on expensive diesel fuel, solar mini-grids reduce costs and dependence on fuel shipments that storms can interrupt.

## Early warning systems

Early warning systems are among the most cost-effective adaptation measures. As noted in Chapter 8, just 24 hours' warning of a storm or heat wave can reduce damage by 30 percent. The United Nations' *Early Warnings for All* initiative, launched in 2022, aims to ensure that everyone on Earth is protected by early warning systems by 2027.

An effective early warning system has four elements:

1. **Risk knowledge** — understanding the hazards and who is exposed and vulnerable.
2. **Monitoring and forecasting** — observing conditions and predicting hazards.
3. **Warning dissemination and communication** — getting clear, timely, actionable warnings to everyone at risk, including the most remote and marginalised.
4. **Preparedness and response capability** — ensuring people know what to do when they receive a warning, and are able to do it.

Systems often fail at the "last mile": warnings are generated but do not reach people, are not understood, or are not trusted or acted upon. This is why community involvement, multiple communication channels (sirens, mosques, radio, SMS, apps, messengers on motorbikes), and regular drills are so important.

Indonesia operates several warning systems: BMKG's weather and climate warnings and the Indonesia Tsunami Early Warning System (InaTEWS), established after the 2004 tsunami; volcano monitoring by PVMBG; flood warnings in some river basins; and fire danger ratings. The 2018 Sulawesi earthquake and tsunami and the Sunda Strait tsunami of the same year — caused by a volcanic flank collapse that the standard earthquake-based system could not detect — revealed gaps that have since driven improvements. Each event underscores that technology must be combined with public awareness, so that people respond to natural warning signs even when official alerts fail.

## Data, satellites and artificial intelligence

The amount of data about the Earth's climate and environment has exploded. Satellites monitor temperature, rainfall, sea level, vegetation, fires, floods and land subsidence in near real time. Ground sensors, weather stations and even mobile phones provide additional data.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are being applied to this data in many ways:

- **Weather forecasting.** AI-based models developed in recent years can produce global weather forecasts that rival or exceed traditional physics-based models in accuracy for many variables, at a fraction of the computing cost. National meteorological agencies are beginning to integrate them alongside traditional models.
- **Flood forecasting.** Machine-learning models trained on river and rainfall data can extend flood warnings to river basins that lack dense monitoring networks, and some services already provide flood forecasts for many countries.
- **Fire detection.** Satellite data processed with algorithms detects hotspots, helping authorities respond to fires early. Indonesia uses satellite hotspot data for fire monitoring.
- **Disease surveillance.** Models that combine climate data with disease reports can predict dengue outbreaks weeks in advance, giving health authorities time to act.
- **Damage assessment.** After disasters, AI analysis of satellite and drone images can rapidly map damaged buildings and roads, guiding relief efforts.
- **Agriculture.** Apps provide farmers with tailored advice on planting, irrigation and pest control based on weather forecasts and soil data.

AI is not a panacea. Models are only as good as the data they are trained on, and data are often sparse in exactly the places that are most vulnerable. AI systems can be opaque, biased and misused, and data centres consume significant energy and water. The most useful applications put AI in service of human decision-makers and communities, with transparency about uncertainties.

::: {.box title="Using technology wisely" kind="note"}
The best technology for resilience is often the simplest one that works reliably in local conditions. A battery radio may be more useful than an app when the mobile network is down. A well-maintained hand pump may serve a village better than an advanced treatment plant without spare parts or trained operators. Ask: Does it work without electricity or internet? Can it be repaired locally? Is it affordable? Do people trust it and know how to use it?
:::

## Low-tech and appropriate technology

Not all resilience technology is high-tech. Many of the most effective tools are simple, cheap and locally made:

- ceramic water filters and solar water disinfection;
- rainwater harvesting tanks and infiltration wells;
- improved cookstoves that reduce smoke and fuel use;
- evaporative coolers (clay pot refrigerators) to keep vegetables fresh;
- hand-crank radios and solar lanterns;
- bamboo structures for coastal protection and earthquake-resistant houses;
- traditional *kentongan* (slit drums) for local warnings.

Appropriate technology is suited to local conditions, affordable, maintainable with local skills and materials, and acceptable to the people who use it.

## Digital tools for households and communities

Smartphones put useful resilience tools in many hands:

- **Info BMKG** — weather forecasts, extreme weather warnings, earthquake notifications.
- **InaRISK Personal** — hazard information for your location and preparedness guidance.
- **Messaging groups** — neighbourhood and family groups for rapid communication.
- **Mobile banking and e-wallets** — quick access to funds and aid transfers.
- **Cloud storage** — secure copies of important documents.
- **Offline maps** — navigation when networks are down.

Digital tools also carry risks: misinformation spreads quickly on social media during disasters (Chapter 18), and not everyone has a smartphone or the skills to use it. Communities should ensure that information reaches people through multiple channels.

## Technology and mitigation

Technology is essential not only for adapting to climate change but for limiting it. Beyond renewable energy, key technologies include electric vehicles and two-wheelers (particularly relevant in Indonesia, with its tens of millions of motorbikes), energy-efficient appliances and buildings, low-emission cement and steel, methane reduction in agriculture and waste, and, more controversially, carbon capture and storage. Indonesia's nickel reserves, a key ingredient for batteries, place it at the centre of the global electric vehicle supply chain — an opportunity that must be balanced against the environmental and social impacts of mining and smelting.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Solar power costs fell about 90 percent from 2010 to 2023; renewables are now the cheapest new power.
- Decentralised solar and batteries keep essential services running during outages.
- Early warning systems save lives when they reach the last mile and people know how to act.
- AI improves forecasting, flood and fire detection and disease prediction, but has limits.
- Simple, appropriate technologies are often the most reliable.
:::
