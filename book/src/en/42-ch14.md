# Cities and Villages That Adapt

::: lead
More than half of Indonesians now live in cities, and the share keeps growing. Yet the country's food, water and much of its resilience still depend on villages, forests and coasts. Adaptation must work in both.
:::

## The urban challenge

Cities concentrate people, wealth and infrastructure — and therefore risk. They also concentrate the capacity to respond: institutions, expertise, finance and innovation. Indonesian cities face a distinctive mix of climate hazards: urban heat islands, flash and pluvial flooding, tidal flooding and subsidence on the coast, water shortages, air pollution and disease. These hazards hit hardest in informal settlements (*kampung*) along rivers, railways and coasts, where housing is crowded, drainage poor and services limited.

## Principles of urban resilience

**Plan with risk in mind.** Spatial plans (*Rencana Tata Ruang Wilayah*) should keep new development out of floodplains, riverbanks, coastal hazard zones and unstable slopes, and protect green spaces and water catchments. Enforcement is as important as planning.

**Work with water, not only against it.** Instead of relying solely on walls and pumps, cities can create space for water: restored rivers with natural banks, retention ponds, wetlands, green corridors and permeable surfaces. These measures reduce flooding, recharge groundwater, cool the city and provide recreational space.

**Stop the sinking.** In coastal cities affected by subsidence, the single most important measure is to replace groundwater extraction with reliable piped water supply from surface sources, combined with regulation and enforcement. Sea walls can buy time but do not address the underlying cause, and they can fail catastrophically.

**Cool the city.** Urban greening — street trees, parks, green roofs and walls — reduces temperatures, especially at night. Cool roofs and pavements reflect sunlight. Building codes can promote ventilation and shading. Shaded walkways, markets and public transport stops protect people who spend time outdoors.

**Upgrade informal settlements.** Programmes that improve drainage, water supply, sanitation, paths and housing in existing *kampung* — with residents' participation — reduce risk more effectively and humanely than evictions. Indonesia's long history of kampung improvement, beginning with the Kampung Improvement Program in Jakarta and Surabaya in the 1970s, offers lessons. Surabaya, in particular, has been recognised for community-based greening and cleanliness campaigns.

**Strengthen services.** Reliable water, electricity, health care, waste collection and public transport are themselves forms of resilience. A city where waste is collected has fewer clogged drains and mosquito breeding sites; a city with good public transport is less vulnerable to fuel shortages and emits less.

**Plan for heat and health.** Heat action plans, cooling centres and surveillance of heat-related illness belong in every city's disaster plan.

## Nature-based solutions on the coast

Mangrove forests are among the most valuable ecosystems for climate resilience. Their dense roots reduce wave energy and erosion, trap sediment that can help coastlines keep pace with rising seas, provide nursery habitats for fish and shrimp, and store large amounts of carbon in their soils. Studies show that mangrove belts can significantly reduce wave heights and storm surge damage behind them (Spalding et al., 2014).

Indonesia has the world's largest area of mangroves — around 3.4 million hectares, roughly a fifth of the global total — but large areas have been lost to aquaculture ponds, agriculture and coastal development. National programmes now aim to rehabilitate hundreds of thousands of hectares. Success depends on planting the right species in the right places (where tidal conditions suit mangroves), involving local communities, and addressing the causes of loss. Poorly planned planting — for example on mudflats where mangroves never grew — often fails.

Other coastal approaches include "hybrid" engineering that combines natural and built elements, such as permeable bamboo structures that trap sediment and allow mangroves to re-establish, as tested along Central Java's coast. Seagrass beds and coral reefs also protect coasts from waves; their health depends on reducing pollution, destructive fishing and, ultimately, warming.

## Rural resilience

Rural communities depend directly on natural resources and the weather. Their resilience rests on several foundations:

**Water.** Small reservoirs (*embung*), check dams, rainwater harvesting, spring protection and catchment reforestation secure water for crops, livestock and households through dry seasons.

**Diversified farming.** Agroforestry, crop diversity, integrated rice-fish systems and home gardens (*pekarangan*) reduce dependence on a single crop and spread risk.

**Soil and land.** Terracing, contour planting, cover crops and organic matter reduce erosion and landslides, retain moisture and maintain fertility. Protecting forests on steep slopes is critical for preventing landslides and flash floods downstream.

**Information.** Access to weather forecasts, early warnings, market prices and agricultural advice through mobile phones, extension officers and farmers' groups helps rural households make better decisions.

**Finance and markets.** Savings groups, cooperatives, crop insurance and access to markets help farmers manage risk and invest in adaptation.

**Village governance.** Since the Village Law of 2014, Indonesian villages receive direct transfers (*Dana Desa*) and have considerable autonomy over their use. Villages can use these funds for adaptation: drainage, water infrastructure, early warning, mangrove and forest restoration, and training. Integrating risk assessments into village development plans (*RPJMDes*) ensures that funds are directed where they reduce risk most.

## Peatlands and forests

Indonesia's forests and peatlands are both a global climate asset and a local resilience asset. Intact forests regulate water flows, reduce flooding and landslides, cool local climates and sustain livelihoods. Rewetting drained peatlands — by blocking drainage canals — reduces fire risk and carbon emissions. Community-based forest management, including social forestry permits (*perhutanan sosial*) that give communities legal rights to manage forests, can align conservation with local livelihoods when backed by secure tenure and support.

## Planned relocation

In some places, the risks will become too great for people to stay safely. Coastal villages being swallowed by the sea, settlements on unstable slopes, and floodplains that flood repeatedly may eventually need to be relocated. Planned relocation is one of the most difficult forms of adaptation: it severs people from land, livelihoods, graves, community and identity. Experience worldwide shows that it succeeds only when communities lead the decision, when new sites offer livelihoods and services, when social networks are kept together, and when the process is adequately funded. Forced or poorly planned relocation can leave people worse off than before.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Urban resilience requires risk-based planning, space for water, cooling and upgraded informal settlements.
- Stopping groundwater extraction is the key to halting subsidence in coastal cities.
- Mangroves protect coasts, support fisheries and store carbon; restoration must be done carefully.
- Rural resilience rests on water, diverse farming, healthy soils, information, finance and village governance.
- Planned relocation may become necessary; it must be community-led and well resourced.
:::
