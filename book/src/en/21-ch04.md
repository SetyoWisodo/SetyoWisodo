# The Hazards {.part number="II"}

# Heat: The Silent Killer

::: lead
Heat kills more people than any other weather hazard, yet its victims are often uncounted. They die quietly, at home, from strain on the heart and kidneys, days after the temperature peaks.
:::

## An underestimated danger

Floods and storms produce dramatic images. Heat does not. There is no destroyed bridge, no collapsed house — only people who feel unwell, become confused, and in the worst cases die, often alone and indoors. Death certificates rarely list "heat" as the cause; they record heart failure, stroke, kidney failure or respiratory disease, conditions that heat has triggered or worsened. Only by comparing deaths during hot spells with deaths expected for the time of year do researchers see the full toll.

That toll is large and growing. The 2025 report of the *Lancet* Countdown on health and climate change estimated that heat-related deaths worldwide averaged about 546,000 per year in 2012–2021, and that the rate of heat-related mortality has risen by 23 percent since the 1990s. Heat exposure also cost an estimated 639 billion potential hours of work in 2024, equivalent to income losses of about US$1.09 trillion (Romanello et al., 2025).

## How the body handles heat

The human body keeps its core temperature close to 37 °C. When it gains heat — from the environment or from physical work — it sheds it in two main ways. Blood vessels near the skin widen, carrying heat to the surface where it can radiate away, and sweat glands release moisture that cools the skin as it evaporates. Both mechanisms have costs. Widened blood vessels make the heart pump harder. Sweating loses water and salts that must be replaced.

Problems arise when the environment makes heat loss difficult. When the air is hotter than the skin (around 35 °C), the body gains heat from its surroundings instead of losing it, and evaporation of sweat becomes the only cooling mechanism. When the air is also very humid, sweat evaporates slowly and drips off without cooling. This is why humid heat, common across Indonesia, is especially dangerous.

## Measuring dangerous heat

Air temperature alone is a poor guide to heat stress. Several measures combine temperature with humidity and other factors:

- **Heat index** ("feels like" temperature) combines air temperature and relative humidity.
- **Wet-bulb temperature** is the lowest temperature to which air can be cooled by evaporating water. It indicates how effectively sweat can cool the body.
- **Wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT)** also accounts for sunlight and wind. It is used to set safe work and exercise limits.

A wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C has been proposed as the theoretical upper limit of human survival, because at that point even a healthy person at rest in the shade cannot shed heat (Sherwood & Huber, 2010). Laboratory studies suggest the practical limit is lower: around 30–31 °C wet-bulb for young, healthy adults in humid conditions (Vecellio et al., 2022), and lower still for older people. Such conditions are still rare, but they are being approached more often in parts of South Asia, the Persian Gulf and, during extreme events, Southeast Asia.

## The spectrum of heat illness

Heat illness ranges from mild to fatal. Recognising the stages saves lives.

**Heat rash and cramps.** Small red bumps where sweat ducts are blocked; painful muscle spasms caused by salt loss. Rest, cool down and drink fluids with electrolytes.

**Heat exhaustion.** Heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, a fast weak pulse, cool clammy skin, and sometimes fainting. Move the person to a cool place, loosen clothing, cool them with water and fanning, and give water or oral rehydration solution if they can drink. If symptoms worsen or do not improve within an hour, seek medical help.

**Heat stroke.** A medical emergency. Body temperature above 40 °C, confusion, slurred speech, agitation, seizures or loss of consciousness. Skin may be hot and dry or still sweating. Call emergency services (112 or 119 in Indonesia) immediately and start cooling at once — ideally by immersing the body in cool or cold water up to the neck, or by soaking with water, applying ice packs to the neck, armpits and groin, and fanning continuously. Do not give fluids to a confused or unconscious person. Rapid cooling within the first 30 minutes is the most important factor in survival.

::: {.box title="Who is most at risk?" kind="note"}
- Older people, especially those living alone
- Infants and young children
- Pregnant women
- People with heart, lung or kidney disease, diabetes or obesity
- People taking certain medications (for example diuretics, some blood-pressure and psychiatric drugs)
- Outdoor workers and athletes
- People in poorly ventilated housing, informal settlements or top-floor rooms under metal roofs
- People experiencing homelessness
:::

## Heat in the city

Cities are hotter than the surrounding countryside — often by several degrees, especially at night. This urban heat island effect arises because asphalt, concrete and dark roofs absorb sunlight and release it slowly; buildings trap heat and block wind; vehicles, air conditioners and industry add waste heat; and there is little vegetation or open water to provide evaporative cooling.

Hot nights are especially harmful. When temperatures stay high after dark, the body cannot recover from the day's heat stress, and sleep suffers. In dense neighbourhoods with small, poorly ventilated houses and metal roofs, indoor temperatures can exceed outdoor temperatures well into the night.

The solutions are known: more trees and green spaces, reflective or green roofs, lighter pavements, better ventilation, shade over walkways and markets, and building designs that use cross-ventilation and shading. Traditional Indonesian architecture — raised floors, steep roofs with wide overhangs, open walls and natural materials — embodied much of this knowledge.

## Heat and work

In much of Indonesia and Southeast Asia, a large share of workers labour outdoors or in hot indoor environments such as factories and kitchens. As temperatures rise, they must either slow down, take more breaks or risk illness. The International Labour Organization projects that heat stress will cause productivity losses equivalent to tens of millions of full-time jobs globally by 2030, with South and Southeast Asia most affected.

Simple measures make a large difference: scheduling heavy work in the cooler morning and late afternoon; providing shade, cool drinking water and rest breaks; allowing workers to acclimatise gradually; training supervisors to recognise heat illness; and adjusting quotas on hot days. Over the longer term, mechanisation, better building design and labour standards that set heat limits are needed.

## Heat action plans

Many cities and countries have developed heat action plans that combine early warning, public communication, health-system preparation and targeted support for vulnerable people. The city of Ahmedabad in India launched one of the first in South Asia in 2013, after a severe heat wave in 2010; studies found that heat-related mortality fell after the plan was implemented.

A good heat action plan includes:

1. **Forecasting and thresholds** — using weather forecasts to issue graded alerts when heat is expected to reach dangerous levels.
2. **Communication** — clear messages via television, radio, social media, mosques, schools and community leaders about how to stay safe.
3. **Health system readiness** — training health workers, stocking oral rehydration salts and cooling equipment, and preparing for surges in patients.
4. **Protection of vulnerable groups** — checking on older people living alone, opening cooling centres, adjusting school and work hours.
5. **Long-term measures** — urban greening, cool roofs, better housing.

BMKG publishes temperature forecasts and warnings, and local health offices can build on them. Every community can adapt the same principles at a smaller scale.

::: {.box title="Take action: staying safe in extreme heat" kind="action"}
- Drink water regularly, before you feel thirsty. Limit alcohol and very sugary drinks.
- Stay out of direct sun between about 11:00 and 15:00. Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing and a hat.
- Keep your home cool: close curtains on sun-facing windows during the day, open windows at night when it is cooler, use fans, and spend the hottest hours in the coolest room.
- A damp cloth on the neck, a cool shower or soaking your feet in water helps lower body temperature.
- Never leave children, older people or pets in a parked vehicle, even for a few minutes.
- Check on older relatives and neighbours at least once a day during heat waves.
- Know the signs of heat stroke — confusion, very high temperature, collapse — and call 112 or 119.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Heat is the deadliest weather hazard; about 546,000 people die from heat each year globally.
- Humid heat is especially dangerous because sweat cannot evaporate efficiently.
- Heat stroke is an emergency: cool first, fast, and call for help.
- Cities trap heat; trees, shade, ventilation and cool roofs reduce it.
- Heat action plans and simple workplace measures save lives and livelihoods.
:::
