# A Life Plan for a Changing Century

::: lead
Surviving the future is not a single act but a way of living: informed, prepared, connected and engaged. This final chapter brings the book's lessons together into a practical plan for the years ahead.
:::

## Looking back, looking forward

This book has covered a great deal of ground: the physics of the greenhouse effect and the evidence of warming; the scenarios that describe possible futures; the particular exposure of Indonesia and Southeast Asia; the hazards of heat, water, food insecurity, disease and cascading crises; the practical steps of household preparedness; the power of communities, adaptive cities and villages, technology and psychological resilience; and the policies, finance and justice that shape the larger trajectory.

A few themes run through all of it.

**The warming is real, human-caused and continuing.** There is no serious scientific doubt. The coming decades will be hotter than the past, and sea level will keep rising for centuries.

**How much worse it gets is still up to us.** The difference between 1.5 °C, 2 °C and 3 °C is enormous. Every fraction of a degree avoided reduces suffering.

**Preparation saves lives.** From *smong* on Simeulue to heat action plans in Ahmedabad and early warning systems around the world, knowledge acted upon reduces death and loss dramatically.

**Resilience is collective.** No household is resilient alone. Strong communities, competent institutions and fair policies are the deepest foundations of safety.

**Justice matters.** Those who did least to cause the crisis are suffering most. A response that ignores fairness will be neither legitimate nor effective.

## A personal resilience plan

The following plan organises the book's practical recommendations by time horizon. Adapt it to your circumstances.

### This week

- Install the *Info BMKG* app and enable warnings for your area.
- Look up your home on InaRISK.
- Save emergency numbers in your phone and write them on paper: 112 (general emergency), 110 (police), 113 (fire), 115 (search and rescue), 117 (BNPB), 118/119 (ambulance).
- Talk with your household about what you would do in a flood, earthquake or heat wave.
- Do a *3M Plus* check around your home to remove mosquito breeding sites.

### This month

- Complete a household risk assessment (Chapter 9).
- Write a family emergency plan with meeting points, routes and an out-of-area contact (Chapter 11).
- Pack an emergency bag for each household member.
- Begin storing drinking water and a three-day supply of non-perishable food (Chapter 10).
- Scan important documents and store them securely in the cloud (Chapter 12).
- Open or designate an emergency savings account and make the first deposit.

### This year

- Take a first-aid course, ideally including CPR.
- Make at least one heat-proofing and one flood-proofing improvement to your home.
- Review your insurance coverage: health (BPJS), property and, if relevant, crops.
- Build your water and food stock towards two weeks if you live in a high-risk area.
- Get to know your neighbours; join or help start a community preparedness group (Chapter 13).
- Hold a family evacuation drill before the rainy season.
- Reduce your own emissions where you can: energy efficiency, public transport or electric two-wheelers, less food waste, rooftop solar if feasible.

### This decade

- Consider climate risk in major decisions: where to buy land or a house, how to build or renovate, what to study, which career or business to pursue.
- Diversify income and assets to reduce exposure to climate shocks.
- Engage with local government on adaptation: drainage, green space, water supply, early warning, mangrove and forest protection.
- Support policies and leaders committed to reducing emissions and protecting the vulnerable.
- Pass on what you know to the next generation — as the people of Simeulue did.

## For communities and organisations

Communities, schools, workplaces, religious organisations and businesses can apply the same logic at a larger scale:

- **Assess risks** to people, buildings, operations and supply chains.
- **Plan** for continuity: who does what in an emergency, how to communicate, how to keep essential services running.
- **Invest** in prevention: drainage, cooling, backup power and water, nature-based solutions.
- **Protect the vulnerable**: identify those who need help and assign responsibilities.
- **Practise** through drills and exercises.
- **Learn** from every event and update plans.
- **Reduce emissions** through energy efficiency, renewable energy and sustainable practices.

## Reasons for hope

It is easy to feel overwhelmed by the scale of the climate crisis. But there are real grounds for hope — not naive optimism, but hope grounded in evidence.

Renewable energy is now the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world, and its growth has exceeded expectations year after year. Projected warming by 2100 has fallen significantly since the Paris Agreement, from more than 4 °C under the old business-as-usual to around 2.5–3 °C under current policies — still dangerous, but proof that action works. Early warning systems have dramatically reduced deaths from cyclones in countries such as Bangladesh and India, where tropical cyclones that once killed hundreds of thousands now kill far fewer. Indonesia itself has built a disaster management system that did not exist two decades ago, and its subsidence in Jakarta has slowed where groundwater use was curbed.

Above all, human beings have an extraordinary capacity for cooperation, innovation and care. *Gotong royong* is not a relic; it is a resource. Every community that cleans its drains, plants mangroves, checks on its elderly during a heat wave, or teaches its children what to do when the ground shakes is writing a different future.

## A final word

The future described in this book is demanding. It will test individuals, families, communities and nations. But it is not a future without hope or choice. We know what is happening. We know what works. The question is whether we will act — early enough, fairly enough, and together.

As the IPCC warned in 2022, "any further delay in concerted anticipatory global action on adaptation and mitigation will miss a brief and rapidly closing window of opportunity to secure a liveable and sustainable future for all" (IPCC, 2022). That window is still open. Let us use it.
