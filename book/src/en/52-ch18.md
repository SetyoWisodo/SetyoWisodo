# Thinking Clearly: Evidence, Misinformation and Climate Literacy

::: lead
In an age of information overload, the ability to distinguish reliable knowledge from rumour, spin and falsehood is a survival skill. During disasters, it can be a matter of life and death.
:::

## Why climate literacy matters

Climate literacy means understanding the essential principles of the climate system, knowing how to assess scientific information, communicating about climate in a meaningful way, and being able to make informed and responsible decisions. It does not require a degree in science. It requires curiosity, a basic understanding of how science works, and habits of critical thinking.

Climate literacy helps individuals make better decisions about where to live, how to prepare, what to buy and whom to vote for. It helps communities resist misinformation. And it helps societies sustain support for policies whose benefits may take years to appear.

## How science works

Science is not a collection of fixed facts but a method for reducing uncertainty through evidence. Its key features include:

- **Evidence and testing.** Scientific claims must be testable against observations. Ideas that fail tests are revised or abandoned.
- **Peer review.** Before publication in reputable journals, research is evaluated by independent experts. Peer review is not perfect, but it filters out many errors.
- **Replication and convergence.** Confidence grows when independent studies using different methods reach similar conclusions. The evidence for human-caused climate change rests on many independent lines of evidence — thermometers, satellites, ocean buoys, ice cores, physics and models — that converge.
- **Assessment.** Bodies such as the IPCC do not conduct new research but assess thousands of studies, weighing the evidence and expressing confidence levels. IPCC reports are reviewed by experts and governments and represent the most comprehensive synthesis of climate knowledge.
- **Self-correction.** Science changes as evidence accumulates. This is a strength, not a weakness.

## Reading a scientific claim

When you encounter a claim about climate change — in a news article, a social media post, a speech — ask:

1. **Who is making the claim, and what are their qualifications and interests?** Is the source a recognised scientific body, a peer-reviewed journal, a reputable news organisation, or an anonymous account? Does the source have a financial or political interest?
2. **What is the evidence?** Is the claim based on data and research, or on anecdote and opinion? Can you find the original study?
3. **Is it consistent with the broader body of evidence?** A single study that contradicts many others should be treated with caution until it is confirmed.
4. **Is it being presented accurately?** Headlines often exaggerate or oversimplify. Check whether the original study actually supports the claim.
5. **What is the uncertainty?** Good science reports uncertainty. Claims of absolute certainty — or claims that uncertainty means we know nothing — are red flags.

## Understanding numbers

Climate information is full of numbers. A few concepts help interpret them:

- **Averages and extremes.** A small change in the average can produce a large change in the frequency of extremes. If the average temperature shifts by 1 °C, days that used to be rare may become common.
- **Trends and variability.** Climate is defined over decades. A single cold day or year does not disprove warming, just as a single hot day does not prove it. Look at long-term trends.
- **Baselines.** Always check what a change is measured against: pre-industrial (1850–1900), a recent period (for example 1991–2020), or the previous year.
- **Relative and absolute risk.** "Doubles the risk" sounds alarming, but if the original risk was 1 in a million, it is now 2 in a million. Conversely, a small percentage change applied to a large population can affect millions.
- **Correlation and causation.** Two things happening together does not mean one causes the other. Attribution science uses careful methods to establish causal links.
- **Likelihood language.** The IPCC uses calibrated terms: "virtually certain" (99–100 percent probability), "very likely" (90–100 percent), "likely" (66–100 percent), "about as likely as not" (33–66 percent), and so on.

## Misinformation and disinformation

**Misinformation** is false or misleading information shared without intent to deceive. **Disinformation** is false information deliberately spread to deceive. Both are common in climate discussions.

Climate misinformation has evolved. Early forms denied that warming was happening or that humans were responsible. As the evidence became overwhelming, misinformation shifted towards claims that impacts will be mild or beneficial, that solutions will not work or are too costly, that climate science is unreliable, or that action is pointless because others are not acting. Researchers have catalogued common techniques, often summarised by the acronym FLICC: **F**ake experts, **L**ogical fallacies, **I**mpossible expectations, **C**herry-picking and **C**onspiracy theories (Cook, 2020).

During disasters, misinformation can be dangerous: false reports of an impending tsunami or eruption can cause panic; false "all clear" messages can lead people to return too early; and rumours about aid distribution can cause conflict. After the 2018 Central Sulawesi earthquake and tsunami, hoaxes spread rapidly on social media, prompting authorities to issue repeated clarifications.

## Protecting yourself and others

Research shows that people can be "inoculated" against misinformation by learning about manipulation techniques in advance — a strategy called prebunking (van der Linden et al., 2017). Other practical steps include:

- **Pause before sharing.** Emotional content — fear, outrage — spreads fastest. Take a moment to check before forwarding.
- **Check the source.** Is it an official agency (BMKG, BNPB, BPBD, the Ministry of Health), a reputable news outlet, or an unknown account?
- **Look for confirmation.** Do multiple reliable sources report the same information?
- **Use fact-checking services.** Indonesian fact-checkers such as Cek Fakta, the government's anti-hoax portal and news organisations' fact-check sections regularly debunk false claims.
- **Correct gently.** When friends or family share misinformation, respond respectfully with accurate information and a reliable source. Ridicule tends to entrench beliefs.
- **During disasters, rely on official channels.** Follow BMKG, BNPB and your local BPBD for warnings and instructions.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: official sources" kind="local"}
- **BMKG** — bmkg.go.id; *Info BMKG* app; official social media accounts — weather, climate, earthquakes, tsunamis, air quality.
- **BNPB** — bnpb.go.id; InaRISK; official social media — disaster information, risk maps, situation reports.
- **PVMBG / MAGMA Indonesia** — magma.esdm.go.id — volcanic activity and alert levels.
- **Ministry of Health** — kemkes.go.id — disease outbreaks and health guidance.
- **Local BPBD** — regional warnings, evacuation orders and shelters.

## Talking about climate change

Many people want to discuss climate change with family, friends or colleagues but are unsure how. Research on climate communication suggests:

- **Start with shared values** — family, faith, health, community, prosperity — rather than with facts alone.
- **Make it local and personal.** Talk about how climate change affects the places and things people care about: the flooding on our street, the heat in our children's school, the price of rice.
- **Focus on solutions.** People engage more when they see that action is possible and effective.
- **Listen.** Understanding others' concerns builds trust and opens minds more than lecturing.
- **Use trusted messengers.** Religious leaders, doctors, teachers, farmers and community figures are often more persuasive than distant experts.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Climate literacy means understanding the basics, evaluating evidence and making informed decisions.
- Science works through testing, peer review, replication and self-correction.
- Ask who, what evidence, consistent, accurate, and how uncertain.
- Misinformation uses fake experts, logical fallacies, impossible expectations, cherry-picking and conspiracy theories.
- Pause before sharing; rely on official channels during disasters.
:::
