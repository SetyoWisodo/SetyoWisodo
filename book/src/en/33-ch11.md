# Emergencies: Plans, Kits, Evacuation and First Aid

::: lead
In an emergency, there is no time to think from scratch. People fall back on what they have rehearsed. A family plan, a packed bag and a few first-aid skills turn panic into action.
:::

## The family emergency plan

A family emergency plan answers a few simple questions in advance, so that everyone knows what to do even if they are separated or communication networks are down.

1. **How will we receive warnings?** Identify official channels — the *Info BMKG* app, local BPBD social media, radio, mosque loudspeakers, village sirens or *kentongan* — and make sure at least one adult receives alerts on their phone.
2. **Where will we go?** Identify a safe place inside the home (for example an upper floor during floods), a meeting point near home (a field, a mosque), and an evacuation destination outside the neighbourhood (a relative's house, a designated evacuation site) for each major hazard. Tsunami evacuation requires reaching high ground or a designated vertical evacuation building quickly — know the route.
3. **How will we get there?** Plan at least two routes. Know whether you will walk, ride or drive, and what to do if roads are blocked.
4. **How will we communicate?** Choose an out-of-area contact — a relative in another city — whom everyone will call or message to report their status, since local networks are often overloaded. Write important numbers on paper. Agree to use text messages, which get through more easily than calls when networks are congested.
5. **Who needs help?** Assign responsibilities: who picks up the children from school, who helps grandparents or a family member with a disability, who takes the pets, who grabs the emergency bag.
6. **What about school and work?** Learn the emergency plans of your children's schools and your workplace, including how children will be released to parents.

Write the plan down, keep copies in the emergency bag and on everyone's phone, and discuss it with all members of the household, including children, in a calm and practical way.

## Practise

A plan that has never been practised is likely to fail. Hold a short drill at least once or twice a year — before the rainy season is a good moment. Walk the evacuation route, time how long it takes, check the emergency bag, and test the communication plan. Schools and communities in Indonesia increasingly hold earthquake and tsunami drills; participate when they do. Children who have practised are far less likely to panic.

## The emergency bag (go-bag)

An emergency bag — sometimes called a go-bag or *tas siaga bencana* — is a pre-packed bag that allows you to leave home within minutes and survive for about three days. Keep one for each family member (or one larger bag per household) in an accessible place near the exit.

::: {.box title="Emergency bag checklist" kind="action"}
- Drinking water (at least 1–2 litres per person) and high-energy food that needs no cooking
- Copies of important documents in a waterproof bag: ID cards (KTP), family card (*Kartu Keluarga*), birth certificates, land and house certificates, insurance policies, BPJS cards, bank details, prescriptions — plus digital copies in secure cloud storage
- Some cash in small denominations
- Flashlight or headlamp with spare batteries; power bank and charging cables
- Battery or hand-crank radio; whistle to signal for help
- First-aid kit and a supply of regular medicines
- Change of clothes, raincoat, sandals or sturdy shoes, blanket or sarong
- Masks (N95 for haze or ash), hand sanitiser, soap, toothbrush, sanitary pads
- Items for infants, older people or people with disabilities: formula, diapers, spare glasses, hearing-aid batteries, mobility aids
- Paper list of emergency contacts and a copy of the family plan
- Multi-purpose knife, duct tape, plastic sheet, strong bags
:::

Check the bag every six months: replace expired food, water, medicines and batteries, and update documents and clothes as children grow.

## Evacuation

When authorities issue an evacuation order, follow it. Many disaster deaths occur among people who stayed too long. Evacuation is easier and safer when started early, before roads flood, darkness falls or panic sets in.

**Before leaving:** if there is time, switch off electricity, gas and water at the main supply; lock the house; take the emergency bag; and inform your out-of-area contact.

**On the way:** follow designated routes; avoid floodwater, fallen power lines and unstable slopes; help neighbours who need assistance.

**At the evacuation site:** register with the officials so relatives can find you; follow hygiene rules; look after children; and report health problems early. Evacuation shelters can be crowded and stressful; women, children, older people and people with disabilities have particular needs for safety, privacy and accessibility, and community organisers should plan for these.

**Tsunami:** in coastal areas, a strong or long earthquake (one that makes it hard to stand, or lasts more than about 20 seconds), an unusual roar from the sea, or a sudden withdrawal of the sea are natural warnings. Do not wait for an official alert: move immediately to high ground or inland, on foot if possible, and stay there until authorities declare it safe. Tsunamis come in a series of waves, and the first is often not the largest.

## Basic first aid

Professional medical help may be delayed after a disaster. Basic first aid skills can save lives in the critical minutes and hours. Organisations such as the Indonesian Red Cross (PMI) offer first-aid training; it is one of the best investments a family can make.

Key skills include:

- **Checking safety and calling for help** — make sure the scene is safe before approaching; call 112 or 119.
- **CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)** — for a person who is unresponsive and not breathing normally: push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, about 100–120 compressions per minute, and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) if available.
- **Controlling bleeding** — apply firm, direct pressure to the wound with a clean cloth; keep pressing.
- **Treating burns** — cool with clean running water for at least 20 minutes; do not apply toothpaste, butter or ice.
- **Recognising and treating heat illness** — see Chapter 4.
- **Treating dehydration** — oral rehydration solution.
- **Stabilising fractures** — keep the injured limb still with a splint or sling.
- **Recovery position** — for an unconscious person who is breathing, to keep the airway open.

::: {.box title="First-aid kit contents" kind="note"}
Sterile gauze and bandages of various sizes; adhesive plasters; elastic bandage; triangular bandage; medical tape; scissors and tweezers; antiseptic solution or wipes; disposable gloves; thermometer; oral rehydration salts (*oralit*); paracetamol; antihistamine; burn gel; insect repellent; a first-aid manual; and any personal medicines.
:::

## After the emergency

Recovery begins as soon as the immediate danger passes. Return home only when authorities say it is safe. Inspect the house for structural damage, gas leaks and electrical hazards before entering; if in doubt, ask for professional help. Photograph damage for insurance and assistance claims. Clean and disinfect thoroughly after floods. Watch for signs of illness in the days and weeks after — fever, diarrhoea, skin infections — and for signs of psychological distress in family members, especially children (see Chapter 16). Finally, review what worked and what did not, and update your plan.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Make a written family plan: warnings, safe places, routes, communication, responsibilities.
- Practise at least once or twice a year.
- Keep a packed emergency bag with water, food, documents, cash, light, radio and first aid.
- Evacuate early when instructed; for tsunamis, natural warning signs mean go now.
- Learn first aid and CPR; keep a stocked first-aid kit.
:::
