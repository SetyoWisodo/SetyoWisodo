# Policy, Finance and Justice

::: lead
Households and communities can do a great deal, but the scale of the climate crisis demands action by governments, businesses and the international community. Understanding how climate policy works helps citizens hold leaders to account.
:::

## The international framework

The modern international climate regime rests on three agreements. The **United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change** (UNFCCC), adopted in 1992, set the goal of preventing "dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system" and established the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities" — recognising that rich countries, which emitted most historical greenhouse gases, should lead. The **Kyoto Protocol** of 1997 set binding emission targets for developed countries but did not cover developing countries and lacked participation from the largest emitter of the time, the United States.

The **Paris Agreement** of 2015 took a different approach. Nearly every country agreed to the goal of holding global warming "well below 2 °C" above pre-industrial levels and "pursuing efforts" to limit it to 1.5 °C. Rather than imposing targets from above, it asks each country to set its own Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), to report transparently on progress, and to strengthen its NDC every five years. A "global stocktake" assesses collective progress; the first, concluded at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, called on countries to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems and to triple renewable energy capacity by 2030.

The Paris approach has achieved real progress: projected warming by 2100 has fallen substantially since 2015. But the sum of current NDCs still puts the world on track for warming well above 2 °C, and implementation lags behind pledges.

## Adaptation and loss and damage

For many years, international negotiations focused mainly on cutting emissions. As impacts have grown, adaptation and "loss and damage" — impacts that cannot be avoided through mitigation or adaptation — have moved to the centre. At COP27 in 2022, countries agreed to establish a fund for responding to loss and damage, which was operationalised at COP28 in 2023 with initial pledges of several hundred million US dollars. This is a small fraction of the estimated needs of vulnerable countries, which run into hundreds of billions of dollars per year, but the fund represents a historic recognition of responsibility.

## Climate finance

Developing countries need large sums to adapt to climate change and transition to clean energy. In 2009, developed countries pledged to mobilise US$100 billion a year in climate finance for developing countries by 2020; the goal was met only in 2022, two years late. At COP29 in Baku in 2024, countries agreed on a new collective goal: developed countries should take the lead in mobilising at least US$300 billion a year by 2035, within a broader aim of scaling up finance from all sources to US$1.3 trillion a year. Many developing countries criticised the target as insufficient.

Much climate finance takes the form of loans rather than grants, adding to the debt burdens of vulnerable countries. Adaptation receives a much smaller share than mitigation, even though the needs are vast. The quality, accessibility and fairness of climate finance are therefore as important as its quantity.

## Indonesia's climate policy

Indonesia's approach to climate policy reflects its dual position as a highly vulnerable country and a major emitter with large development needs.

**Emission targets.** As described in Chapter 3, Indonesia's Second NDC (2025) projects emissions peaking in 2030 at about 1.34–1.49 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent and aims for net-zero emissions by 2060 or sooner. Forests and land use (FOLU) are central: the "FOLU Net Sink 2030" policy aims for the forestry and land-use sector to absorb more carbon than it emits by 2030.

**Energy transition.** Coal remains dominant in electricity generation. The Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP), launched in 2022 with an initial pledge of US$20 billion from developed countries and international banks, aims to accelerate the shift to renewables and the early retirement of coal plants. Implementation has been slow: by early 2025 only around US$1.1 billion had been disbursed, and the United States withdrew from the partnership in 2025, though Germany and Japan have continued to lead and total commitments have been reported at more than US$21 billion. Captive coal plants built to power industrial parks, especially for nickel processing, have added to emissions.

**Carbon pricing.** Indonesia's 2021 Harmonisation of Tax Regulations Law (*UU HPP*) introduced a legal basis for a carbon tax, though its full implementation has been delayed. An emissions trading system for coal-fired power plants began in 2023, and a carbon exchange (IDXCarbon) was launched in September 2023.

**Adaptation and disaster management.** Indonesia's disaster management law (Law No. 24 of 2007) established BNPB and regional BPBDs and shifted the focus from response to risk reduction. National development plans increasingly incorporate climate resilience, and the government has developed disaster risk financing mechanisms (Chapter 12).

## How carbon pricing works

Carbon pricing puts a price on greenhouse gas emissions, making polluters pay for some of the damage they cause and creating an incentive to reduce emissions. There are two main approaches. A **carbon tax** sets a price per tonne of emissions and lets the market determine how much emissions fall. An **emissions trading system** (cap-and-trade) sets a limit on total emissions, distributes or auctions permits, and lets companies trade them, so the market determines the price.

Carbon pricing is considered by many economists to be an efficient way to reduce emissions, but it faces political challenges, particularly concerns about rising energy prices for poor households. Revenues can be used to address these concerns — through rebates, social protection or investment in clean energy and public transport. Fossil fuel subsidies, which in many countries including Indonesia have historically consumed large shares of public budgets, work in the opposite direction, effectively paying for emissions.

## Climate justice

Climate change raises profound questions of justice. Those who contributed least to the problem often suffer most. The richest 10 percent of the world's population is responsible for roughly half of global consumption-based emissions, while the poorest half accounts for around a tenth. Low-income countries, small island states and poor communities within every country face the greatest risks and have the fewest resources to adapt.

Justice also has a temporal dimension: today's emissions impose costs on future generations who have no voice in current decisions. And it has a procedural dimension: those affected by climate policies — including indigenous peoples, women, workers in fossil fuel industries, and communities near mines or dams — deserve a voice in decisions.

A "just transition" means ensuring that the shift to a low-carbon economy is fair: protecting workers and communities dependent on fossil fuels through retraining, social protection and new economic opportunities; respecting the rights of communities affected by new projects such as mines, dams and plantations; and sharing the benefits of clean energy widely.

## The role of citizens

Policy does not change by itself. Citizens influence climate policy through elections, public consultation, advocacy, the media, consumer choices, and participation in community and civil society organisations. In Indonesia, local governments have significant authority over spatial planning, disaster management, waste, water and public transport; engaging with them can produce tangible results. Courts are another avenue: climate litigation is growing worldwide, and in 2025 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion affirming that states have legal obligations under international law to protect the climate system.

::: {.box title="Take action: engaging with climate policy" kind="action"}
- Learn your local government's disaster management and climate plans, and attend public consultations (*Musrenbang*).
- Ask candidates in elections about their plans for climate adaptation, clean energy and disaster preparedness.
- Support organisations working on climate, environment and disaster risk reduction.
- Use your voice as a consumer, employee and investor to encourage businesses to reduce emissions and risks.
- Join or form community groups that advocate for local adaptation measures — drainage, green space, clean water.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- The Paris Agreement asks every country to set and strengthen NDCs; current pledges are not enough.
- Loss and damage and adaptation finance are growing priorities, but funding falls far short of needs.
- Indonesia aims for an emissions peak in 2030 and net zero by 2060; the energy transition is the biggest challenge.
- Carbon pricing can cut emissions efficiently if designed fairly.
- Climate justice and a just transition are essential to legitimacy and success.
:::
