# The Resilient Household: Water, Food, Energy and Shelter

::: lead
A resilient household can keep its members safe, fed, hydrated and reasonably comfortable for several days without outside help — and can recover quickly afterwards. Achieving this is less about expensive equipment than about planning and habit.
:::

## The three-day minimum, the two-week goal

After a major disaster, it can take days for help to arrive. Roads may be cut, electricity and water supplies interrupted, shops closed and emergency services overwhelmed. Disaster agencies around the world recommend that households be able to sustain themselves for at least three days. In areas exposed to major hazards — earthquakes, large floods, volcanic eruptions — aiming for one to two weeks of supplies is sensible.

This does not require a bunker full of supplies. It means keeping a modest, rotated stock of essentials and knowing how to use them.

## Water

Water is the most critical supply. A person can survive weeks without food but only a few days without water, and less in hot conditions.

**How much?** Plan for at least 4 litres per person per day — about 2–3 litres for drinking and the rest for cooking and minimal hygiene. In hot weather, for nursing mothers, sick people and those doing physical work, more is needed. The Sphere humanitarian standards set 15 litres per person per day as the minimum for all basic needs in emergency settings (Sphere Association, 2018). For a family of four, a three-day minimum supply is therefore around 50 litres for drinking and cooking.

**Storage.** Use clean, food-grade containers with tight lids, stored in a cool, dark place. Commercially bottled water or sealed gallons (*galon*) are convenient. Rotate stored water every six months. Covered tanks for rainwater harvesting provide a larger backup for washing and flushing. Always cover containers to prevent mosquito breeding.

**Treatment.** When the safety of water is uncertain:

- **Boiling** — bring water to a rolling boil for at least one minute (longer at high altitude). This is the most reliable method.
- **Chlorination** — use water purification tablets or drops according to the instructions, or unscented household bleach in the dose recommended by health authorities, and wait at least 30 minutes.
- **Filtration** — ceramic and membrane filters remove many pathogens; use them in combination with chlorination for best results.
- **Solar disinfection (SODIS)** — fill clear plastic (PET) bottles with clear water and leave them in full sunlight for at least six hours (two days if cloudy).

Let cloudy water settle and pour it through a clean cloth before treatment.

## Food

Keep a stock of non-perishable food that your family will actually eat and that requires little or no cooking or water. Good options in an Indonesian kitchen include:

- rice, instant noodles (in moderation), oats and biscuits;
- canned fish (sardines, tuna), canned meat, canned beans and corned beef;
- *abon* (shredded dried meat or fish), *kering tempe*, peanuts and dried fruit;
- powdered or UHT milk, especially for children;
- sugar, salt, cooking oil, instant seasoning, coffee and tea;
- special foods for infants, older people or those with dietary needs.

Follow the "first in, first out" principle: use the oldest items in daily cooking and replace them, so the stock stays fresh. Keep a manual can opener. Plan for a way to cook without electricity or piped gas: a small LPG stove with a spare canister, or a charcoal stove used only outdoors, never indoors because of carbon monoxide.

## Energy and light

Power cuts accompany most disasters, and extreme heat increasingly strains electricity grids. Prepare with:

- **Light** — LED flashlights and headlamps with spare batteries; solar or rechargeable lanterns. Avoid candles where possible because of fire risk.
- **Communication** — a power bank (kept charged), a battery-powered or hand-crank radio for official announcements, and a list of important phone numbers on paper.
- **Small-scale solar** — a solar panel kit with a battery can power lights, phones and a fan. Costs have fallen dramatically, and such systems are useful in daily life as well as emergencies.
- **Generators** — if you use one, operate it outdoors, far from windows and doors, because exhaust contains deadly carbon monoxide.

## Shelter: making the home safer

Your home is your first line of defence. Improvements need not be expensive.

**Against heat:**

- Improve ventilation with openings on opposite walls for cross-breezes; vents near the roof let hot air escape.
- Shade windows and walls with awnings, blinds, trees or climbing plants.
- Paint roofs and walls in light colours, or install a reflective layer under metal roofs. Ceiling insulation greatly reduces heat from metal or concrete roofs.
- Use ceiling or standing fans; they consume far less electricity than air conditioners and are effective up to quite high temperatures in humid conditions, provided the person can sweat and drink.
- If you have air conditioning, set it to a moderate temperature (around 25–27 °C) to reduce costs and strain on the grid.

**Against flooding:**

- Raise electrical outlets, meters and key appliances above likely flood levels.
- Install one-way valves on drains to prevent sewage backflow.
- Keep a place on an upper floor or high shelf for documents, valuables and emergency supplies.
- Use water-resistant materials (tiles, concrete, raised wooden furniture) on ground floors in flood-prone homes.
- Keep gutters and drains clear; create infiltration wells or biopores in the yard.
- For new houses or major renovations in flood-prone areas, consider raising the floor level or building on stilts, as traditional houses in many coastal and river communities do.

**Against wind, earthquakes and landslides:**

- Make sure roof sheets and tiles are securely fixed.
- Secure tall furniture, water heaters and gas cylinders to walls.
- Follow earthquake-resistant building practices: reinforced concrete frames with proper ties between columns, beams and foundations. The Ministry of Public Works publishes guidelines for safe houses (*rumah tahan gempa*).
- Do not cut steep slopes behind or below the house without engineering advice; plant deep-rooted vegetation; manage drainage so water does not pool on slopes.

## Sanitation and hygiene

When water and sewerage systems fail, disease spreads quickly. Keep soap, hand sanitiser, wet wipes, sanitary pads, diapers if needed, strong plastic bags and toilet paper in your supplies. Know how to create an emergency toilet: a bucket lined with a heavy plastic bag, with a little lime, ash or soil added after each use, and sealed bags disposed of safely.

## Medicines and first aid

Keep a well-stocked first-aid kit (see Chapter 11) and at least a two-week supply of any regular prescription medicines, along with copies of prescriptions. Medicines can be damaged by heat; store them in the coolest part of the house.

## Keep it practical

The best preparations are those that serve daily life as well as emergencies. Rainwater tanks reduce water bills; solar lights reduce electricity bills; shade trees cool the house every day; a pantry with a rotated stock smooths out food price fluctuations. When preparation becomes part of normal household routine, it is far more likely to be maintained.

::: {.box title="Take action: household resilience in five steps" kind="action"}
1. Store at least 4 litres of drinking water per person per day for three days, and rotate it.
2. Build a three-day (then two-week) food stock using "first in, first out".
3. Buy a flashlight, power bank and battery radio; consider a small solar kit.
4. Choose one heat-proofing and one flood-proofing improvement for your home this year.
5. Put regular medicines, documents and supplies in a place that stays dry.
:::

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Aim for at least three days of self-sufficiency; one to two weeks in high-risk areas.
- Water first: 4 litres per person per day minimum; know how to treat water.
- Rotate non-perishable food; have a safe way to cook without electricity.
- Simple home improvements reduce heat and flood damage.
- Preparation works best when it serves everyday life too.
:::
