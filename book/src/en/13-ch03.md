# Indonesia and Southeast Asia on the Front Line

::: lead
An archipelago of more than 17,000 islands, home to about 280 million people and one of the longest coastlines on Earth, sits where the world's warmest ocean meets its most volatile climate patterns.
:::

## A country shaped by water

Indonesia's geography makes it unusually exposed to climate change. Its coastline stretches for roughly 99,000 kilometres. A large share of its population, its major cities, its ports, its fish ponds and much of its rice land lie on low coastal plains and river deltas. The country straddles the equator and sits within the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool, the largest body of warm surface water on the planet, which powers the monsoon and the convection that drives weather across Asia and beyond.

Indonesia's climate is also governed by powerful natural cycles. El Niño, in which the eastern Pacific warms and rainfall shifts away from the Maritime Continent, typically brings drought, crop losses and forest fires. La Niña brings the opposite: heavier rain and flooding. The Indian Ocean Dipole, a similar seesaw in the Indian Ocean, can intensify or offset these effects. Climate change does not replace these cycles; it loads the dice, adding warming on top of natural variability and altering how extremes play out.

This exposure combines with vulnerabilities familiar across much of the developing world: rapid urbanisation, informal settlements in hazardous locations, pressure on forests and peatlands, and large numbers of households that depend on farming and fishing and have limited savings to absorb shocks. At the same time, Indonesia has deep institutional experience with disasters — earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, floods and landslides — and a growing capacity for early warning and disaster management.

## Rising temperatures and heat

Average temperatures across Indonesia have risen in line with the global trend, and hot extremes have become more frequent. BMKG, Indonesia's meteorology, climatology and geophysics agency, has recorded a clear warming trend at stations across the archipelago, with the warmest years concentrated in the most recent decade. Because the tropics have a narrow natural temperature range, even modest warming pushes conditions outside the range that people, crops and ecosystems have historically experienced.

The combination of heat and humidity is particularly dangerous. In a humid climate, sweat evaporates less efficiently, so the body struggles to shed heat. Chapter 4 explains the physiology; here it is enough to note that the outdoor workforce — farmers, construction workers, fishers, street vendors, motorcycle couriers — makes up a large share of employment in Indonesia, and is directly exposed.

## The sinking coast

The most dramatic climate-related threat to Indonesia's cities is the combination of sea-level rise and land subsidence. Global sea level is rising by about 4–5 millimetres a year. In parts of northern Jakarta, the land itself has historically sunk by several centimetres to more than ten centimetres per year, mainly because of excessive groundwater extraction, compounded by the weight of buildings and the natural compaction of soft sediments (Abidin et al., 2015). Some locations recorded rates of 20–25 centimetres a year in earlier decades. Over the past four decades, parts of North Jakarta have sunk by up to 4 metres, and a significant share of the area now lies below sea level.

There are signs of progress. GPS measurements by the Geological Agency for 2015–2023 suggest that subsidence rates within the Jakarta groundwater basin have slowed to between less than one and about five centimetres per year, compared with 10–20 centimetres in 1997–2005, as groundwater use has been restricted and piped water supply expanded (Badan Geologi, 2025). Satellite radar studies still find rates of up to about 8.5 cm per year along parts of the northern coast (Harintaka et al., 2024). The lesson is clear: subsidence is a human-made problem with human-made solutions, chiefly replacing groundwater with reliable piped water, but the land that has already sunk will not rise again.

Jakarta is not alone. Semarang, Pekalongan and other cities along the north coast of Java face similar problems, and tidal flooding (*rob*) has become a routine part of life in many coastal neighbourhoods. The decision to move the national capital to Nusantara in East Kalimantan, enacted in Law No. 3 of 2022, reflects many factors, but the vulnerability of Jakarta's coast is part of the context.

::: {.box title="Indonesia focus: the north coast of Java" kind="local"}
Along the northern coast of Central Java, villages have been abandoned as the sea moves inland. Fish ponds and rice fields have turned brackish, schools and mosques have been raised or relocated, and families have rebuilt floors again and again. Community-led mangrove restoration, permeable sea defences built from bamboo and brushwood that trap sediment, and managed relocation are among the responses being tested. Their success depends on combining engineering with community ownership and on halting the groundwater extraction that drives subsidence.
:::

## Water: floods, droughts and saltwater

Indonesia receives abundant rainfall on average, but it arrives unevenly in space and time. Climate change is expected to intensify both ends of the spectrum: heavier downpours during wet seasons and La Niña years, and longer, harsher dry spells during El Niño years and in already drier regions such as Nusa Tenggara.

Urban flooding in Greater Jakarta in early 2020, when record rainfall over New Year's Eve triggered floods that killed dozens of people and forced hundreds of thousands from their homes, illustrated how extreme rain, clogged rivers, inadequate drainage and subsidence combine. In rural areas, flash floods and landslides on deforested slopes are recurring dangers. Meanwhile, droughts threaten rice harvests, drinking water supplies and hydropower, and seawater intrudes into coastal aquifers and rivers as sea level rises and groundwater is overdrawn.

## Fires and haze

Indonesia contains roughly 13 million hectares of tropical peatland — waterlogged layers of partly decomposed plant matter, built up over thousands of years, that store vast amounts of carbon. When peatlands are drained for plantations and agriculture, they dry out and become highly flammable. During El Niño droughts, fires used to clear land can spread uncontrollably, smouldering underground for weeks.

The 2015 fire season was among the worst on record. Around 2.6 million hectares burned between June and October — an area about four and a half times the size of Bali — at an estimated cost to Indonesia of US$16.1 billion, or about 1.9 percent of GDP (World Bank, 2016). Toxic haze blanketed Sumatra, Kalimantan and neighbouring countries for months. One study estimated that the smoke caused around 100,000 premature deaths across Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore (Koplitz et al., 2016).

Since then, Indonesia has strengthened peatland restoration, fire prevention and law enforcement, and fire seasons in several subsequent years were less severe. But the risk returns with every strong El Niño, and a warmer climate makes droughts more intense.

## Oceans, coral reefs and fisheries

Indonesia lies at the heart of the Coral Triangle, the global centre of marine biodiversity. Its reefs support fisheries that provide a major source of animal protein and livelihoods for millions of coastal families, as well as tourism in places such as Raja Ampat, Bali and Wakatobi. Marine heat waves cause coral bleaching; repeated bleaching without time to recover kills reefs. The global bleaching event that began in 2023 has hit Indonesian waters, as it has reefs across the tropics.

Warming seas also push fish towards cooler waters. Studies project that the potential fish catch in tropical waters could decline substantially by mid-century under high emissions, with some of the largest losses in Southeast Asia (Cheung et al., 2010). Ocean acidification, caused by the ocean absorbing carbon dioxide, adds further stress to corals and shellfish.

## Health

Rising temperatures and changing rainfall alter the spread of infectious diseases. Dengue fever, transmitted by *Aedes* mosquitoes, is the clearest example. Indonesia recorded its highest number of dengue cases in 2024, with more than 250,000 cases and around 1,400 deaths (Kemenkes, 2025). Warmer temperatures shorten the mosquito's breeding cycle and the time the virus needs to develop inside it, while irregular rainfall and water storage create breeding sites. Chapter 7 explores the health dimension in detail, including Indonesian innovations such as *Wolbachia*-infected mosquitoes that have sharply reduced dengue in trials in Yogyakarta.

## Policy context

Indonesia is both vulnerable to climate change and a significant contributor to it: among the world's larger emitters, with emissions dominated historically by land-use change and peat fires and increasingly by energy, especially coal-fired electricity. Its climate commitments have evolved. In its Enhanced Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) of 2022, Indonesia pledged to reduce emissions by 31.89 percent by 2030 relative to a business-as-usual scenario on its own, or by 43.20 percent with international support. In its Second NDC, submitted in October 2025, it shifted to absolute targets based on actual 2019 emissions of about 1.145 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent, projecting that emissions will peak in 2030 at about 1.34–1.49 billion tonnes before declining towards net-zero emissions by 2060 or sooner (Republic of Indonesia, 2025).

Indonesia is also a recipient of the Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP), launched at the G20 summit in Bali in 2022 to finance a shift away from coal. Its implementation has been slower than hoped, a subject Chapter 17 returns to. On adaptation, the national development plans and BNPB's disaster risk management framework increasingly integrate climate risk, and programmes such as *Desa Tangguh Bencana* (Disaster Resilient Villages) and *Program Kampung Iklim* (Climate Village Programme) work at the community level.

## Why the regional view matters

Indonesia's neighbours face similar threats. The Philippines endures some of the world's strongest typhoons. Vietnam's Mekong Delta, which produces much of the country's rice, is sinking and salinising. Bangkok and Manila face subsidence and flooding comparable to Jakarta's. Myanmar, Bangladesh and the Pacific islands are among the most exposed places on Earth. The region's economies are linked by trade in rice, fish, palm oil and manufactured goods, and by migration. A drought in one country can raise food prices in another; haze from one country's fires fills the lungs of another's citizens.

This shared exposure is also an opportunity. ASEAN countries cooperate on disaster management through the AHA Centre, share weather data and early warnings, and increasingly coordinate on haze and energy. Regional cooperation will be one of the most important tools for surviving the decades ahead.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Indonesia's long coastline, low-lying cities, peatlands, reefs and climate-sensitive livelihoods make it highly exposed.
- Land subsidence from groundwater extraction multiplies sea-level rise in northern Java; it can be slowed by replacing groundwater with piped water.
- El Niño years bring drought and fire risk; the 2015 fires cost US$16.1 billion and caused an estimated 100,000 premature deaths regionally.
- Dengue reached record levels in 2024.
- Indonesia's Second NDC targets an emissions peak in 2030 and net zero by 2060 or sooner.
:::
