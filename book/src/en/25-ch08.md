# Cascading Risks: Economy, Migration, Conflict and Infrastructure

::: lead
Climate shocks rarely stay in one place or one sector. A drought becomes a price spike, a power cut, a migration, a political crisis. Understanding how risks cascade is the key to stopping them.
:::

## When risks connect

The IPCC describes climate risks as increasingly "complex and compound": multiple hazards occur at the same time or in sequence, and their impacts cascade across sectors and borders (IPCC, 2022). A heat wave during a drought increases fire risk and electricity demand while reducing hydropower and cooling water for thermal power plants. A flood that cuts a highway disrupts supply chains far from the flooded area. A harvest failure in one exporting country raises food prices around the world.

These connections mean that the total damage from climate change can be much larger than the sum of individual impacts. They also mean that resilience in one system — a robust power grid, a diversified food supply, a strong social safety net — can protect others.

## The economic toll

Climate-related disasters already impose large and rising economic costs. Insured losses from natural catastrophes worldwide have exceeded US$100 billion in each recent year, and total economic losses — including uninsured damage — are considerably higher. In developing countries, most losses are uninsured, falling on households, businesses and governments.

Beyond the direct damage, climate change acts as a drag on economic growth. Heat reduces labour productivity, especially in agriculture, construction and manufacturing. Damage to infrastructure diverts public budgets from development to reconstruction. Uncertainty discourages investment. Studies of historical data suggest that unusually hot years reduce economic growth in already hot countries, and that the effects can persist.

For Indonesia, the costs are visible in recurring flood damage in cities, losses from drought and fire, coastal erosion and tidal flooding, and the costs of disease. The 2015 fires alone cost an estimated US$16.1 billion (World Bank, 2016). Investments in adaptation — drainage, coastal protection, early warning, resilient infrastructure — are expensive, but the evidence consistently shows that they cost far less than the damage they prevent.

::: {.box title="The economics of prevention" kind="note"}
The Global Commission on Adaptation estimated that investing US$1.8 trillion globally between 2020 and 2030 in five areas — early warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure, improved dryland agriculture, mangrove protection, and resilient water resources — could generate US$7.1 trillion in total net benefits. It also found that just 24 hours' warning of a coming storm or heat wave can cut the ensuing damage by 30 percent (Global Commission on Adaptation, 2019).
:::

## Displacement and migration

People have always moved in response to environmental change. Today, climate change is intensifying the pressures that drive movement, while also making it harder for the poorest to move at all.

Sudden disasters cause large-scale displacement. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre records tens of millions of new internal displacements caused by disasters every year — the large majority from weather-related events such as floods and storms. Most people displaced by disasters return home within weeks or months, but some cannot, and repeated displacement erodes livelihoods and savings.

Slow-onset changes — sea-level rise, salinisation, drought and declining agricultural productivity — drive more gradual migration, typically from rural areas to cities. The World Bank's *Groundswell* report projected that, without strong action, climate change could force up to 216 million people across six world regions to move within their own countries by 2050, including up to 49 million in East Asia and the Pacific (Clement et al., 2021).

Migration is not only a problem. It can be an effective adaptation strategy, allowing households to diversify income through remittances and to move away from danger. The challenges arise when migration is forced, unplanned and unsupported — when people arrive in cities without jobs, housing or services, often settling in informal neighbourhoods that are themselves exposed to floods and heat. Policies that support safe, orderly and voluntary movement, including well-planned relocation where staying is no longer viable, can turn a source of vulnerability into a source of resilience.

## Conflict and security

Climate change does not cause wars by itself, and the relationship between climate and conflict is debated among researchers. But there is broad agreement that climate change acts as a "threat multiplier": it can intensify existing tensions over land, water and resources, strain governments, undermine livelihoods and increase the pool of people with grievances, particularly where institutions are weak and inequality is high. Disputes between farmers and herders, competition over water, and tensions arising from displacement have been linked to climate stress in some regions.

The best protection against climate-related conflict is the same as the best protection against conflict generally: inclusive governance, fair access to resources, effective conflict resolution, and economic opportunities. Climate adaptation programmes that strengthen these foundations — for example through participatory water management or equitable land rights — contribute to peace as well as resilience.

## Infrastructure: the hidden backbone

Modern life depends on networks: electricity grids, water and sewerage systems, roads, bridges, ports, telecommunications and the internet. Much of this infrastructure was designed for the climate of the past. Drains are sized for historical rainfall; bridges for historical flood levels; power lines and transformers for historical temperatures.

Climate change pushes these systems beyond their design limits. Heat reduces the efficiency of power lines and transformers just when demand for cooling peaks. Floods inundate substations, pumping stations and roads. Landslides cut transport and communication links. Sea-level rise threatens ports, coastal roads and airports. Because infrastructure systems are interconnected, the failure of one can trigger others: a power cut disables water pumps and mobile phone towers; a flooded road prevents repair crews from reaching a damaged substation.

Building resilient infrastructure means designing for future climate conditions rather than past ones, adding redundancy (more than one route, more than one source), protecting critical facilities (raising substations and generators above flood levels), maintaining existing assets, and planning for rapid recovery. It also means using nature: mangroves, wetlands and forests protect infrastructure at low cost.

## Systemic risk and the role of finance

Financial regulators increasingly recognise climate change as a source of systemic risk. Physical risks — damage to property and assets — can threaten banks and insurers. Transition risks arise as the world shifts away from fossil fuels, potentially leaving coal plants, mines and related assets "stranded". Indonesia's financial regulator (OJK) has developed a sustainable finance taxonomy and is introducing climate risk management requirements for banks. For households, the same logic applies at a smaller scale: assets in high-risk locations may lose value, and diversification protects against shocks (Chapter 12).

## Breaking the cascade

Cascading risks can be interrupted. The key principles are:

- **Redundancy** — backup systems, alternative routes, multiple suppliers.
- **Diversity** — diverse crops, energy sources and income streams.
- **Modularity** — systems that can fail in parts without collapsing entirely, such as local solar mini-grids.
- **Early warning** — detecting problems early enough to act.
- **Social protection** — cash transfers and safety nets that prevent a shock from forcing families into irreversible losses, such as selling productive assets or withdrawing children from school. Indonesia's social protection programmes, including *Program Keluarga Harapan* and food assistance, can be scaled up rapidly after disasters if designed to be "adaptive".
- **Coordination** — breaking down silos between agencies responsible for different sectors.

::: {.box title="Key points" kind="note"}
- Climate risks are compound and cascading; impacts in one sector spread to others.
- Prevention pays: early warning and resilient infrastructure deliver large returns.
- Climate change could drive up to 216 million internal migrants by 2050; well-supported migration can be adaptive.
- Climate acts as a threat multiplier for conflict, especially where institutions are weak.
- Infrastructure designed for past climates must be upgraded for future ones.
:::
